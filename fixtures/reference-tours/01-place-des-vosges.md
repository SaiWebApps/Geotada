## ANCHOR ESSAY 1 — The 1612 inauguration

> Henri IV was assassinated shortly before the square was finished, and so it was inaugurated by his son, the young Louis XIII — which is why the equestrian statue in the center of the square is of Louis XIII, not of Henri IV. The inauguration, on April 5, 6, and 7 of 1612, was a three-day spectacle. Jousting was forbidden now, but five equestrian ballets were staged for the cavaliers, each in different exotic costumes — one group dressed as American Indians. One quadrille alone involved the Duc de Longueville and his retainers in gray-violet satin with silver embroidery, their pages and horses in red velvet, four winged horses pulling a chariot with two rhinoceroses harnessed behind, and two blue-satin giants preceding the duke himself on a horse draped in *repoussé* silver and gold. There were one hundred and fifty musicians, continuous parades, fireworks at night, and ten thousand spectators. Members of the royal entourage had been assigned specific houses and balconies — and for the first time, Paris houses had been built with real balconies instead of the thin stone ledges Parisians had leaned on before. Once the place Royale had shown the way, balconies became *de rigueur*. The common people had to hang from chimneys or squeeze into the arcades. Of one delegation of ambassadors Mme de Motteville wrote: "In general they are so fat that they give you heartburn, and concerning their bodies, they are dirty." **[PW:1612_inaugural_tournament_balconies]**

## ANCHOR ESSAY 2 — Richelieu and the duels

> The place Royale was the favourite dueling ground of the French aristocracy; fashionable ladies of the square enjoyed the spectacle, and swordsmen were encouraged by these admirers. In 1614 three out of four antagonists were killed in a single encounter. In 1626 Cardinal Richelieu, first minister of Louis XIII, forbade dueling on pain of death. The ban itself was the challenge: a year later, a six-man duel — two principals and four seconds, who were expected to do more than hold the combatants' coats — took place right in front of the Pavillon du Roi. Two dead, two fled to England, two off to Lorraine. The latter two were caught en route, brought back to Paris, and decapitated, over the protests of the nobility of France. Richelieu was not a man to take insolence lightly. In 1643, after his death, the last scion of the Protestant Colignys dueled with Henri, Duc de Guise — grandson of the man who had killed Coligny's grandfather, Admiral Coligny, in the 1572 Saint Bartholomew's Day Massacre. Guise was wounded first, but managed to grab Coligny's *épée*, hold it, and simply finish him off. The king, hoping to end the enmity between Catholics and Protestants, chose not to punish the victor. **[PW:richelieu_ban_six_man_duel]**

## CIRCUMNAVIGATION

### no. 1 — Pavillon du Roi
> The Pavillon du Roi at no. 1 — the central pavilion on the south side — was never occupied by the king himself. It was too small for him and his retinue; his concierge, the gatekeeper, was the first resident. Since 1666 it has been rented to different tenants, and today it is divided into apartments served by ugly stairs and an elevator crammed into the right-hand side of the central arcade — an eighteenth-century intrusion that still rankles, because it blocks part of the vista from the rue de Birague. As early as 1752 the writer Germain Brice argued that the three arcades of the pavilion should be torn down to leave the Louis XIII statue visible all the way from the rue Saint-Antoine: "It has to hurt," he wrote, "to see one of the three arcades blocked to make a nasty stairway… we have such little care for public embellishments in Paris." Look up, though: the balcony here is stone rather than wrought iron, and a bust of Henri IV watches the square from above. Walk through the arcade and, on the rue de Birague side, you'll see the king's arms and initials above the arches, still there from the original construction. **[PW:pavillon_du_roi_never_occupied]**

### no. 1 bis — Mme de Sévigné's birthplace
> The Hôtel de Coulanges at no. 1 bis is famous as the birthplace, in 1626, of the Marquise de Sévigné — Marie de Rabutin-Chantal — whose letters to her daughter remain one of the great collections in French literature. This was her grandfather Philippe de Coulanges's house. Orphaned at two, Marie lived here for ten years, was married off at seventeen to the Marquis de Sévigné, was widowed young when he was killed in a duel in 1651, and spent the rest of her life in the Marais, turning proposals of marriage into friendships. Her daughter married the Comte de Grignan and moved to Provence; the separation was painful, and the letters are the record of it. Look up — this is one of the oldest wrought-iron balconies in Paris, built in 1655, its straight, plain support poles marking its authenticity. **[PW:no_1_bis]**

### no. 2 — Coconnas
> Restaurant Coconnas at no. 2 (its front door actually opens on the rue de Birague around the corner) is owned by Claude Terrail of the Tour d'Argent. Both of Terrail's places appear in the film *Who Is Killing the Great Chefs of Europe?* The specialty here is *poule-au-pot* — stewed chicken — a menu that quietly commemorates Henri IV's famous promise of "a chicken in every pot." **[PW:no_2_coconnas]**

### no. 3 — Trompe-l'oeil courtyard
> Press the button marked *porte* at no. 3 and step into the charming courtyard behind — the first in the square to have been restored, and the only one with "brick" walls inside. Look closely: the brick is painted, *trompe l'oeil*, even down to the pipes in the corners. The pipes are painted pink where they run against the red "brick," and ivory against the stone trim. The building went up in 1613 on the remains of the stables of the Hôtel des Tournelles, and two huge bronze urns stand against the back wall. The ground-floor shop today is Issey Miyake. **[PW:no_3]**

### no. 5 — Factory rails
> Look at the pavement in front of no. 5: rails still set into the stones, installed to run heavy loads to the factory inside. If you want to see what the factory-filled place des Vosges was like, open the door here and look. **[PW:no_5]**

### no. 6 — Hugo's house, Anne de Guéménée's house
> The Musée Victor Hugo at no. 6 was Hugo's residence from 1832 to 1848 — until his disagreements with Louis Napoléon sent him into political exile on Guernsey. The museum is free; the top floor gives you Hugo's life, the second his drawings and mementos, the first a re-creation of his apartment, with each room dedicated to a period of his life. Don't miss the restored bedroom with his Louis-XIII deathbed and his death mask, or the striking Chinese-style dining room with its bright red chimney, which he built himself during his Guernsey exile. Before Hugo's time this was the *hôtel* of the Princesse de Guéménée — Anne de Montbazon — the most famous *précieuse* of the place. She was married at twelve, and like other premature wives of the day never settled into monogamy. Her lovers read like a guillotine list: the Comte de Montmorency-Bouteville, beheaded 1627 for his famous duel; Henri II of Montmorency, beheaded 1632; the Comte de Soissons, dead 1641; Auguste de Thou, beheaded 1642. She brought no luck to her lovers, but she herself lived to be eighty-one. The property stretched back to no. 17 rue des Tournelles with a discreet rear alley for quick exits — a feature Hugo himself often used when visiting his own mistresses. Before you leave the museum, look out of a second-floor window: from that height, above the trees, you can see the whole square. **[PW:no_6_hugo_residence_before_exile]**

### no. 6 sidebar — Marion Delorme
> Marion Delorme may also have lived in a small pavilion at the back of no. 6, though most historians doubt she and Anne de Guéménée could have shared the same house peaceably — the traffic alone would have caused jams. Marion was Anne's equal in lovers and in the brilliance of her salon. She began as courtesan to Jacques Vallée — a notorious epicurean and avowed atheist — moved on to the handsome Marquis de Cinq-Mars, who plotted against Richelieu and lost his head for it (Alfred de Vigny's novel *Cinq-Mars* is about him). Then George Villiers, the first Duke of Buckingham, whom you may remember from *The Three Musketeers*. Then Louis II de Bourbon, Prince de Condé — the great Condé — who imprisoned his own wife on faked charges and wrote to the king asking she never be released. Nice fellow. Marion is said, finally, to have got Cardinal Richelieu himself into her bed — which neatly keeps everything in the family, since Condé's unfortunate wife was Richelieu's niece. Victor Hugo wrote a drama about her; Bottesini an opera. **[PW:marion_delorme_lovers — sidebar/skippable]**

### no. 8 — Théophile Gautier
> No. 8, as the plaque on the wall will tell you, was the home of the poet Théophile Gautier — who lent his name to the technical high school right next door. **[PW:no_8]**

### no. 9 — Hôtel de Chaulnes
> The Hôtel de Chaulnes at no. 9 was one of the most luxurious houses of the place Royale when it was built in 1607. Louis XIII stayed here during the 1612 inaugural tournament. In 1644 the house was bought by Honoré d'Albret, *maréchal de France*, Duc de Chaulnes. His wealthy widow remodelled; their third son, who liked living well, later hired Mansart to extend the right wing back to the rue de Turenne — Mansart gave it a large triangular *fronton* in the roofline and a monumental staircase whose wrought-iron rail was sold to an Englishman in the nineteenth century. In 1695 the Nicolai family took over. A Revolutionary legend says Aymard Charles de Nicolai hid in his wife's boudoir when the *sans-culottes* came looking; he was found and led straight to the guillotine. The actress Mlle Rachel lived here in the mid-nineteenth century. Today the whole first floor belongs to the Architecture Society. Step into the courtyard and look at the painted pink-and-green beams just visible through the last second-floor window on the left — the oratory of the duchess, decorated in 1654, among the last such ceilings in Paris. **[PW:no_9_chaulnes_mansart_extension]**

### no. 9 — L'Ambroisie restaurant
> Also at no. 9 is L'Ambroisie, Bernard Pacaud's Michelin three-star restaurant — the only world-class kitchen in the Marais. Dinner for two runs about seven hundred euros; reservations come a month out. The *trou normand* arrives as a fruit sorbet set in a *sabayon* sauce — a modern descendant of the original, a shot of calvados to clear the palate between courses. Maupassant wrote of it in *Contes de la Bécasse*: "Between each serving, the cleansing of the palate was done with a glass of *eau-de-vie* that lit a fire in the belly and madness in the head." **[PW:no_9_ambroisie]**

### no. 13 — Mirror salon
> No. 13, once the Hôtel d'Antoine de Rochebaron, was one of the most elegant houses on the square. According to an old description it was a gem: stucco ornaments by Van Obstal painted by Vouet, an alcove by the wood-sculptor Buirette who had *épuisé tout son savoir* — exhausted all his skill — in making it. There was a salon *à l'italienne* two storeys high, its ceiling shaped like a lantern, its walls lined with mirrors — a rare commodity in the seventeenth century. A chronicler wrote: "When reclining in this salon, if we look to the right, we see, through the two glass doors, opening on to the place, carriages, people on foot or on horseback, and all that is happening in the place Royale. If we turn to the left, the same thing reflected in the mirrors is presented to our sight, so that, without getting out of bed, in summer as in winter, in sickness or health, we can enjoy the diversion." After Villequier the house passed to M. des Hameaux, ambassador to Venice, who brought back still more mirrors from Italy. Today the back wings have been re-extended to the rue de Turenne, a Versailles-pattern parquet runs through the apartments, and the first underground parking garage in the square has been dug beneath the courtyard. **[PW:no_13]**

### no. 14 — Synagogue
> No. 14 — the Hôtel de l'Abbé de la Rivière — is now one of the best-restored *hôtels* in the square, its six arcades redone in real brick and its wrought-iron balconies cleaned of rust. It's a synagogue today. The half facing the place serves the Ashkenazic community (eastern-European, mostly Yiddish-speaking); the half on the rue des Tournelles serves the Sephardic (Jews who settled in Spain and Portugal, now largely from North Africa). When the doors on the place are unlocked, Friday evenings and Saturday mornings, step inside to see wood-panelled walls, stairs carpeted with Oriental rugs, plants in the entry — the interior of a Victorian home, because that's exactly what it was: the chief rabbi of France once lived here with his wife and five children. The *hôtel* takes its name from a seventeenth-century owner, the Abbé de la Rivière, favourite of the Duc d'Orléans — a tailor's son who rose to duke and bishop of Langres, though he never quite made cardinal. The best painters of the era, Le Brun and Mignard, decorated his ceilings. Those ceilings are preserved today in the Musée Carnavalet. **[PW:no_14]**

### Pavillon de la Reine — north center
> You're now standing opposite Henri's pavilion, in front of the Pavillon de la Reine — the north-centre pavilion. It's an identical twin of the Pavillon du Roi except for two details: the Médicis sun emblem on its façade, and an unusually long iron balcony. The small street at the back, the rue de Béarn, was the exit route for every parade that passed through the place Royale. **[PW:pavillon_de_la_reine]**

### no. 16 — François le Roux's wife
> No. 16 was owned in the seventeenth century by a royal counsellor named François le Roux, who had the dubious distinction of marrying "*une petite garce qui se donnait pour un quart d'écu*" — a little bitch who gave herself for a quarter of an écu. That was slightly less than a *livre*, but in those days a *livre* was a day's wages for a manual worker, so Mme le Roux was not so cheap after all. Today the courtyard needs restoration, but step in to see the wrought-iron railing on the left and the *mascarons* over the portal and entry. **[PW:no_16]**

### no. 18 — The bronze gypsy
> No. 18 was the *hôtel* of Marguerite de Béthune, Duchesse de Rohan, daughter of Sully, Henri IV's first minister. She was married at nine, in 1604, had innumerable lovers — many of them neighbours on this square — and of her nine children, only one daughter survived her. In the stairwell on the left you can see a bronze statue of a gypsy girl; she's missing the lantern from her left hand and one earring. The concierge will tell you, proudly, that Victor Hugo put her there, though we've never found that in writing. What we do know is that the gypsy was stolen some years ago. A resident called the police and got immediate results — she was found minutes later at a nearby traffic light, her head sticking out of the back of a pickup truck. She's now firmly bolted to her red-marble base, which is firmly bolted to the floor. **[PW:no_18]**

### no. 19 — Ma Bourgogne
> The café at no. 19, Ma Bourgogne, has been here since 1920 and specialises in *charcuterie* from Auvergne and wines from Burgundy and Bordeaux. The waiters tease; the coffee with steamed milk — *café crème* — is good at any hour. In the seventeenth century, though, this building belonged to the Conseiller d'État Robert Aubrey and his wife Claude de Pretevel, and the chronicler Tallement des Réaux recorded that "she hated him so much that more than once she pissed into the soup she gave him." Hardly an appetising legacy for a building that now houses a café — fortunately those manners are far behind us. **[PW:no_19]**

### no. 20 — Colombage exposed
> Press the button above the keypad at no. 20 and step into the courtyard. The front wall has been paused mid-restoration for twenty-five years. An architect who specialised in prefabricated building wanted to recase the façade in hollow brick; the authorities objected that the original was plaster painted to resemble brick. The decision: status quo. So the ugly glass balcony and the partially-completed seventeenth-century façade stay exactly as you see them — and give you the best look anywhere in the square at the *colombage* technique, where large cornerstones frame the wall and small stones and rubble are bound together with plaster. **[PW:no_20]**

### no. 21 — Richelieu's *pied-à-terre*, Revolutionary forge
> No. 21 was given to Cardinal Richelieu by Henri IV. No one knows if he ever lived here, but his grand-nephew's son certainly used this *pied-à-terre* to get to know almost every woman in the square. Look closely at the sides of the arcade pillars just above the pediments: you'll see little square stone patches. They date from the Revolution, when this stretch of arcade was used as a forge for making arms. The patches cover the points where metal curtains were hung to close off the forge. Until about 1930 these arcades were packed with butchers, shoemakers, and small shops that walled themselves in by setting beams into cuts in the stone; the very last was a shoemaker, still working here in the 1950s. Restoration in 1978 sent the owners back to the Versailles brickworks — the same kilns that had supplied the originals in the seventeenth century — to match the missing bricks, and their example inspired other owners to restore with real brick rather than paint. **[PW:no_21]**

### no. 23 — Marie-Charlotte d'Entragues
> Look at the heavy wooden door of no. 23, decorated with thick wooden plaques attached by metal bolts. Inside, the small garden and vaulted brick entry belong to the Hôtel de Marie-Charlotte de Balzac d'Entragues — a woman whose career more than matched her name. She lived here with her mother, who had been the mistress of Charles IX; her sister Henriette had been the mistress of Henri IV. Decidedly these ladies did not bring good luck to their lovers. Marie-Charlotte was the mistress of the *maréchal* de Bassompierre and bore him a son, Louis, in 1610. When he refused to marry her despite a written promise, she consoled herself with a string of distinguished replacements: the archbishop of Paris Jean-François de Gondi, and the financier Le Plessis-Guénégaud among them. **[PW:no_23]**

### no. 24 — Boufflers's wife
> No. 24, the Hôtel du Maréchal de Géran, is less illustrious than its neighbours, but it did have one notorious resident: the wife of Duc de Boufflers, *maréchal* of France. She was fourteen when she married, and quickly became famous for her beauty, caustic wit, and number of lovers. A contemporary verse caught her reputation: "Quand Boufflers parut à cour, / On crut voir la *mère d'amour*, / Chacun s'empressait à lui plaire / Et chacun l'avait à son tour." (When Boufflers appeared at court, / You would have thought you were looking at Venus; / Everyone tried to please her / And everyone had her in turn.) **[PW:no_24]**

### no. 25 — Guirlande de Julie
> The restaurant Guirlande de Julie at no. 25 is named for a small seventeenth-century book of *madrigals* — each poem surrounded by a different flower — that the Duc de Montausier offered his wife on May 22, 1641. It's owned, like Coconnas at no. 2, by Claude Terrail of the Tour d'Argent. Look up at the right side of no. 25: the large hemisphere of glass on the ground floor usually marks a shop, but this is a private home, and the owners let the window serve as their view of the square. **[PW:no_25]**

### no. 26 — Charlotte de Vieux-Pont (micro)
> No. 26 belonged to Charlotte de Vieux-Pont, the first *précieuse* of the place des Vosges. **[PW:no_26]**
