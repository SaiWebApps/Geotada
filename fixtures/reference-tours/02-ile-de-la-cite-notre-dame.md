## OPEN — Pont Neuf Metro, on the bridge

`[GLUE: Stand halfway across the bridge, by the statue. Look around.]`

> The graceful, twelve-arched Pont-Neuf — despite its name, meaning *New Bridge* — is Paris's oldest surviving bridge. It was built by Henri IV, who is commemorated with a statue halfway across. Made of stone and free of the usual medieval complement of houses, it was a radical departure from previous structures, hence its name. Henri IV, one of the capital's first great town-planners, took much interest in the Pont-Neuf's progress and would sometimes come to inspect it, on one occasion delighting the workmen by taking a flying leap over an incomplete arch. **[RG:pont_neuf_henri_iv_flying_leap]**

> Pont Neuf means *New Bridge*, but, ironically, it is the oldest in Paris and was the first to be built of stone and to be free of houses. The semicircular side bays were originally market stalls used by teeth-pullers, quack doctors and other merchants. The original statue of Henry IV was melted down during the Revolution. The anti-Royalist who replaced it secretly hid a statuette of Napoleon in the horse's belly; this was recently discovered during restoration work. **[F:pont_neuf_history + F:statue_napoleon_hidden]**

> The picturesque green bookstalls of the *bouquinistes* along the quais are a remnant of the days when the Pont-Neuf was a world of its own, crowded with strollers, pedlars, acrobats, prostitutes, and riff-raff. **[V:bouquinistes_seasoning]**

## ANCHOR ESSAY — Île de la Cité origins

`[GLUE: This island below you is where Paris began.]`

> Archaeological evidence suggests that a Celtic tribe called the Parisii first settled on the Île de la Cité in the middle of the River Seine during the 3rd century BC. The island was a secure settlement, having the Seine as a natural moat, but no defence was sufficient to protect the Celts from Roman invasion in 52 BC. There was relative peace between Romans and Celts, and the city, renamed *Lutetia* — meaning born of the waters — prospered. **[F:parisii_settlement]**

> The Parisii had been boat builders and competent sailors, inspiring Roman administrators to choose the ship as their symbol. The Latin motto for Paris became *Fluctuat Nec Mergitur*, meaning 'Paris the unsinkable ship'. The emblem and motto are still used today. **[F:fluctuat_nec_mergitur]**

> In 360 the Roman general Julian, the future emperor Julian the Apostate, was proclaimed emperor on this very spot by his own soldiers. The kings of France lived here until 1358, when on the 22nd of February, with Jean II le Bon held captive in England, the provost of merchants Étienne Marcel thought he could overthrow the monarchy with English help. That afternoon the young Dauphin — the future Charles V — was sitting in the palace with two of his counsellors when Marcel's men burst in and slew the counsellors before his eyes. The Dauphin was unhurt, but the victims' gushing blood stained his clothes, an experience he never forgot. A prudent politician, deservedly called *le Sage* — the Wise — he slipped out of the palace one night by way of the river, moved to the Hôtel de Saint-Pol in the Marais, and arranged for himself a new residence within the safer walls of the Louvre. The kings of France never returned to the island. **[V:palais_de_justice_marcel_revolt — anchor]**

`[GLUE: It takes imagination to picture the medieval city today, because most of it was erased in the nineteenth century.]`

> Baron Haussmann, Napoleon III's *Préfet de la Seine* — the equivalent of mayor of Paris — displaced 25,000 people and destroyed ninety streets, which had, admittedly, become squalid and notoriously dangerous at night. In their place rose four imposing Neoclassical edifices, largely given over to housing the law and police. The few corners untouched by Haussmann include the tranquil square du Vert-Galant, place Dauphine, and the medieval streets rues Chanoinesse, des Ursins and de la Colombe, north of the cathedral. **[RG:haussmann_erasure]**

## STOP — Square du Vert-Galant

> Steps beside the statue lead down to the square du Vert-Galant, where Jacques de Molay, Grand Master of the Templars, was burnt at the stake, cursing the kings as he was consumed by the flames. **[F:vert_galant_molay_curse]**

> The nickname *Vert-Galant* — the Green Gallant — honours the king's famously amorous career. From the garden, the view of the Hôtel de la Monnaie and the dome of the Institut de France on the left, the Louvre on the right, and the Pont-des-Arts ahead is unforgettable. **[V:vert_galant_view]**

> Henri IV's early adventures had earned him that affectionate nickname. The charm faded as he grew older. The population resented his dozens of bastard children scattered through the kingdom, which all but plunged France into civil strife again. Worse, he had expressed his intention to marry his favourite, Gabrielle d'Estrées — an outrage to the *raison d'état*. Only her premature death prevented the infamy. **[V:vert_galant_tarnished]**

## STOP — Place Dauphine

`[GLUE: Cross back over the bridge and turn right into the brick-and-stone triangular square just east of the statue.]`

> Place Dauphine was created by Henri IV in 1607 in honour of his son the *Dauphin*, the future Louis XIII, and enclosed on all sides by arcaded brick-and-stone houses — like its contemporary, the Place des Vosges in the Marais. The location was deliberate: bankers and tradesmen could conduct their business undisturbed, a few steps from the Louvre. In the 18th century, talented young artists not yet admitted to the official Salons were allowed to hold an annual exhibition here. Today the business and the art trade have moved elsewhere, and the square has become a quiet, provincial-feeling nest of boutiques and pretty eating-places — a haven of peace in the heart of Paris. **[V:place_dauphine_henri_iv]**

> By night the square is lit up by its lamp-posts like a shining diamond. Simone Signoret and Yves Montand made their home here — Paris's romantic gem was once theirs. **[V:dauphine_signoret_montand]**

## STOP — Conciergerie

> From the statue, cross the road into place Dauphine and turn left into rue de Harlay, walking around the Conciergerie. Turn right into boulevard du Palais, cross over to place Louis Lépine, stopping to admire the Conciergerie and the Sainte-Chapelle. **[F:transit_pont_neuf_to_palais]**

### salle-des-gens-darmes
> The Conciergerie is one of the few remaining vestiges of the old medieval Palais de Justice and is Paris's oldest prison — where Marie-Antoinette and the leading figures of the Revolution were incarcerated before execution. Inside are several splendid, vaulted Gothic halls, including the *Salle des Gens d'Armes* — Hall of the Men-at-Arms — built in 1301 to 1315. The far end is separated off by an iron grille; during the Revolution this area was reserved for the *pailleux*, prisoners who couldn't afford to bribe a guard for their own cell and had to sleep on straw — *paille*. **[RG:conciergerie_pailleux]**

> It became a prison in 1391 and remained so until 1914. Not unlike a modern *concierge* supplementing his income, the Palace concierge profited from vendors who leased the many shops within the Palace walls. But the prison's reality was far darker. **[V:conciergerie_origin_concierge]**

### tour-bonbec
> The westernmost tower facing the Seine is where prisoners were tortured. Since their screams could be heard across the river, it is called the *Tour Bonbec* — Big Mouth Tower. **[F:tour_bonbec_screams]**

> In the 15th century passers-by could still hear the screams of tortured prisoners from the Tour Bonbec. During the tower's restoration in 1828, workers found two *oubliettes* in the basement: concealed dungeons spiked with iron points and connected to the river. Needless to dwell on the fate of the prisoners dropped into them. The name *bon-bec* — medieval French for prate — probably alluded to the interrogations on the ground floor. **[V:tour_bonbec_oubliettes]**

### Notorious prisoners
> Among the prisoners held here in later centuries were Ravaillac — the assassin of Henri IV — the mass poisoner La Marquise de Brinvilliers, who had eliminated the better part of her own household, and Cartouche, who lived from 1693 to 1721, the most notorious bandit of his age. To be admitted into Cartouche's gang you had to prove yourself an expert cutpurse: candidates practised on dummies hung with little bells, and had to strip the dummies without the slightest tinkling. All three awaited execution here. **[V:notorious_prisoners_cartouche]**

### salle-de-toilette + Marie-Antoinette's cell
> Beyond the great hall is a corridor where prisoners were allowed to wander freely. Among the reconstructed rooms here is the *salle de toilette*, where the condemned had their hair cropped and their shirt collars ripped off in preparation for the guillotine. **[RG:salle_de_toilette]**

> During the Revolution the Conciergerie boasted an impressive list of prisoners awaiting their deaths. Madame Roland, who had supported the Revolution but sided with the Girondins at the wrong moment. Marie-Antoinette, who occupied a dreary cell from 2 August to 16 October 1793, until she was taken to the guillotine on the Place de la Révolution — today's Place de la Concorde. Later Danton, Robespierre, and Saint-Just were detained here in turn — Danton and Robespierre occupying the cells adjacent to the Queen's. In all, some 2,780 victims of the Revolutionary Tribunal passed through the arcade entrance to the jeers of delighted *tricoteuses* huddled on the *perron*. **[V:revolution_prisoners]**

> On the upper storey is a mock-up of Marie-Antoinette's cell, in which the condemned queen's crucifix hangs forlornly against peeling *fleur-de-lys* wallpaper. **[RG:marie_antoinette_cell_mockup]**

### rue-de-paris-cells / The Sansons
> North of the Gothic halls ran a section of the prison leading to the dark cells, known as *rue de Paris* — named after the Great Executioner, *Monsieur de Paris*. That post was held by seven successive generations of the Sanson family, from 1688 to 1847, transmitted from father to son. Shortly before the Revolution, Louis XVI had set his heart on improving the executioners' plight and granted Charles-Henri Sanson an annual fee of 16,000 pounds plus 2,000 crowns for the upkeep of the scaffold and pillory — never suspecting he would become his own executioner. Being a royalist, Charles-Henri had no desire to carry out that deed. On 15 August 1792 he was arrested as 'the despot's employee'; with each execution he was dispatched from his cell to the Place du Carrousel, where the guillotine then stood, and afterwards taken back. Once the guillotine began to work at full swing and his expert hand could not be spared, he was released. He retired soon after he had executed his sovereign. His son Henri held the position through the Terror and well into the reign of Louis-Philippe. **[V:sanson_dynasty — anchor]**

> The film-maker Jean Renoir marvelled that his own grandfather — the painter Renoir's father, a tailor from Limoges — had known Henri Sanson's chief assistant during the Terror. The old assistant, Renoir wrote, "may have passed Voltaire and Benjamin Franklin in the street, heard Mozart play the harpsichord, and attended performances of Monsieur Beaumarchais." Old Renoir met him in 1845. The two became friends — the tailor and the executioner's assistant — united by the pleasure of doing a good job. "After all," Jean Renoir observed, "they were two good workmen: one cut out cloth, the other cut off heads just as conscientiously." **[V:renoir_executioner_friendship]**

### Tour de l'Horloge
> Outside the Conciergerie stands the *Tour de l'Horloge* — Clock Tower — built around 1350, so called because it displayed Paris's first public clock. The ornate face, set against a background of *fleur-de-lys*, is flanked by statues representing Law and Justice, added in 1585. The clock tower's bell — which once rang out to mark special royal occasions, and sounded during the St Bartholomew's Day massacre — was melted down during the Commune. **[RG:tour_horloge]**

## STOP — Sainte-Chapelle

> The only part of the Île de la Cité's old palace that remains in its entirety is the Sainte-Chapelle, its fragile-looking spire soaring above the Palais buildings, its excessive height in relation to its length giving it the appearance of a lopped-off cathedral choir. **[RG:sainte_chapelle_lopped_choir]**

> It was built by Saint Louis to house a piece of the Holy Cross and the Crown of Thorns, which he bought from Baudouin II, the last Emperor of Constantinople, who was in desperate need of funds to defend his city. The architects — Pierre de Montreuil, or perhaps Jean de Chelles — were set an unusual brief: design a chapel to have the light, lacy aspect of a reliquary. The result was stunningly beyond expectation. It took barely five years. The silver and gold reliquary that originally held the Crown of Thorns was sent to the Mint during the Revolution, but its contents — genuine or not — miraculously survived and are kept today at Notre-Dame. The chapel itself was shown no such consideration: it served as a flour store, then as a depot for court archives, growing so dilapidated that in 1847 it still bore an inscription reading 'National property, for sale.' Fortunately enlightened souls called on Viollet-le-Duc, Lassus, and others to restore the church, and 720 of the 1,134 original stained-glass scenes were preserved. **[V:sainte_chapelle_full_history]**

### upper-chapel
> The Sainte-Chapelle actually consists of two chapels: the simple lower chapel was intended for the servants, and the upper chapel, reached via a spiral staircase, was reserved for the court. The upper chapel is dazzling — its walls made almost entirely of stained glass held up by powerful supports that the medieval builders cleverly crafted to appear delicate and fragile by dividing them into clusters of pencil-thin columns. When the sun streams through, the glowing blues and reds of the stained glass dapple the interior and you feel like you're surrounded by myriad brilliant butterflies. **[RG:upper_chapel_butterflies]**

> There are 1,113 glass panels in the upper chapel, two-thirds of them original — the rest date from the nineteenth-century restoration. Together they tell virtually the entire story of the Bible: starting on the north side with Genesis, continuing with the Passion of Christ at the east end and the history of the Sainte-Chapelle relics on the south side, and ending with the Apocalypse in the rose window. **[RG:upper_chapel_glass_panels]**

> On 24 May 1871, barely four years after the full restoration was complete, the Communards poured petrol over the Sainte-Chapelle and only failed to set it on fire for lack of time. Everything you see inside is what they almost burned. **[V:sainte_chapelle_communards_petrol]**

## TRANSIT — to Notre-Dame

> Walk along rue de Lutèce; the colourful flower market is on the left. Turn right into rue de la Cité, then left onto the place du Parvis, in front of Notre-Dame. **[F:transit_lutece_to_parvis]**

## STOP — Hôtel-Dieu (south side of the parvis)

> The 19th-century hospital, *Hôtel-Dieu* — Hostel of God — is on the left side of the square. This building replaced the medieval hospital that was run by the cathedral. In medieval times there were three people per bed — one sick, one dying and one dead. **[F:hotel_dieu]**

> Founded in the 12th century by Maurice de Sully, the same bishop who initiated Notre-Dame, this was for centuries the only hospital serving the city, and conditions were appalling. Contemporary accounts described the wards as containing 'the ill, the dying and the dead' sharing the same beds. Philippe-Auguste donated the straw from his royal horses to serve as bedding. The stench was so overwhelming that visitors could smell the hospital from across the river. The original buildings were on the Pont au Double, linking the island to the Left Bank — and that bridge featured an enclosed glass gallery where recovering patients could promenade, an early form of therapeutic architecture. **[LEG:hotel_dieu_founding + LEG:wards_three_per_bed + LEG:pont_au_double_gallery]**

## STOP — Crypte Archéologique

> Beneath the parvis is the *Crypte Archéologique* — Archaeological Crypt — where remains of Gallo-Roman houses can be seen alongside the foundations of ancient buildings, a medieval road and part of Paris's third-century city wall. The atmospherically-lit excavation holds the remains of the original cathedral, *St-Étienne*, plus vestiges of the streets and houses that once clustered around Notre-Dame — most are medieval, but some date as far back as Gallo-Roman times and include parts of a Roman *hypocaust*, an underfloor heating system. **[F:crypte + RG:crypte_archeologique]**

## STOP — Notre-Dame parvis [parvis + kilometre-zero]

> Notre-Dame, at the heart of Paris, is also the symbolic heart of France. Outside on the pavement by the cathedral's west door is a bronze star, known as *kilomètre zéro*, from where all main-road distances in France are calculated. The large windswept square in front of the cathedral, built by Haussmann in the 1860s, is the *Parvis* — from the word paradise — Notre-Dame. White paving blocks set into it trace the outlines of the small streets and buildings that stood here in medieval times. **[RG:kilometre_zero_parvis]**

> A brass stud in the pavement marks the Zero Point of Paris. This has become a 'Wishing Stone' but is actually the point from which distances to other towns are measured. **[F:wishing_stone]**

## STOP — Notre-Dame [facade + anchor]

> A Gothic masterpiece, the *Cathédrale de Notre-Dame* — Cathedral of Our Lady — rears up from the Île de la Cité like a great ship moored by huge flying buttresses. It was built on the site of the Merovingian cathedral of *St-Étienne*, itself sited on the old Roman temple to Jupiter. Construction began in 1160 under the auspices of Bishop de Sully and was completed around 1345. The cathedral's seminaries became an ecclesiastical powerhouse, churning out six popes during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, though it later lost some of its pre-eminence to other sees, such as Rheims and St-Denis. The building fell into decline, and during the Revolution the frieze of Old Testament kings on the façade was damaged by enthusiasts who mistook them for the kings of France. Napoleon restored some of the cathedral's prestige, crowning himself emperor here in 1804 — though the walls were so dilapidated they had to be covered with drapes to provide a sufficiently grand backdrop. **[RG:great_ship_construction — anchor]**

> The first stone of the cathedral was laid at the rear end in 1163, and the builders finished with the façade in the 1220s — some sixty years at work. *Notre-Dame* means *Our Lady* — the Virgin Mary. **[F:sixty_year_construction] [facade]**

> The façade is Notre-Dame's most impressive exterior feature. The Romanesque influence is still visible — not least in its solid H-shape — but the overriding impression is one of lightness and grace, created in part by the filigree work of the central rose window and the gallery above. **[RG:facade_lightness_grace] [facade]**

### Notre-Dame [facade-gallery-of-kings]
> The row of 27 huge figures beneath the rose window represents the biblical kings who are the ancestors of Christ. These statues are copies, because the originals, mistaken for French kings during the Revolution, were destroyed. The 28 heads were rediscovered in 1977 during construction work near the Opera and are now displayed at the Musée de Cluny. **[F:kings_of_judah_destroyed + LEG:kings_heads_found_1977]**

### Notre-Dame [central-portal]
> Of the magnificent carvings over the portals, perhaps the most arresting is the scene over the central one showing the Day of Judgement. The lower frieze is a whirl of movement as the dead rise from their graves, while Christ presides above, sending those on his right to heaven and those on his left to hell. All around the arch peer out alert and mischievous-looking angels, said to be modelled on the cathedral choirboys of the time. Above the main entrance, the sculptures show St Michael weighing souls, and a devil leading the damned towards the gates of hell. The curious figure in a round medallion at the foot of Christ on the central column is an allegory of *Alchemy*; the ladder of nine steps symbolises the path to attaining the secret knowledge. **[RG:central_portal_day_of_judgement + F:judgement_day_central_portal]**

### Notre-Dame [side-portals]
> The left portal shows Mary being crowned by Christ, with scenes of her life in the lower friezes, while the right portal depicts the Virgin enthroned and, below, episodes from the life of St Anne — Mary's mother — and the life of Christ. These are masterfully put together, using visual devices and symbols to communicate more than just the bare-bones story. In the nativity scene, for example, the infant Christ is placed above Mary to show his elevated status, and lies on an altar rather than in a crib, symbolising his future sacrifice. **[RG:side_portals_mary_st_anne]**

`[GLUE: Enter Notre-Dame through the right-hand portal.] [F:enter_right_portal]`

### Notre-Dame [interior-bell-tower-vestibule]
> Immediately inside the door you are beneath the bell tower. Look up at the vault to see the trapdoor through which the bells were hoisted. The two pillars that support the weight of the towers are massive. **[F:bell_trapdoor_pillars]**

### Notre-Dame [interior-nave]
> Inside Notre-Dame, you're struck immediately by the dramatic contrast between the darkness of the nave and the light falling on the first great clustered pillars of the choir. It is the end walls of the transepts, nearly two-thirds glass and including two magnificent rose windows coloured in imperial purple, that admit all this light. These, the vaulting and the soaring shafts reaching to the springs of the vaults are all definite Gothic elements, while a strong influence of Romanesque remains in the stout round pillars of the nave and the general sense of four-squareness. **[RG:interior_nave_rose_windows]**

> The cathedral itself can accommodate a congregation of 9,000. The only original 13th-century glass can be seen in the three rose windows. The façade rose is partially obscured by the organ, which is the largest in France, with 7,500 pipes, 110 stops and 5 keyboards — crafted by the great nineteenth-century organ-maker Aristide Cavaillé-Coll, one of France's finest. Free organ recitals are held every Saturday at 8 p.m. **[F:capacity_rose_windows_organ + RG:cavaille_coll_organ]**

> Notre-Dame was the birthplace of polyphonic music in Europe. In the 12th and 13th centuries, the composers Léonin and Pérotin developed the art of *organum* — music in multiple voices — at the cathedral's choir school. Their innovations laid the foundations for all Western harmony. **[LEG:polyphony_leonin_perotin]**

### Notre-Dame [choir + treasury]
> The 14th-century carved screen around the altar shows easily recognisable bible stories. The white marble *Pietà* at the main altar stands between statues of Louis XIII and his son Louis XIV. **[F:choir_screen_pieta] [choir]**

> At the rear, to the right, is the Treasury containing what is purported to be the Crown of Thorns — bought from the bankrupt Byzantine Empire by Saint Louis, the same relic that had once filled the Sainte-Chapelle. **[F:treasury_crown_of_thorns] [interior-treasury]**

### Notre-Dame [history & survival]
> Notre-Dame hosted only two coronations, both controversial: Henry VI of England, who had conquered France, and Emperor Napoleon. The legitimate French dynasties always used the cathedral of Reims. Henri IV's conversion to Catholicism was celebrated here in 1593 with a *Te Deum*, though the conversion ceremony itself took place at Saint-Denis. **[LEG:coronations]**

> Notre-Dame endured centuries of vandalism before the Revolution even began. The medieval chancel screen was destroyed in 1699 to make way for a more fashionable baroque interior. The original stained glass windows — except for the three great rose windows — were ripped out and replaced with plain white glass to 'let in more light.' During the Revolution, Notre-Dame was nearly lost forever. Stripped of its treasures and rededicated to the Cult of Reason, it was put up for sale as building material. A certain *Citoyen* Simon nearly purchased the cathedral — one of the closest brushes with destruction in the building's history. **[LEG:vandalism + LEG:nearly_sold]**

> In May 1871, during the Paris Commune, Communards attempted to burn Notre-Dame by piling chairs and furniture against the doors and setting them alight. Doctors and staff from the neighbouring Hôtel-Dieu rushed across and extinguished the flames, saving the cathedral from destruction. **[LEG:commune_fire]**

> In the 1820s Notre-Dame was given a much-needed restoration, largely thanks to a petition drawn up by Victor Hugo. Hugo had stirred public interest through his novel *Notre-Dame de Paris*, in which he lamented the building's sorry state — Gothic architecture was particularly favoured by Romantic novelists, who deemed the soaring naves of the great cathedrals singularly suited to sheltering tormented souls. The task was given to architect Viollet-le-Duc, who carried out an extensive and thorough renovation, some would say too thorough: he remade much of the statuary on the façade and added the steeple and the baleful-looking gargoyles. Viollet-le-Duc's parting contribution was a statue of himself among the angels lining the roof: it's the only one looking heavenwards. **[RG:hugo_petition_viollet_le_duc — anchor]**

### Notre-Dame [towers + left-tower]
> If you climb the towers — access is round the side of the cathedral — you can see Viollet-le-Duc's gargoyles and angels up close, as well as the great thirteen-ton Emmanuel Bell in the south tower. According to tradition, the ladies of the court threw their jewellery into the molten metal when the bell was recast in 1680, which is said to give it its exceptionally pure tone. It is only rung at Christmas and other special occasions, or in times of mourning — such as the day after 9/11. **[RG:emmanuel_bell_towers + LEG:emmanuel_bell_jewellery]**

> The Emmanuel bell hangs in the left tower but has not been rung since the Liberation of Paris, as its vibration causes structural damage. **[F:emmanuel_silent_since_liberation]**

> The buttresses have gutters along their backs, which channel rainwater into the mouths of the gargoyles, made famous by Hugo in *The Hunchback of Notre-Dame* and the subsequent film starring Charles Laughton as the hunchback, Quasimodo. **[F:hugo_quasimodo_gargoyles]**

### Notre-Dame [exterior-east-end-square-jean-xxiii]
> Leave the church by the north portal and turn right along rue d'Arcole, then right into rue Chanoinesse. Take the first left into picturesque rue de la Colombe, then turn right into rue des Ursins. Go up the steps on your left to the quai aux Fleurs and walk along the quai until you come to square Jean XXIII behind Notre-Dame. **[F:transit_chanoinesse_to_garden]**

> The views from the garden are superb. Stop to admire the Seine, the cathedral spire and its magnificent flying buttresses. These buttresses enabled the builders to achieve great height because they carry the weight of the stone-vaulted roof. The walls could be pierced with large bays containing stained glass because they were no longer needed for support. Before the discovery of this technique in the 13th century, large windows could not be used. **[F:flying_buttresses_great_height]**

### Sidebar — School for Scandal: Abélard & Héloïse
> On rue Chanoinesse you just walked through, the cathedral school of Notre-Dame — forerunner of the Sorbonne — once flourished. Around the year 1200, one of the teachers was Peter Abélard, a philosophical whizz kid and cocker of snooks at establishment intellectuals. He was very popular with students but not with the authorities, who thought they caught a distinct whiff of heresy. Forced to leave the school, he set up shop on the Left Bank with his disciples, in effect founding the University of Paris. Less successful was the story of his love life. While living near rue Chanoinesse, he fell passionately in love with his landlord's niece, Héloïse, and she with him. She gave birth to a baby, her uncle had Abélard castrated, and the story ended in convents, lifelong separation and lengthy correspondence. They were reunited in death, however, and lie side by side in Père-Lachaise cemetery. **[RG:abelard_heloise — sidebar]**

## CLOSE — Mémorial des Martyrs de la Déportation

> Cross *quai de l'Archevêché* to leave the garden. Walk into the *square de l'Île de France* at the east end of the island. Go down steps into Le Mémorial des Martyrs de la Déportation. **[F:transit_garden_to_memorial]**

> This is the stark and moving symbolic tomb of the 200,000 French who died in Nazi concentration camps — among them Resistance fighters, Jews and forced labourers. The Memorial is barely visible above ground; stairs hardly shoulder-wide descend into a space like a prison yard, and off here is a stifling crypt where thousands of quartz pebbles represent the dead. Floor and ceiling are black, and it ends in a dark, raw hole, with a single naked bulb hanging in the middle. On either side are empty barred cells. Above the exit are the words *"Pardonne. N'oublie pas"* — Forgive. Do not forget. The little green park surrounding the memorial is more of a celebration of life and a popular hangout. **[RG:memorial_pardonne_noublie_pas — anchor]**

> The poet Robert Desnos, active in the Resistance, died at the Theresienstadt concentration camp just weeks before liberation. **[LEG:desnos_theresienstadt]**
