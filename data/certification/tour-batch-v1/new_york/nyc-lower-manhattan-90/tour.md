## 1. National Museum of the American Indian

Settle in.

Stand inside Bowling Green Park at the very foot of Broadway and turn north, up toward the bull.

From here Broadway runs twelve miles, the longest avenue in America — yet until the second quarter of the nineteenth century the city itself went no farther than City Hall, a fifteen-minute walk north.

The ground under your feet carries the city's oldest story, because the original Dutch fort stood in this exact spot, and Bowling Green is reputedly where the Dutch struck their deal for Manhattan Island with its Native inhabitants.

The grand building in front of you is the Alexander Hamilton U.S. Customs House, designed by Cass Gilbert and finished in 1907 — a Beaux Arts colossus with over four hundred and fifty thousand square feet inside.

And it mattered enormously, because before 1913, when Washington first taxed personal income, the federal government ran almost entirely on customs duties from imported goods — and a full seventy-five percent of that money came through the Port of New York, counted right here.

Beaux arts means simply "fine arts," after the great École des Beaux Arts in Paris, but in New York the phrase takes on something larger — buildings that wear their civic pride out loud, grand enough to match what they do, whether they house trains, books, or tax collectors.

Today it holds the Smithsonian's museum in the George Gustav Heye Center, spread across two floors, where exhibits of Native literature, language, history, and art fill a splendid elliptical Rotunda lit from above by a glass skylight that weighs a hundred and forty tons.

Before you go in, look up.

Twelve figures ring the top, each standing for a great trading nation of history — Greece, Rome, Phoenicia, Genoa, Venice, Spain, Holland, Portugal, Denmark, England, France, and Belgium.

Belgium? That one hides a story.

The official line is that during World War I, 'vandals' carved 'Belgium' across a shield that had originally read 'Germany' — and no one has ever explained how vandals could quietly re-cut one of the most visible, most heavily guarded buildings in the city.

## 2. Bowling Green Park

Leaving the National Museum of the American Indian behind, head for Bowling Green Park, about a three-minute walk away.

You've just stepped out of the old Custom House that Cass Gilbert designed in 1907.

Before the income tax arrived in 1913, customs were the government's main money — and three-quarters of it came through the Port of New York, right where you stand.

Now you're at the foot of Broadway, the longest avenue in America at twelve miles to the top of Manhattan, on the ground where the original Dutch fort once stood.

One night in 1989, the artist Arturo Di Modica backed a truck up to the New York Stock Exchange and dumped a seven-thousand-pound bronze bull beneath its giant Christmas tree.

Nobody had asked him for it.

A recession, two years after a market crash, had moved him to make it — a piece of pure boosterism, a symbol of the bull markets he was sure were coming.

New Yorkers loved the thing so much the city decided to keep the gift, though it moved Charging Bull to the park's northern edge, where it stands today.

You're standing in New York's oldest public park, Bowling Green, the city's first official green, opened in 1733.

Its ground had already done every kind of duty under the Dutch and the English — pasture for cows, parade ground for soldiers, and, true to the name, a bowling field for colonists.

And supposedly this very green is where the Dutch settler Peter Minuit bought Manhattan from Native Americans for the equivalent of twenty-four dollars.

## 3. Fraunces Tavern

From Bowling Green Park, carry on to Fraunces Tavern, about an 8-minute walk away.

Here's the first surprise — the tavern in front of you isn't quite the one Washington knew.

How much of it is genuinely old is a matter of dispute: one account calls it a conjectural rebuild the Sons of the Revolution financed in 1907, with only a handful of original bricks left, while another dates the reconstruction to 1904, built from the period's architectural styles, and reckons about forty percent is original.

The style is Georgian, named for those British kings — though the new, antiroyalist states were quick to rebrand it "colonial" or "federal."

Long before any revolution, this was a private mansion — a wedding present that Colonel Stephanus Van Cortlandt, the city's tenth mayor, handed to his son-in-law Stephen Delancey, the French immigrant whose name still hangs on Delancey Street.

The tavern that made the place famous was run by Samuel Fraunces, an immigrant from the West Indies who'd go on to become Washington's steward.

It's where the Committee of Correspondence — the body that became the Continental Congress — held its very first meeting, and where the Sons of Liberty gathered dozens of times to plot how to throw the British out.

When the war was won, Washington came back here to say goodbye to his officers in his famous Farewell to the Troops, and Alexander Hamilton set up his first Office of the Treasury under this roof.

Look east along Pearl Street and you'll catch one of the last block-long runs of late-1700s and early-1800s shipping offices and warehouses in the city — among the few buildings that came through the Great Fire of 1835 untouched, and, thanks to the New York Landmarks Conservancy, saved to become the Fraunces Tavern Block Historic District.

The darkest chapter, though, is the most recent one.

In 1975 the Armed Forces Puerto Rican National Liberation Front bombed Fraunces Tavern, killing four people and injuring dozens.

They chose it deliberately, for two reasons — as a symbol of American ideals, and because it's one of the few landmark buildings in New York with a Caribbean connection.

## 4. Wall Street

From Fraunces Tavern, make your way onto Wall Street — about a nine-minute walk.

Take a second to think about where you've been.

Inside Fraunces Tavern, Washington gave his Farewell to the Troops, and Hamilton set up his very first Office of the Treasury.

The Sons of Liberty had gathered in that same building to plot evicting the British — and in 1975 the FALN bombed it, killing four.

You began the morning at Bowling Green, the city's first official park, laid out in 1733.

Now start at 1 Liberty Plaza, the U.S. Steel Building, which quietly holds a record.

The tallest building ever demolished once stood right here — the Singer Building.

When it came down, the grief wasn't quite what New York felt when Pennsylvania Station was slaughtered in 1963, but its long place in the skyline still brought tears of rage.

And when crews dug the foundations here from 1979 to 1982, they turned up three-century-old fragments of the Lovelace Tavern, once housed nearby.

Head west to 14 Wall, the Bankers Trust Building.

Bankers Trust is gone, but Trowbridge and Livingston's pyramidal crown remains, modeled on antiquity's Mausoleum at Halicarnassus and still one of the great pinnacles in a city bathed in sumptuous finishing touches.

J. P. Morgan even shacked up beneath that pyramid so he could watch his own building rise across the street.

For years the pyramid was the bank's logo, though — such is the depth of these downtown canyons — you can't see it from Wall Street itself.

Duck inside for the bronze lobby gates, cast with the enterprises of capitalism: metallurgy, shipping, mining, agriculture, and power.

At 22 William, between Beaver and Exchange Place, is the City Bank Farmers' Trust Building of 1931.

Its irregular site pushed the architects to experiment, so it pivots off the grid as if it's about to blast off — always a curiosity in these canyons.

The bronze doors are the showpiece, filled with trains, boats, and airplanes, and inside there's more than forty kinds of marble laced with nickel and copper.

Cross to the west side of Broadway, where No. 25 was Cunard's central ticket office.

Cunard led the pack among the era's steamship lines, and in 1921 put up this building for the firm that ran the fastest, most luxurious liners in the world.

In the age of expensive cruises and mass immigration, that was huge business — so much so that this bottom stretch of Broadway went by a nickname, Steamship Row.

Step inside, but not on a Sunday, for spectacular wall frescoes and maps tracing the old Cunard routes.

Now look up Broadway to No. 26, on the corner of Beaver, crowned with a cast-iron oil burner.

That lantern tells you exactly what the building was for: it housed Standard Oil, John D. Rockefeller's monopolistic firm.

But by the time this tower went up in 1924, the flame was long out — in 1911 the U.S. Supreme Court had ordered the Standard Oil Holding Company dissolved and its directors stripped of control over the subsidiaries.

The offices lingered here until the 1930s, when the firm moved uptown to the new Rockefeller Center.

And long before any of it, in 1796, another financial giant lived on this very spot — Alexander Hamilton kept his town residence here.

On the corner of Pearl and Whitehall, No. 33 houses NASDAQ, the National Association of Securities Dealers, though unlike the Stock Exchange its visitor center sits up in Times Square, not here.

The real story is across the street to the south, where the Whitehall Induction Center once stood — the place Arlo Guthrie immortalized in "Alice's Restaurant" as where he was "injected, inspected, detected, infected, neglected, and selected," and where, in 1967, during Stop the Draft Week, they arrested Benjamin Spock, the famed baby doctor turned antiwar leader, alongside the poet Allen Ginsberg.

End at 55 Wall Street, a building born from disaster.

The Great Fire of 1835 took seven hundred buildings, the Merchants Exchange among them, and Isaiah Rogers designed this to replace it in 1842.

It served as the U.S. Custom House from 1863 to 1899, then later as a branch of Citibank.

Watch how the columns stack — a lower Ionic colonnade from 1842, each shaft a single block of stone, working hand in glove with a Corinthian row added seventy years above it.

Inside, the lobby runs 198 feet long and 132 feet high, and holds the largest display of Wedgwood china in the world around its central chandelier.

## 5. Federal Reserve Bank of New York

Leave Wall Street behind — the Federal Reserve Bank of New York is about eight minutes ahead.

Here's a question worth chewing on as you walk: what happens when a whole country's money rests on one man's nerve?

In 1907, that man was J. P. Morgan, who personally mobilized enough banking support to stave off a collapse that could have rivaled the Great Depression — and seven years later, in 1914, the Federal Reserve was created so a central bank, not a single tycoon, would make that call.

The building in front of you went up like a fortress in 1924, and it's no New York snobbery to call it the most significant of the thirteen Federal Reserve sites.

Look at the limestone as you pass — it weathers in different tones, shifting light to dark — and those magnificent wrought-iron lanterns are the work of Philadelphia's Samuel Yellin.

Nearby, Federal Hall, a Greek Revival masterpiece, holds a museum devoted to postcolonial New York.

But the real story is eighty feet down.

Descend to the basement of this financial fortress and you'll see more lucre than you ever will again in your life — behind a door a good five feet thick sits a gold vault straight out of Mission: Impossible.

It holds upward of $110 billion in bullion — about a quarter of the official monetary gold of eighty countries, a full twenty-five percent of all the gold reserves on Earth, and far more than is kept in Fort Knox.

Ninety-five percent of it belongs to foreign nations, who trust it here because, embedded in the bedrock of Manhattan and guarded by a small army of marksmen with their own on-site firing range, it's considered the safest place in the world for such storage.

And how do they move it from one country's room to the next? Wheelbarrows — still.

No currency has been printed here since that function moved to New Jersey in 1992.

If you want to see it for yourself, the hour-long, reservation-only tour at 33 Liberty Street takes you into the gold vault and the bank's museum, where money from all over the world is on display.

## 6. 9/11 Memorial and Museum

From the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, carry on about twelve minutes on foot to the 9/11 Memorial and Museum.

Before you head in, look hard at the glass pavilion out front — it leans, on purpose, like a tower knocked onto its side.

Above ground, the eight-acre Memorial Plaza is built around the one-acre footprints of both towers, and if you're short on time, that's the part to walk — either way, reserve your tickets online first to skip some of the line at this sobering place.

Most museums send you up.

After a thorough security check, this one takes you down, down, down, because the whole museum lives underground — a descent meant to echo the escape route survivors took by stair, and the darkness the attacks plunged the country into.

On the way down the escalator you pass beneath two steel tridents pulled from the bedrock at the base of the North Tower, each one weighing fifty tons.

They once helped the towers soar more than 1,360 feet, and when everything else came down, they stayed standing in the sea of rubble — instant symbols of resilience.

At the bottom of the last staircase, oddly to the left when everyone wants to turn right, is the museum's beating heart — its history exhibition.

It mixes video, survivor audio, and wall text with artifacts you won't shake: burnt-edged papers that fluttered from the towers, children's clothing recovered from Flight 93.

Among more than 10,300 objects here are the Vesey Street Stairs, dubbed the Survivors' Staircase, which let hundreds of workers flee the site that morning — and a remnant of them sits right beside the museum's own staircase.

At the foot of that staircase is the In Memoriam gallery, its walls lined with the photographs and names of everyone who perished.

Around the corner stands the burnt-out cab of the Fire Department's Engine Company 21, one of the largest artifacts on display — a blunt measure of the inferno the responders walked into.

It marks the entrance to the main exhibition, split into three parts: Events of the Day, Before 9/11, and After 9/11.

That exhibition spills into the monumental Foundation Hall, where a massive section of the original slurry wall — built to hold back the Hudson while the towers rose — still stands.

Beside it is the last steel column removed during the cleanup, covered in messages and mementos left by recovery workers, first responders, and loved ones.

Nearby you'll find the Ground Zero cross — a severed chunk of beams that fell by accident into the shape of a cross — alongside a fire truck crushed flat.

One thought-provoking section traces the rise of Al Qaeda, down to a single brick from Bin Laden's compound in Abbottabad.

And the Memorial Room does something quieter and harder — it shows the face and tells the story of every single person who died.

One of those stories sits in a dust-covered wallet that belonged to Robert Joseph Gschaar, an insurance underwriter who worked on the 92nd floor of the South Tower.

Inside were a photo of his wife, Myrta, and a two-dollar bill — because when he proposed, a second marriage for them both, he gave her a two-dollar bill as a symbol of their second chance at happiness, and kept the matching one for himself.

When you climb back into the light, look for the Oculus, Santiago Calatrava's dramatic white structure over the new WTC Transportation Hub, said to be inspired by a dove taking flight.

Built from 36,500 tons of steel, it streams daylight into a 3.9-billion-dollar station that moves 250,000 train commuters a day, two and a half times the size of Grand Central, with levels of shops and restaurants.

And every September 11, its central skylight is opened for 102 minutes — the exact stretch of time from the first attack to the collapse of the second tower.

## 7. City Hall

From the 9/11 Memorial and Museum, make your way to City Hall — about a sixteen-minute walk.

Before this last stop, think back to where you began — Bowling Green, New York's oldest public park, where the story goes that Peter Minuit bought Manhattan for twenty-four dollars.

You've walked the whole spine of the old city, because until the second quarter of the nineteenth century, New York went no farther north than City Hall.

Notice how close you can actually get, because if you can approach the building at all, it means New York has eased its restrictions on people nearing the seat of government.

That seat has been here since 1812 — the mayor's office, the city council, and a clutter of other offices under one Federal-style roof.

Inside there's a small collection of memorabilia, and free tours run twice a week on weekdays — though you'll need to book online.

They take you through the coffered rotunda and the City Council chamber to the Governor's Room, where portraits by John Trumbull hang near George Washington's writing table and the surviving remnants of a flag flown at his 1789 inauguration.

City Hall was finished in 1811, only the third New York has ever had, and it came in under its $250,000 budget.

Most of it was marble.

The north face and basement, though, got cheaper brownstone, because the planners bet hardly anyone would bother looking at the building from behind.

Then, between 1954 and 1956, crews stripped the top four inches off the entire facade, recut it in Alabama veined limestone, redid the basement in red granite, and re-carved every column, capital, balustrade, and length of trim.

So the graceful building in front of you is, stone for stone, a replica of the original.

And that brings our walk to a close.
