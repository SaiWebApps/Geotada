## 1. Battery Park

Settle in.

Walk into Battery Park, toward the river, and find a spot where you can see the water.

Now look at that harbor.

It's the reason this whole city got built.

Back in 1609, Henry Hudson and a crew of sixteen sailed up this river, certain they'd find Asia waiting at its mouth.

They were wrong about Asia, but Hudson's reports on the great harbor and the money to be made in animal pelts pulled the Dutch across the Atlantic to settle.

They guessed, rightly, that this harbor would be the hinge connecting Europe to the whole rich interior of the continent, by way of the river.

And once the Erie Canal linked the Hudson to the Great Lakes, New York became the most powerful city in the nation.

Those first Dutch settlers landed right here in 1625, and to guard the pocket of land they called New Amsterdam they lined a battery of cannon along the shore.

That row of guns is exactly where the park gets its name.

There's a round stone fort in the park called Castle Clinton, thrown up to hold off a British invasion in 1812.

After the war it found a second life as an immigration station, taking in newcomers before Ellis Island ever opened, and today it holds displays of historic Manhattan.

It's also where you buy your ferry ticket out to the Statue of Liberty and Ellis Island.

These twenty-five acres are downtown's largest public space, laid out with gardens, sculptures, and winding walkways.

One memorial remembers the dead of the Korean War; another honors the Italian navigator Giovanni da Verrazzano.

Look out and the Statue of Liberty stands across the harbor, with tugboats and barges working the water past her.

And if it all looks familiar, this is where Madonna worked her magic in the 1985 film Desperately Seeking Susan.

That's Battery Park — where a harbor made a city.

## 2. New York Stock Exchange

Leaving Battery Park behind, head for the New York Stock Exchange, about a sixteen-minute walk away.

Walk around Castle Clinton, past the ferry terminal, and head inland toward the tall buildings.

Look for the scraggly little buttonwood tree out front — a sycamore, easy to miss.

It stands in for the buttonwood where twenty-four brokers gathered in 1792 to trade the young nation's Revolutionary War debt — the first stock market in America.

Raise your eyes from that twig to the columns above, and you're looking at the world's most famous — some say infamous — financial institution.

The towering columns, the crowded pediment, the huge flag all trumpet might and prestige, far grander than the plain Federal Hall across the street.

And here's the odd part — though the world links it forever with Wall Street, its front actually faces Broad Street.

The real buttonwood stood further east, on Wall Street itself, and the deal the brokers struck there became known as the Buttonwood Agreement.

As the history called Gotham puts it, that agreement "laid the foundations for a structured securities market."

Early the next year the traders moved indoors, to the Tontine Coffee House at the corner of Water and Wall.

That corner hides an ugly past — it had been the site of the Royal African Trading Company, where the English bought and sold enslaved Africans.

Across the way, on the southeast corner of Broad and Wall, look for a building with no sign at all.

That's J. P. Morgan's bank.

Morgan wanted it plain — if you didn't know what it was, you had no business being there.

Its only decoration is a scatter of pockmarks along the Wall Street side, scars from a bomb blamed on anarchists that killed thirty-eight people and wounded hundreds in 1920.

Morgan left them there as a testament to his own invincibility.

The exchange gives no public tours now, but you can raise a coffee to the brokers over in Zuccotti Park, birthplace of Occupy Wall Street.

That's the New York Stock Exchange — a scraggly tree out front, and a bank next door that kept its scars.

## 3. Your finish point

From here, make your way to your final destination.

The downtown that began as a 1625 Dutch settlement now holds towers like this.

Look up — when the Woolworth Building opened in 1913, nothing on Earth stood taller.

Cass Gilbert designed it, sixty stories climbing 792 feet, a neo-Gothic tower elegantly dressed in masonry and terra-cotta.

That reign didn't last, though; in 1930 the Chrysler Building rose higher and surpassed it.

Nearby stands another landmark, the building at 1 Wall Street.

Today it houses the Bank of New York.

That's the walk — the tower that once stood taller than anything on Earth.
