## 1. Washington Square Park

Settle in.

You're starting in the West Village, right at Washington Square Park, so take a moment and take it in.

Half the stories on this block were secrets when they happened — and the first one is hiding on a façade above your head.

Look up at 82 Washington Place.

From about 1908 to 1913, this was home to the novelist Willa Cather and her partner, Edith Lewis.

Cather wrote My Ántonia and Death Comes for the Archbishop, had two central relationships with women, and as an undergraduate at the University of Nebraska signed her letters to a woman she admired as 'William.'

The two of them stayed together at addresses around the Village for nearly forty years, until Cather died in 1947.

But she guarded that life fiercely — she burned most of her personal letters before she died, and the one surviving college note laments her 'unnatural' attraction to another woman.

A few steps on, at 86 University Place, a very different door once opened.

In the 1950s this was the Bagatelle, one of the only lesbian bars in the whole city, alongside the more working-class Sea Colony and the Swing Rendezvous.

The poet and essayist Audre Lorde wrote about it in her autobiography, Zami: A New Spelling of My Name.

There was a tiny dance floor in back, and the front room, she remembered, 'smelled like plastic and blue glass and beer and lots of good-looking young women.'

Out and Black, Lorde and the other women like her rarely acknowledged one another inside — and yet, as she put it, 'the Bag was always The Club.'

Now picture 125 East Eleventh Street roaring.

From 1913 through the 1930s, Webster Hall there threw the annual Greenwich Village Ball — masquerade nights for the neighborhood's bohemians, many of them gay men in drag.

By the early 1920s, gay men and lesbians were holding events of their own there.

In 1934 the New York Herald Tribune tried to capture the scene: 'Men danced with women in men's clothes. Women danced with men in women's clothes. And strange androgynous couples careened about the floor, oblivious to the workings of society and nature.'

Today it's a popular, mostly straight nightclub.

From the 1920s into the 1950s, 171 West Twelfth Street held a whole network of lesbian couples and comrades: the artist Nancy Cook and the educator Marion Dickerman; the activist Polly Porter and the Democratic official Molly Dewson; and Grace Hutchins and Anna Rochester, both Communist Party leaders.

Cook and Dickerman especially were close friends with Eleanor Roosevelt, who kept apartments nearby at 20 East Eleventh Street and later at 29 Washington Square West.

When Franklin became president, Eleanor turned into a fierce champion for women in government, and her friends landed New Deal posts — Dewson ran the Women's Division of the Democratic National Committee, while the president's appointees included Frances Perkins, the first woman in a cabinet, and Grace Abbott of the Children's Bureau.

Eleanor had her own intimate relationship, with the Associated Press reporter Lorena Hickok, assigned in 1932 to cover the new first lady; their letters read as passionate, though many biographers still strenuously insist Roosevelt was not a lesbian.

One last house — 457 Sixth Avenue.

This was home to Murray Hall, a Tammany Hall 'ward heeler' who organized the vote right here in this district.

Married twice, a heavy drinker, a brawler, and by all accounts 'sweet on women,' Hall died in 1901, leaving a wife and an adopted daughter.

And then the revelation: Hall had died of breast cancer, and had been a woman who passed as a man for all of her adult life.

The New York Times ran the story for days.

One lawyer friend simply couldn't accept it: 'I saw him play poker like a veteran... I just can't believe he was a woman, that's all' — still calling Hall 'he.'

## 2. 29 Washington Place

Leaving Washington Square Park behind, head for 29 Washington Place, about a 4-minute walk away.

It's nearly five on a Saturday afternoon, March 25th, 1911, and some six hundred young women are finishing their shift in a garment factory spread across the eighth, ninth, and tenth floors right here — most of them Jewish and Italian immigrants, some as young as thirteen, none older than twenty-three.

Just as they're getting ready to leave, a fire springs up in the southeast corner of the eighth floor, and no one has ever worked out how it started.

Within minutes all three floors were an inferno — and an inescapable one, because many of the doors had been locked from the outside, to stop theft, the owners said afterward.

A fire escape collapsed, and the elevator operator ran.

But a man off the street named Joseph Zito rushed in, got the elevator working, and rode it up through the flames five times, hauling out twenty-five to thirty workers on every trip.

It still wasn't enough — some women suffocated, some were crushed at the locked doors, and others jumped to their deaths in front of horrified passersby below.

In all, 146 people were killed at the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory, most of them in the first ten minutes of a blaze that burned for an hour.

This time the city refused to look away, and after wave upon wave of protest the state legislature rewrote its labor code into the most stringent in the nation.

The labor movement, struggling until then, gathered steam, and the International Ladies Garment Workers Union — the ILGWU — grew into a real force in the industry.

## 3. Stonewall Inn

From 29 Washington Place, carry on to Stonewall Inn, about a 16-minute walk away.

The blocks you're leaving keep their own quiet history.

At 82 Washington Place, Willa Cather lived with Edith Lewis from about 1908 to 1913 — the writer who'd give us My Ántonia and Death Comes for the Archbishop, who signed her college letters 'William,' and who shared her life with Lewis for nearly forty years until her death in 1947, then destroyed most of her correspondence before she went.

A few streets on, at 86 University Place, the Bagatelle was one of the only lesbian bars in 1950s New York, and Audre Lorde wrote it into Zami: A New Spelling of My Name, remembering that 'the Bag was always The Club.'

Look at No. 53 — the bar that stood here, the Stonewall Inn, was nothing fancy, crowded on weekends and, like most bars serving a gay crowd, owned by the Mob, which paid off the police.

Raids and shakedowns were routine, and a light over the door flashed when the cops were coming.

In the early morning hours of June 28, 1969, the patrons fought back.

When police singled out several drag queens for arrest and shoved the rest into the street, one butch lesbian struck the officer who'd hit her, and the crowd let loose a hail of coins and invective.

The cops were driven back inside, the crowd attacked the building and tried to set it on fire, and the police drew their guns.

Riot police averted a shooting, but the crowd would not back down.

Those nights of unrest launched a new phase of activism — gay liberation.

This stretch of Christopher Street proclaims itself the birthplace of the modern lesbian and gay rights movement, where that police raid sparked the clash near Christopher Park that began the fight for equal rights.

Since then the park and its surroundings have become a symbol of gay liberation, and the street around you is lined with LGBTQ bars and shops.

On the riots' first anniversary, New York held its first Gay Pride march, up Sixth Avenue from Christopher Street to Central Park — and every June since, the world has remembered with parades.

As one activist quipped, he was probably one of six people who lived in the Village at the time who didn't claim to have been at the Stonewall riots.

The uprising spurred two organizations: the Gay Liberation Front, launched immediately after, and the Gay Activists Alliance, which split off in December 1969 after infighting over how far the work should reach.

The GLF saw itself as part of the New Left and pushed coalition politics, while the GAA's constitution barred any alliance with a group not directly tied to the homosexual cause.

Together they answered homophobia with 'zap' actions and forced a new visibility.

The first GLF meetings met at Alternate U, at 534 Sixth Avenue, and from 1971 to 1974 the GAA ran out of a renovated firehouse at 99 Wooster Street — a center of gay life until it was firebombed in October 1974, amid a rash of arson attacks on gay bars, homes, and offices.

In 1999 the blocks around the former inn became a National Historic Landmark — the Stonewall National Historic District, the first time the federal government recognized lesbian and gay history.

The bar standing here now, called Stonewall, isn't the original, though it keeps the room alive with gay cabaret, karaoke, and comedy.

In 2016, President Barack Obama went further, naming the Stonewall Inn, Christopher Park, and the surrounding streets a national monument — the first ever to honor the LGBTIQ+ civil rights movement.

Rangers stationed in the park, and the memorials around it, now tell more of the story.

And if you want the Village's other heartbeat, walk to Smalls at 183 West 10th Street — a hole-in-the-wall basement that still puts up-and-coming talent on its tiny stage.

## 4. Greenwich Village

From Stonewall Inn, make your way on to Greenwich Village, just ahead.

For more than a hundred years, these crooked streets were the center of New York's lesbian and gay life, and a walk through them still passes landmark after landmark of that past.

The city's literary life started near here too, with Washington Irving — the first American to make a living from writing.

Born and raised in post-colonial Manhattan, and famous for "Rip Van Winkle," he launched a satirical magazine called Salmagundi in 1807, and in its pages he coined "Gotham" as a nickname for New York — one that's clung to the city ever since.

A century and a half later, Allen Ginsberg, Jack Kerouac, and their friends spent many a happy night at the Kettle of Fish, a bar still going at 59 Christopher Street.

Here's a question the Village keeps asking: what do you call a love the law won't name?

In 1777, one man wrote to another: 'I wish, my dear Laurens, it might be in my power, by action rather than words, to convince you that I love you.' The writer was Alexander Hamilton, and the letter went to a fellow soldier in the Continental Army.

Historians still argue over letters like these — the effusive same-sex correspondence of the 1700s and 1800s they call 'romantic friendships,' written in an intensely homosocial age.

You find the same warmth in the letters of Ralph Waldo Emerson, Margaret Fuller, and Susan B. Anthony.

And by the late 1800s, women's long partnerships had a name of their own: 'Boston marriages,' borrowed straight from Henry James's novel The Bostonians.

The love that dare not speak its name has, in fact, gone by many.

In the 1860s a gay German writer and activist, Karl Heinrich Ulrichs, reached back to Plato's Symposium and coined 'Uranians' — people male or female in body but with the spirit of the opposite sex — a respectable label to replace 'pervert' and 'sodomite,' and the word homosexual first surfaced in an 1868 letter written to him.

On New York's own streets the words were blunter — a 'fairy' was an effeminate gay man, 'queer' the catch-all, and black gay men and lesbians spoke of being 'in the life.'

As for 'gay,' it first meant prostitutes — 'gay women' — before it became a code the police didn't crack until the late 1940s.

By the 1950s, those words were turning into organizing.

The Daughters of Bilitis started in San Francisco in 1955 and put out a magazine, The Ladder, from 1956 to 1970.

Its first New York chapter was organized in 1958 by the activist Barbara Gittings, and from 1963 it ran out of a basement office at 26-32 Charlton Street.

From there the chapter pushed into the open — the 'Annual Reminder' marches at Independence Hall in Philadelphia every year from 1963 to 1969, and a 1965 picket outside the White House against the ban on gay federal employees.

It was one of the 'homophile' groups of the fifties, alongside the Mattachine Society, founded in 1950, and ONE, in 1954 — and they played it careful, asking members to dress the part, men in suits, women in skirts.

And all of it circles back to one building nearby, the movement's most sacred site — the place that once housed the Stonewall Inn, where three days of unrest in June 1969 are widely counted as the spark of the modern lesbian-and-gay liberation movement.

One last thing, so you don't get lost down here.

Everything below 14th Street was settled first, so it never got Manhattan's tidy grid, and the streets curve, twist, and double back.

The famous troublemaker is West 4th, which goes rogue and angles north to cross 10th, 11th, 12th, and 13th — the kind of turn that stumps even locals.

Learn that one break in the grid, and you'll pass for a Village pro.

And on the corner of Grove Street, number 90 might look oddly familiar — it's the apartment building from Friends.

## 5. Bleecker Street

Leaving Greenwich Village behind, head for Bleecker Street, about a 6-minute walk away.

The Village you've been walking held the center of this city's lesbian and gay life for more than a hundred years.

Willa Cather signed her college letters 'William,' lived with Edith Lewis for nearly forty years, and burned most of her correspondence before she died in 1947.

Audre Lorde found her own refuge at the Bagatelle on University Place, where, she wrote in Zami, 'the Bag was always The Club.'

In 1892, a vice cop walked into a club on this street and left disgusted, filing a report on the waiters — 'men of degenerate type,' he wrote, some 'rouging their necks,' who sang 'filthy ditties' in falsetto and dropped uninvited into any visitor's chair.

The place was the Slide, a drag club an entrepreneur had opened in the early 1890s at 157 Bleecker Street, in the building that now houses Kenny's Castaways.

By then the whole Lower East Side had a name for scandalous 'resorts' — dancing halls, saloons and brothels where the city's 'inverts' and 'fairies' gathered for cross-dressing spectaculars, dancing, and back-room encounters.

Bleecker was notorious, second only to the Bowery for its rough and racy reputation, with Greene and Mercer Streets among the busiest red-light districts in the city.

Places like the Slide, or Columbia Hall — better known as Paresis Hall, at 392 Bowery — did a strange double duty: straight thrill-seekers came to gape and closeted middle-class men came for a secret thrill, yet they were also genuine centers of gay male social life and relatively safe places to gather.

For curious straights it was a stop on the voyeuristic 'slumming tours,' and detectives would steer out-of-towners hunting 'the low life' to the Slide first, as the New York Herald reported that same year.

The police shut the Slide down in 1892.

Down the block at 183 Bleecker Street, a sex club called the Black Rabbit, with gay-themed acts, held on until a police raid finally closed it in 1900.

Half a century later, a different kind of hidden life was being lived just up the street.

No. 337 Bleecker was the home of Lorraine Hansberry, whose A Raisin in the Sun opened on Broadway in 1959 and put a Black family's life on the American stage like nothing before it.

It ran two years, and made her the first Black playwright to win the New York Drama Critics Circle Award for Best Play of the year.

What almost no one knew was that through the 1950s she was quietly writing anonymous letters to One magazine and The Ladder, early gay-rights publications, while married to Robert Nemiroff, who knew she was a lesbian — the two divorced in 1964 but stayed close.

One last thing before we finish, and you'll smell it before I name it.

This is the most aromatic stretch of the whole tour — home to the fabulous Murray's Cheese and to Faicco's Italian Specialties, run by the same family since 1900 and selling what the book swears is the best sopressata in the United States.

And that closes the loop, back where we started.

Thank you for coming along with me today. When you're ready, take your time to keep exploring on your own.
