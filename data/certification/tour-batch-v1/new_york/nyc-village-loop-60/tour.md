## 1. 29 Washington Place

Settle in.

You're starting in West Village.

When you're ready, head for 29 Washington Place — about a 4-minute walk from here.

Look up at the corner of Washington Place and Greene Street, where the fire tore through the upper floors.

On March 25, 1911, a fire on this site killed 146 people and reshaped New York State law and the whole history of the American labor movement.

That Saturday, some 600 young women were at work in a garment factory on the eighth, ninth, and tenth floors — most of them Jewish and Italian immigrants, between 13 and 23.

At five, just as they got ready to leave, fire sprang up in the southeast corner of the eighth floor, and no one has ever learned how it started.

Within minutes all three floors were an inferno — and an inescapable one, because many of the doors had been locked from the outside, to stop theft, the owners later said.

A fire escape collapsed, and the elevator operator fled.

But a man on the street named Joseph Zito ran in, got the elevator working, and rode it up through the flames five times, carrying 25 or 30 workers out on every trip.

Even so, some of the young women jumped to their deaths in front of horrified passersby, others suffocated, and some were crushed against those locked doors.

Most of them died in the first ten minutes of an hour-long blaze at the Triangle Shirt Waist Factory.

After many protests, the state legislature answered by rewriting its labor code into the most stringent in the nation.

The labor movement had been struggling until then; now it gathered steam, and the International Ladies Garment Workers Union — the ILGWU — became a force in the industry.

That's 29 Washington Place — where 146 deaths changed the law.

## 2. Washington Square Park

Leaving 29 Washington Place behind, head for Washington Square Park, about a 4-minute walk away.

These blocks held the dance floors, and watched the Triangle fire kill 146.

Up the block sits 82 Washington Place.

From about 1908 to 1913 it was home to the novelist Willa Cather.

She lived there with her partner, Edith Lewis.

The two would stay together, at various Village addresses, for nearly forty years — until she died in 1947.

She wrote My Ántonia and Death Comes for the Archbishop.

As an undergraduate she signed herself 'William' in letters to a woman she admired at the University of Nebraska.

And before she died, she took great care to burn most of her personal letters.

In one that survived, a college note, she laments her 'unnatural' attraction to another woman.

A few streets over was the Bagatelle.

It sat at 86 University Place, one of the few lesbian bars in the city in the 1950s.

Nearby ran the more working-class Sea Colony and the Swing Rendezvous.

The poet Audre Lorde wrote about it.

It turns up in her memoir, Zami: A New Spelling of My Name.

There was a tiny dance floor in back, and the front room 'smelled like plastic and blue glass and beer and lots of good-looking young women.'

She remembered that she and the other out Black lesbians rarely acknowledged one another — that 'the Bag was always The Club.'

Then there was Webster Hall.

It stood at 125 East Eleventh Street.

From 1913 through the 1930s it threw the annual Greenwich Village Ball.

These were masquerade nights for the neighborhood's bohemian crowd, many of them gay men in drag.

By the early 1920s, gay men and lesbians were holding events of their own there.

In 1934 the New York Herald Tribune described the floor:

'Men danced with women in men's clothes.

Women danced with men in women's clothes.

And strange androgynous couples careened about the floor, oblivious to the workings of society and nature.' Today Webster Hall is a popular, mostly straight nightclub.

In 2020 and 2021 the park itself became an illegal — and very raucous — after-hours dance party for Covid-weary young people from every corner of the city.

Clashes between the revelers and noise-weary neighbors finally pushed police to enforce a 10pm curfew.

That curfew has since been lifted.

That's the walk — a patch of the city where the dancing never really stopped.
