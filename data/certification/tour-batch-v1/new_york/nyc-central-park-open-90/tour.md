## 1. Strawberry Fields

Settle in.

You're starting in Manhattan.

When you're ready, head for Strawberry Fields — about a 10-minute walk straight ahead.

Look down at the black-and-white mosaic set into the ground, with the word 'Imagine' at its center.

This is the memorial to John Lennon — a 2.5-acre garden shaped like a teardrop, right across the street from the Dakota, where he lived with Yoko Ono when he was murdered in 1980.

When the city rebuilt this ground, Ono asked for something unusual: a living memorial, not a statue.

She thought there were already enough statues in Central Park — that's how the parks landscape architect, Arne Abramowitz, remembered it.

The land had taken his name back in April 1981.

But at the time it sat marooned on a traffic median, boxed in by a park road and two slip roads, so that August the city announced a full rebuild.

The design came from Bruce Kelly, chief landscape architect for the Central Park Conservancy.

Construction began in April 1984.

It opened on October 9, 1985 — what would have been Lennon's 45th birthday.

Ono led the dedication alongside mayor Ed Koch, and her son Sean stood with them, celebrating his own tenth birthday that same day.

Speaking for the United Nations was Marcela Pérez de Cuéllar, its first lady.

The couple loved this quiet corner of the park, and every year fans still gather on his birthday, October 9, and the night he died, December 8, singing his favorite songs late into the dark.

A bronze plaque nearby lists the 121 countries that donated trees, plants, and stones to bless the site as a garden of peace.

That's Strawberry Fields — a living garden where a statue might have stood.

## 2. American Museum of Natural History

Leaving Strawberry Fields behind, head for American Museum of Natural History, about a 15-minute walk away.

A plaque back there named 121 nations; the halls ahead gather the spectrum of life.

Come in through the Gilder Center, and the lobby stops you — walls of pale concrete curving like a canyon.

That sinuous look, echoing the canyons of the American Southwest, came from spraying structural concrete straight onto rebar.

But here's the thing about this place, founded back in 1869: it has never been only a museum.

It's also the country's preeminent private science lab, and its research and its galleries feed each other — which is why what's on display can actually change.

A few years ago, scientists worked out that dinosaurs didn't drag their tails along the ground.

They held them up, waving in the air, as they walked.

So the curators took the famous skeletons apart, bone by bone, and rebuilt them with tails raised.

Then they set one over a slab of Texas riverbed lined with fossilized footprints — and not one tail-drag mark among them.

It's a peephole into how a scientific theory actually comes to be.

That Gilder Center opened in 2023 at a cost of $431 million, and it holds about a twelfth of the whole collection across 230,000 square feet.

Inside there's an insectarium, a butterfly vivarium, open storage, and a 360-degree Invisible Worlds Theater about the networks that link all life.

Down on the first floor, the Hall of Biodiversity has a real showstopper.

It's called "The Spectrum of Life," a wall of specimens lit up almost psychedelic.

Close by, a rain-forest diorama pipes in video, sound, and even smell, so you feel like you've walked inside one.

The message underneath is plain: Earth's life-forms are varied, tangled together, and worth preserving.

Elsewhere there are gem galleries and an IMAX screen.

The Hall of Ocean Life turns on ecology and conservation, all of it under a 94-foot blue whale hung from the ceiling.

At one entrance stands a 63-foot canoe, carved in the 1800s by the Haida people.

They came from the coast of British Columbia.

Up on the third floor, the Margaret Mead Hall of Pacific Peoples carries the work of the anthropologist it's named for.

She lived from 1901 to 1978 and spent most of that life in the museum's own anthropology department.

The hall roams the myriad cultures of the South Pacific islands, from Polynesia to Micronesia.

Its wood-and-bark masks from Papua New Guinea are among the highlights.

Got kids along? The Discovery Room lets them dig for dinosaur bones, chase a scavenger hunt, and peer through microscopes.

It runs on timed free tickets, and they run out, so grab one early.

That's the walk — the museum where even the dinosaurs got their tails lifted.
