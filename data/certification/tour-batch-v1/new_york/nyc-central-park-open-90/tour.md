## 1. Bethesda Terrace

Settle in.

You're starting in Manhattan, at Bethesda Terrace — the great stone gathering place at the middle of Central Park.

Take a moment. Let it land.

Now look up at the fountain — the winged figure crowning it is the Angel of the Waters.

She's the work of Emma Stebbins, the first woman the city ever handed this kind of commission — and to this day, hers is the only sculpture in the entire park made by a woman.

How did a woman land that job in the 1860s? It didn't hurt that her brother was president of the Central Park Board of Commissioners.

The angel blesses the water with one hand and carries a lily — the old symbol of purity — in the other.

The fountain honors the Croton Aqueduct, which finally ended New York's water troubles in 1842; the structure itself was finished in 1864, and the angel dates to 1873.

So why "Bethesda"? The name comes from a pool in Jerusalem named in the Gospel of John, where the sick would wait for an angel to stir the water, then step in to be healed.

There's a debate about the angel that's never quite been settled — is it a man or a woman? Some read her as an early celebration of women; others point out that angels are sexless anyway, and Stebbins simply followed the design conventions of her time.

Here's the odd part: this terrace was the one place in their whole design where Olmsted and Vaux wanted any statue at all, and they ended up with exactly one.

The Moorish arcade you just walked under is the work of Jacob Wrey Mould, who had studied Islamic architecture and rebuilt its style right here; its ceiling once held thousands of tiles, and the Central Park Conservancy is putting them back now.

Off toward the water, the Loeb Boathouse has been renting out the rowboats bobbing on the lake since it joined the park in 1874, and it's a fine spot to eat.

It even had its Hollywood moment — Carrie and Mr. Big of Sex and the City tumbled into the water off its dock at the end of a disastrous date.

Walk north and you're on the Mall, the grand elm-lined walkway Olmsted and Vaux called the Promenade — their "open air hall of reception," bedecked with statues.

One of them, on your right, is Fitz-Greene Halleck, who died in 1867 and was once one of the most famous poets in the whole country — though almost nobody can name a line of his now.

Which makes the unveiling in 1876 almost unbelievable: President Rutherford B. Hayes came, his entire cabinet came, and ten thousand people crowded in for the ceremony.

That we've forgotten him says plenty about putting people on pedestals — a fashion well past its heyday.

This same Mall saw a different kind of drama on June 22, 1901.

The old benches had just been swapped for shiny green rocking chairs, and the instant you sat down, a uniformed attendant walked up demanding five cents for a chair with arms, three for one without.

An entrepreneur named Oscar Spate had wangled the rocking-chair franchise out of Tammany Hall, dreaming of two hundred and fifty dollars a day across the city's parks.

New Yorkers were having none of it. The angry public rioted, smashing and stealing the chairs.

A chastened Parks Commission yanked Spate's franchise and put out new chairs stamped "For the Exclusive Use of Women and Children, FREE."

These days the Mall still throws a party — near its uptown end sits an underused band shell, and just west of it regulars dance-skate for hours every weekend to blasting disco.

It's quite a scene.

## 2. Strawberry Fields

Leaving Bethesda Terrace behind, head for Strawberry Fields, about a ten-minute walk away.

Think back to that fountain you just left — Bethesda Terrace was the one place Olmsted and Vaux ever wanted a statue, and the angel they got was carved by Emma Stebbins, the first woman ever handed a city commission like it.

When the time came to honour John Lennon in this corner of Central Park, Yoko Ono asked for one thing above all — no statue.

She wanted a living memorial instead, because, as the Parks landscape architect Arne Abramowitz recalled, she felt there were already enough statues in the park.

The land itself had been named Strawberry Fields in his memory back in April 1981.

The name comes from "Strawberry Fields Forever," the song Lennon wrote — and that song, in turn, was named for a children's home called Strawberry Field, which stood near his boyhood home in Liverpool.

Back then the spot was a sorry thing, an isolated median stranded between West Drive and two slip roads off 72nd Street, which is why that August the city announced it would be completely renovated and landscaped.

Bruce Kelly, chief landscape architect for the Central Park Conservancy, drew up the design for these 2.5 acres, and construction began in April 1984.

What Kelly shaped is a tear-shaped garden — a grove of stately elms on a wedge of land that falls away on two sides of the park.

It sits right across Central Park West at West 72nd Street from the Dakota, the building where Lennon lived with Ono and was shot dead in 1980.

The garden opened on October 9, 1985 — what would have been Lennon's 45th birthday — dedicated by Ono and mayor Ed Koch, with her son Sean, who was turning ten that very same day.

Speaking for the United Nations was Marcela Pérez de Cuéllar, the UN first lady.

At its heart, a circular path of inlaid black-and-white stones, laid in the Portuguese pavement style, spells out a single word — Imagine, the title of his most famous song.

A bronze plaque nearby names the 121 countries that sent trees, plants, and stones to bless the place as a garden of peace.

Ono helped pay for all of it, and if you'd like, she narrates an audioguide to the garden herself.

Every October 9th and December 8th — his birth and his death — thousands still gather, many of them staying late into the night singing his favorite songs.

But no one belonged to this place quite like Gary dos Santos.

For nineteen years, this Beatles-devoted performance artist knelt on the mosaic and built designs around and inside it — circles of flowers and objects, often shaped into a peace sign.

The New York Times wrote him up.

He lived on tips from tourists, drawn in by the flowers and by a three-minute monologue he'd deliver about his work and about the life of Lennon and his family.

For almost twenty years he came here daily with his girlfriend of fifteen years, Lisa Page, and their dog, Mary Jane, until the long-time locals all knew him by name.

There's even a documentary about him, The Mayor of Strawberry Fields, directed by Torre Catalano and distributed by Nehst Studios.

In September 2013 he was diagnosed with leukemia, and after about nine weeks in the hospital, he was gone that November.

## 3. Dakota Building

From Strawberry Fields, carry on to the Dakota, about a three-minute walk.

Look up at the turrets and spires — the place looks like a castle, and that was entirely the point.

The idea was to convince wealthy New Yorkers that living in an apartment could feel every bit as luxurious as owning a whole house.

It worked from the day it was completed in 1884, designed by Henry J. Hardenbergh, back when the Upper West Side was so desolate they named it after the remote Dakota Territory.

Nine stories of German Renaissance stonework — reliefs, balconies, gables — the building dragged the whole neighborhood upmarket, and in 1976 it was named a National Historic Landmark.

Strip away the fame, though, and it's really just an upscale apartment building.

Never an ordinary one, mind you — Judy Garland lived here, so did Leonard Bernstein.

And in 1968 it played the sinister Bramford in Rosemary's Baby, home to the Woodhouses, Rosemary and John, played by Mia Farrow and John Cassavetes.

Don't expect to get inside, either — the residents guard their privacy fiercely, and a taciturn doorman holds the entrance around the clock.

That guarded quiet was shattered on December 8, 1980, when John Lennon was shot dead on the street right outside his home, 1 West 72nd Street on Central Park West.

People have flocked to this building ever since.

And to this day, his widow, Yoko Ono, still lives inside.

## 4. American Museum of Natural History

From the Dakota, make your way to the American Museum of Natural History, about a 12-minute walk.

You've just passed the Dakota, where John Lennon was shot dead in the street outside on December 8, 1980, and where Yoko Ono still lives.

Across from it lies the tear-shaped garden Yoko partly funded, with its grove of elms and the Imagine mosaic.

Here's a place that changes its mind in public: a few years ago, scientists here concluded that dinosaurs never dragged their tails through the mud but waved them in the air as they walked.

So the curators took apart the famous skeletons, bone by bone, and rebuilt them with tails erect, then set one skeleton over a slab of Texas riverbed where fossilized footprints show no tail-drag marks at all — a peephole into how a scientific theory takes hold.

Since 1869 this has been the country's leading private scientific research center and its top museum for paleontology, zoology, anthropology, and now astronomy, and that constant traffic between researchers and curators is exactly what keeps it so fiercely alive.

Come in through the newest door.

In 2023 the $431-million Richard Gilder Center for Science, Education and Innovation opened, and its lobby stops you cold — 230,000 square feet of curving walls that mimic Southwestern canyons, a sinuous look made by spraying structural concrete straight onto rebar.

Behind those walls sits 12% of the museum's whole collection, an insectarium, a permanent butterfly vivarium, open storage exhibits, and a 360-degree Invisible Worlds Theater about the hidden networks that underlie all life.

On the first floor, the Hall of Biodiversity pulls you toward "The Spectrum of Life," a psychedelic wall of specimens.

Nearby, an interactive rain-forest diorama layers video, sound, and even smell to drop you inside a rain forest, all of it pressing one point: how much we stand to lose if the variety and interdependence of Earth's life-forms unravels.

Follow the crowds and you'll hit the first floor's favorite, the Milstein Hall of Ocean Life, where a beloved 94-foot blue whale hangs from the ceiling over dioramas on ecology and conservation and models of more than 750 sea creatures, from microscopic algae to glowing jellyfish.

Elsewhere you'll find animal halls, galleries devoted to gems, and an IMAX theater.

And at one entrance, a 63-foot, 19th-century canoe carved by the Haida people of British Columbia greets you.

Up on the third floor, the Margaret Mead Hall of Pacific Peoples carries the life's work of the famous anthropologist who lived from 1901 to 1978 and spent most of that life in this museum's Anthropology Department.

Her hall roams the many cultures of the South Pacific islands, Polynesia and Micronesia among them, and its showpiece is a group of traditional masks carved from wood and bark in Papua New Guinea.

On the museum's north side, don't miss the ultramodern Rose Center for Earth and Space, a giant glass cube wrapped around the Hayden Planetarium.

Inside, a powerful reality simulator flies you through space to witness the Big Bang, and past the Hall of Planet Earth the Cosmic Pathway winds you through 13 billion years of evolution.

Walking with kids? The Discovery Room lets them pretend to dig up dinosaur bones, work through a scavenger hunt, and peer down microscopes — but it runs on timed free tickets, so grab one early in the day before they're gone.

First time here? Consider a guided introductory tour; they start at a quarter past each hour until 4:15pm, led by volunteer guides who train for six months, and no two hit quite the same highlights.

One last tip: this place is a magnet for school groups, so it's crowded by midmorning, and attendance runs opposite the weather — sparse on a lovely day, and when it's blustery or rainy, watch out.

## 5. Beacon Theatre

Leaving the Museum of Natural History behind, make your way toward the Beacon Theatre, about a sixteen-minute walk.

Step inside and one critic swore it was like walking into an Arab sheik's tent, while a reporter called the whole place "a true bit of Bagdad on Broadway."

That fever dream was deliberate — Ahlschlager built the Beacon as a miniature of his earlier Roxy Theatre in Midtown, and where the Roxy leaned Moorish and Renaissance, here he blended Renaissance, Ancient Roman, Ancient Greek, and Rococo into one room.

Look toward the stage.

Doric columns hold up the proscenium arch, and flanking it stand two bronze women thirty feet tall — Greek goddesses that Women's Wear Daily dubbed "heroic-size bronzes of Amazons with spear and shield."

Overhead, the ceiling is painted red, gold, green, and blue and shaped to hang like the draped roof of a tent.

East of the vestibule, a circular rotunda carries the Rococo idea even further, its ceiling rising as high as the auditorium itself, packed with rosette and coffer moldings and a huge chandelier hanging at its center.

Above the doorways there's a landscape mural by Valdemar Kjoldgaard, hidden for years under wallpaper until the 2008 renovation stripped it away and brought the painting back.

Down in the orchestra pit sits the theater's original Wurlitzer organ, built in 1928, with four manuals and nineteen ranks.

It's one of only three Manhattan theaters that still keep their original organ, alongside Radio City Music Hall and the United Palace.

The Beacon nearly lost it — by the early 1960s the organ had been abandoned, and it survived only because ripping it out cost more than the operators wanted to spend, before a 1967 restoration put it back in service until it was sealed in 2009.

Since 2007, more than two thousand performers have been talked into signing their names on the walls of one elevator here — Jerry Seinfeld, Bono, Amy Poehler and Tina Fey, Trevor Noah, Ben Schwartz, Eddie Vedder.

But the act that truly made this room its home was the Allman Brothers Band, the most frequent performer the Beacon ever had.

They first played here in 1989, came back in 1992 and 1994, and then turned up almost every year, so reliably that a 2002 New York Times piece called their shows "as sure a sign of spring as the reappearance of robins and bellybuttons."

In March 2000 they recorded a live album in this house, releasing Peakin' at the Beacon that November, and in 2009 they marked their fortieth anniversary here with shows dedicated to their founder and original frontman, Duane Allman.

A Cirque du Soleil run bumped them in 2010, but they were invited back in 2011 — and on October 28, 2014, they played the final show of their career on this stage, their 238th concert at the Beacon.

None of that was guaranteed, because in February 1986, the year Andy Feltz took over as manager, the owners announced they'd gut the Beacon into a nightclub, restaurant, and discotheque.

At the time it was the only mid-sized live-concert venue in Manhattan, since Avery Fisher Hall and Carnegie Hall, the other rooms its size, were mostly given over to classical music.

The developer, Olivier Coquelin, and his architect Charles A. Platt, a former landmarks commissioner, said they'd picked the Beacon precisely for its landmark status, and promised to build the club as a freestanding structure inside the auditorium so they'd barely touch the protected interior.

The neighborhood didn't buy it — residents feared for the landmarked details, and preservationists banded together as the Committee to Save the Beacon Theatre to stop the whole thing.

## 6. Lincoln Center

From the Beacon Theatre, carry on to Lincoln Center — about an eighteen-minute walk.

Before we arrive, think back to where we began.

At Bethesda Terrace, the fountain was crowned by Emma Stebbins's Angel of the Waters — the only sculpture in the whole park made by a woman.

And a little later, that tear-shaped garden at West 72nd Street, where fans still gather every October 9th and December 8th to remember John Lennon, shot dead in the street outside the Dakota in 1980.

You've stood here before, in a way — on screen.

This is where Natalie Portman slowly loses her mind in the psychological thriller Black Swan, and where two lovestruck Brooklynites, Cher and Nicolas Cage, meet for a date in Moonstruck.

But long before the opera and the ballet, this was San Juan Hill — a rundown, predominantly African American neighborhood of tenements that urban planner Robert Moses gleefully bulldozed in the 1960s to raise this campus.

Its streets got one last close-up first: the Oscar-winning gangland musical West Side Story shot its exteriors right here in 1961, just before the demolition.

The movies never stopped tangling with this city.

In the cult musical Fame, from 1980, students from the High School of Performing Arts dance right through Midtown's streets, doing the city's traffic no favors at all.

The film's content was too raw for the Board of Education, who banned shooting at the real school, then at 120 West 46th Street — so the crew used the doorway of a disused church across the street for the entrance, and Haaren Hall, at Tenth Avenue and 59th, for the interiors.

For all its ambition, the campus opened to a chorus of complaints — critics savaged its conservative design, its fortress-like walls, and its poor acoustics.

So for its 50th anniversary in 2009, a $1.2-billion renovation swept through, led by the architects Diller Scofidio + Renfro and finished across 2009 and 2010 to critical acclaim.

It expanded and revived a string of buildings, including Alice Tully Hall, home of the Chamber Music Society.

Today Lincoln Center gathers eleven arts organizations, among them the Metropolitan Opera, the New York City Ballet, and the New York Philharmonic.

And in 2023, the music world was turned on its ear — the New York Philharmonic had stolen wunderkind conductor Gustavo Dudamel away from the Los Angeles Philharmonic.

It was the classical equivalent of Tom Brady abandoning Boston for Tampa.

His tenure opens with the 2025/26 season in the acoustically excellent, newly refurbished David Geffen Hall — the same podium Leonard Bernstein, one of the 20th century's great maestros, once commanded.

Take a moment for the three classic buildings ringing the central Revson Fountain.

The Metropolitan Opera's lobby glows with brightly saturated murals by the painter Marc Chagall; beside it stand David Geffen Hall and the David H. Koch Theater, the last designed by Philip Johnson.

The Revson Fountain itself is the showstopper after dark, throwing up Las Vegas–style water shows right in the lighted plaza in front of the opera house.

Step inside the Metropolitan Opera House and it's colossal — a 24-carat gold-leaf ceiling overhead and a grand staircase spiraling up through the lobby.

Every great voice has filled this room, from Luciano Pavarotti to Enrico Caruso to Maria Callas.

The repertoire runs from Verdi's Aida to Mozart's Don Giovanni — the classics, alive and well.

But New York's real gift to the stage might be dance — for nearly a century, this city has sat at the center of it.

The American Ballet Theatre, founded in 1939 by Lucia Chase and Richard Pleasant, set out to grow homegrown talent, staging works by Jerome Robbins, Twyla Tharp, and Alvin Ailey.

And the city nurtured the rule-breakers too — Martha Graham, who traded grace for boxy, industrial movement on bare, almost abstract sets, and Merce Cunningham, who cut dance loose from music entirely.

And that brings our walk to a close.

Thank you for coming along with me today. When you're ready, take your time and keep exploring on your own.
