## 1. Pont Neuf

Settle in.

You're starting in the Île de la Cité, right at Pont Neuf — take a moment to take it in.

Look to the middle of the bridge, at Henri IV on his horse — that bronze has been hiding things since 1818.

The first statue here was torn down in 1792 and melted down in the Revolution.

When the Bourbon kings returned, Parisians paid to rebuild it in 1818, by public subscription.

The bronze came from melting down a statue of Desaix, and the new figure was cast from a surviving mould of the old one.

Before they sealed it, the sculptor François-Frédéric Lemot slipped four boxes inside.

One held a life of Henri IV, one a 17th-century parchment certifying the original, one an account of how the new statue was commissioned, and one the names of everyone who had chipped in.

But those weren't the only secrets in there.

The anti-Royalist who cast the replacement smuggled a little statuette of Napoleon into the horse's belly — found only recently, during restoration.

Now the bridge itself.

Pont Neuf means New Bridge, yet it's the oldest bridge still standing in Paris.

Henri IV built it — twelve graceful arches — and for its day the whole thing was radical.

Every other bridge was packed with houses; this one was open stone, the first in Paris built that way.

He was one of the city's first great town-planners, and he'd come to inspect the work, once leaping clean over an unfinished arch to the workmen's delight.

Look along the sides for the semicircular bays — those were market stalls once, worked by teeth-pullers, quack doctors and other hawkers.

Just off the bridge lies the Place Dauphine, and once a year it became an open-air art show.

On the Petite Fête-Dieu — the Small Corpus Christi, eight days after the main feast — young painters hung their canvases there from six in the morning until noon.

It was an unofficial salon, a rare chance for unknowns to get noticed, a forerunner of the Salon des Refusés that later sparked the Impressionists.

In 1720 a twenty-two-year-old, the son of the man who kept the king's billiards, showed an antique bas-relief here.

J.-B. Vanloo stopped, studied it a long while, saw real quality, bought it, and took the young man under his wing.

Eight years on, that painter was Vanloo's colleague at the Academy of Painting — Jean-Baptiste-Siméon Chardin.

Steps beside the statue drop down to the square du Vert Galant.

That's where Jacques de Molay, Grand Master of the Templars, was burnt at the stake, cursing the kings as the flames took him.

That's Pont Neuf — the New Bridge that turned out to be the oldest, still keeping its secrets.

## 2. Notre-Dame Cathedral

Leaving Pont Neuf behind, head for Notre-Dame Cathedral, about an 18-minute walk away.

The Revolution that melted the Pont-Neuf king struck down the stone kings here too.

You're standing on the Parvis, the wide, windswept square in front of the cathedral.

Haussmann cleared it in the 1860s, and its name comes from an old word for paradise.

It was inside these walls, in 1804, that Napoleon set the crown on his own head and made himself emperor.

The church was so run-down by then that its crumbling walls had to be hidden behind drapes just to look grand enough.

It rears up from the Île de la Cité like a great ship moored by huge flying buttresses.

This is the symbolic heart of France.

It went up on far older ground, first a Roman temple to Jupiter.

On top of that came the Merovingian cathedral of St-Etienne, and then the Gothic church you see today.

Work began in 1160 under Bishop de Sully and was finished around 1345.

Its seminaries became an ecclesiastical powerhouse, turning out six popes across the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries.

Later it lost ground to rival sees like Rheims and St-Denis, and the building slid into decline.

The facade is the cathedral's most impressive exterior feature.

You can still read the older Romanesque style in its solid H-shape, but the filigree of the central rose window and the gallery above lifts the whole front into lightness and grace.

That row of 27 huge figures beneath the rose window isn't French royalty at all — they're the biblical kings, the ancestors of Christ.

During the Revolution, enthusiasts mistook them for France's own kings and tore them down, so every statue up there now is a copy.

Over the central doorway sits the most arresting carving of all, the Day of Judgement.

In the lower frieze the dead climb from their graves in a whirl of movement, while Christ presides above, sending the souls on his right to heaven and those on his left to hell.

And around the arch peer small, alert, mischievous angels — said to be modelled on the cathedral's own choirboys.

By the west door, look down for a small bronze star, a brass stud in the pavement marking the city's Zero Point, or kilometre zero, from which every main-road distance in France is measured.

People treat it as a wishing stone now, though its real job was always to fix the road to every other town.

And beneath the square, a crypt holds Gallo-Roman walls, a quay, a hypocaust and medieval cellars — two thousand years of the city stacked in one room.

Those white paving blocks set into the square trace the outlines of the little streets and houses that crowded here in medieval times.

Back at the Pont-Neuf's eastern end, across from the statue of Henri IV, red-brick seventeenth-century houses flank a narrow entrance.

They lead into place Dauphine, one of the city's most secluded and handsome squares.

That's the walk — Notre-Dame, the great stone ship at the heart of France.
