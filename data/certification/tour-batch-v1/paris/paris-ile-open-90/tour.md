## 1. Pont Neuf

Settle in.

You're on the Île de la Cité.

When you're ready, make for Pont Neuf, just ahead.

Look to the middle of the bridge, where Henri IV rides in bronze.

Before that statue was sealed in 1818, the sculptor François-Frédéric Lemot slipped four boxes inside it.

One holds a history of Henri IV's life, one a 17th-century parchment certifying the first statue, one a note on how this new one was ordered, and the last a list of everyone who paid for it.

The first Henri IV had been torn down in 1792, and when the Bourbons returned they recast him from a surviving mould of the original.

The bronze itself was melted down from a statue of Desaix.

Steps beside the statue lead down to the square du Vert Galant, where Jacques de Molay, Grand Master of the Templars, was burnt at the stake — cursing the kings as the flames took him.

Just off the bridge sits the Place Dauphine, and once a year it turned into a salon for the unknown.

On the Petite Fête-Dieu, the 'Small Corpus Christi' eight days after the main feast, young painters hung their canvases here from six in the morning until noon.

It was unofficial, a chance for nobodies to be seen — a forerunner of the Salon des Refusés that would later spark the Impressionists.

In 1720 a twenty-two-year-old, son of the man who kept the king's billiards, showed an antique bas-relief.

J.-B. Vanloo stopped, studied it a long while, found real qualities in it, bought it, and took the young man under his wing.

Eight years on, that painter was Vanloo's colleague at the Academy of Painting.

His name was Jean-Baptiste-Siméon Chardin.

Along the sides of the bridge run three hundred and eighty-one carved stone faces, no two alike.

They're forest and field gods from old myth, satyrs and sylvains, copied from originals attributed to the Renaissance sculptor Germain Pilon.

Pilon also carved the tomb of Henry II and Catherine de'Medici at Saint-Denis.

His originals lasted until the bridge was completely rebuilt between 1851 and 1854.

Six went to the Musée Carnavalet, eight more to the Cluny, and today they all sit at the Musée National de la Renaissance at Château d'Écouen.

The copies here now were cut by Maindron, Lavigne, Barye and Fontenelle — and Fontenelle alone made sixty-one, on the upstream side between the right bank and the Île de la Cité.

That's Pont Neuf — Henri IV in bronze, with four boxes hidden inside.

## 2. Notre-Dame Cathedral

Leaving Pont Neuf behind, head for Notre-Dame Cathedral, about an 18-minute walk away.

The same Revolution that destroyed Henri IV's statue tore the cathedral's kings down too.

You're out on the wide square now, the whole west front of Notre-Dame filling the view.

This is the symbolic heart of France.

Set in the pavement by the west door is a small bronze star called kilometre zero, the point every main road in France measures its distance from.

The square itself is the Parvis, from the old word for paradise.

Haussmann laid it out in the 1860s.

Look down, and white blocks in the stone trace the little medieval streets and houses that once crowded here.

The facade in front of you is the cathedral's most impressive exterior feature.

You can still read the Romanesque in its solid H-shape, yet the whole thing feels light and graceful, thanks to the filigree of the central rose window and the gallery above it.

Beneath that rose window runs a row of twenty-seven huge figures — the biblical kings, ancestors of Christ.

During the Revolution, crowds mistook these Old Testament kings for the kings of France and destroyed them, so the ones you see now are copies.

Over the central doorway, the carving to find is the Day of Judgement.

In the lower frieze the dead climb from their graves, while Christ above sends the saved to his right, to heaven, and the damned to his left, to hell.

All around the arch, small angels peer out, alert and mischievous, said to be modelled on the cathedral's own choirboys.

From the island the whole thing rises like a great ship, moored by huge flying buttresses.

It stands on older layers of worship — first a Merovingian church called St-Etienne.

Under that lay a Roman temple to Jupiter.

Work began in 1160 under Bishop de Sully and dragged on until about 1345.

By then its seminaries had turned out six popes across the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, though it later slipped behind rival sees like Rheims and St-Denis.

In time the building fell into decline — then Napoleon gave it back some prestige, crowning himself emperor here in 1804, on walls so shabby they had to be draped in cloth to look grand enough.

And under the square, a crypt holds Gallo-Roman walls, an old quay, a hypocaust, and medieval cellars — two thousand years of history stacked beneath your feet.

Back at the eastern end of the Pont-Neuf stand red-brick seventeenth-century houses, across from the Henri IV statue.

They frame the entrance to place Dauphine, one of the city's most secluded and attractive squares.

That's the walk — Notre-Dame, the symbolic heart of France.
