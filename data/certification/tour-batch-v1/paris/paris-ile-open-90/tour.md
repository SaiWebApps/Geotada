## 1. Pont Neuf

Settle in.

You're starting in the Île de la Cité.

When you're ready, head for Pont Neuf — it's just ahead.

Picture a king taking a running leap over a half-built arch, and his workmen roaring with delight.

That was Henri IV, one of the city's first great town-planners, who built this bridge and kept coming back to watch it rise.

Its name means New Bridge — and yet it's the oldest bridge still standing in Paris.

So why "new"? Because it broke every rule of its day: twelve arches of solid stone, and not one house crowding the roadway.

Those semicircular bays along the sides once held market stalls — teeth-pullers, quack doctors, and hawkers of every kind.

Look to the middle of the span — that bronze horseman is Henri IV, and he's keeping a secret.

The first statue rose in 1614, and revolutionaries tore it down in 1792.

When the Bourbons returned, Parisians paid to rebuild it in 1818 by public subscription.

The bronze came from melting down a statue of the general Louis Charles Antoine Desaix, and the new figure was cast from a mould that had survived from the original.

Before the sculptor François-Frédéric Lemot sealed it up, he tucked four boxes inside — a life of Henri IV, a 17th-century parchment certifying the first statue, a note on how this one was commissioned, and the names of everyone who'd contributed.

Just off the bridge, at the Place Dauphine, one day a year turned into a lottery for young artists.

On the Petite Fête-Dieu — the "Small Corpus Christi," eight days after the main feast — painters hung their canvases outdoors, but only from six in the morning until noon.

It was an unofficial salon, a rare chance for an unknown to be noticed, and a forerunner of the Salon des Refusés that would later spark the Impressionists.

In 1720, a twenty-two-year-old — the son of the man who kept the king's billiards — set out an antique bas-relief here.

The painter J.-B. Vanloo stopped, studied it a long while, found real quality, bought it, and took the young man under his wing.

Eight years on, that unknown was Vanloo's colleague at the Academy of Painting. His name was Jean-Baptiste-Siméon Chardin.

As you cross, look along the parapet: 381 carved stone faces, and no two alike.

They're woodland gods, satyrs and sylvains out of old mythology, copied from originals by the Renaissance sculptor Germain Pilon — the man who carved the tomb of Henri II and Catherine de' Medici at Saint-Denis.

Pilon's originals lasted until the bridge was rebuilt between 1851 and 1854.

Six went to the Musée Carnavalet and eight to the Cluny, and today they all sit at the Musée National de la Renaissance at the Château d'Écouen.

The new copies came from Maindron, Lavigne, Barye and Fontenelle — Fontenelle alone cut 61, on the upstream side between the right bank and the island.

Now here's something you'll have to imagine, because it happened right where you're standing.

You've likely heard that Louis Daguerre's 1838 view of the Boulevard du Temple is the first photograph to show a human being.

But two years earlier, in 1836 or 1837, Daguerre was testing his new technique outdoors, and one of those tests pointed straight at the Pont Neuf and its statue of Henri IV.

Down in the lower-left, in the statue's shadow, you can make out what looks like a worker, maybe two, lying against the fence.

If the dating holds, that faint shape — not the man on the Boulevard du Temple — may be the first human ever photographed.

And those steps beside the statue lead down to the square du Vert-Galant, where Jacques de Molay, Grand Master of the Templars, was burned at the stake — cursing the kings of France as the flames took him.

## 2. Square du Vert-Galant

Leaving Pont Neuf behind, head for the Square du Vert-Galant, just ahead.

See the king on horseback back on the bridge? That's Henri IV, who loved this city mightily — its wine, its women, its good times — and the leafy triangular square below him carries his name.

Vert-Galant means the green, or lusty, gallant, a nod to a love life so legendary the whole garden became a haunt of lovers.

You reach it by the steps behind his statue, down into the triangular western tip of this boat-shaped island.

This is where Paris began, the settlement of a Celtic tribe called the Parisii that Caesar subdued.

The Romans named it Lutetia, and in 358 the emperor Julian called it Cara Lutetia — "my beloved Lutetia."

Centuries later, as king from 1589 to 1610, Henri set out to make this city, in his own words, "a whole world" and "a wonder of the world."

He mostly delivered — the Pont Neuf you just crossed, the "new bridge" that's actually the oldest in Paris, was his, and so were the Place Dauphine and the Place Royale, the square we now call the Place des Vosges.

His private life was busier still: two wives, at least fifty-six mistresses, and households full of children born out of wedlock.

At first the Parisians adored him for it, but the charm faded as he aged, and his dozens of scattered bastards began to grate — enough that France nearly slid back into civil strife.

Worse, he meant to marry his favourite, Gabrielle d'Estrées, an outrage to the reasons of state — only her sudden death spared the kingdom the scandal.

As Femme Ladoucette wrote to the Queen, in the kingdom of France one keeps the sheep apart from the goats.

And yet, above all the scandal, Henri was a peacemaker.

Raised a strict Protestant, he converted to Catholicism, the faith of the majority, to end the religious wars that by the close of the sixteenth century had left Paris starving and looking like a bomb site.

Then, hating the partisanship, he signed the Edict of Nantes in 1598, writing tolerance for the very faith he'd abandoned into law.

It didn't save him. A fanatical Catholic named Francis Ravaillac stabbed him to death, unpersuaded by any of it.

Even so, alongside Charlemagne, Joan of Arc, and Saint Louis, Henri IV is still counted among France's heroes — a king who called kindness and mercy the first virtues of a prince.

These days the prime spot is the tree-dotted knoll right at the point, where the river boats still dock along the north side.

From here the view is unforgettable — the Hôtel de la Monnaie and the dome of the Institut de France to your left, the Louvre to your right, the Pont-des-Arts straight ahead.

Come back after dark. It's pure enchantment.

## 3. Conciergerie

From Square du Vert-Galant, carry on toward the Conciergerie, about a nine-minute walk.

Just back there, on that green tip of the island, the last Grand Master of the Templars, Jacques de Molay, was burnt at the stake — and as the flames took him, he cursed the kings of France.

As you cross to the island, look across from the Henri IV statue — those red-brick seventeenth-century houses frame the entrance to place Dauphine, one of the city's most secluded and lovely squares.

Outside the Conciergerie, look up at the Tour de l'Horloge, built around 1350 to carry Paris's first public clock.

Now the dark part.

In the fifteenth century, passers-by could still hear the screams of tortured prisoners coming from the Tour Bonbec, the westernmost tower, and its name comes from bon-bec — old French for prate, for chatter — a grim nod to the interrogations on the ground floor, where every kind of torture was devised to wring out confessions and denunciations.

When the tower was restored in 1828, workers found two oubliettes in the basement: hidden dungeons spiked with iron points and opening straight onto the river.

You can guess what became of the prisoners dropped into them.

This is Paris's oldest prison, one of the few surviving pieces of the medieval Palais de Justice.

Inside are vaulted Gothic halls, the grandest being the Salle des Gens d'Armes, which went up between 1301 and 1315.

At the far end, an iron grille once penned off the pailleux — prisoners too poor to bribe a guard for a cell of their own, left to sleep on straw, on paille.

Beyond it runs a corridor where prisoners were let to wander freely, and among the rebuilt rooms is the salle de toilette, where the condemned had their hair cropped and their shirt collars torn off, ready for the guillotine.

During the Revolution the cells here held an extraordinary roll of the doomed.

There was Madame Roland, who'd backed the Revolution but sided with the Girondins at exactly the wrong moment.

And there was Marie-Antoinette, who sat in a dreary cell from the 2nd of August to the 16th of October 1793, before they took her to the guillotine on the Place de la Révolution — the Place de la Concorde today.

Then, one after another, came Danton, Robespierre, and Saint-Just, with Danton and Robespierre in the cells right next to the Queen's.

People come now for the history, but the Gothic halls and the great kitchen fireplaces are worth it for the stonework alone.

Up on the top floor there's a mock-up of Marie-Antoinette's cell, her crucifix hanging forlorn against peeling fleur-de-lys wallpaper.

North of the halls ran the stretch leading to the dark cells, called the rue de Paris, after the Great Executioner himself, Monsieur de Paris.

That title stayed in one family, the Sansons, for seven generations, passed father to son from 1688 to 1847.

Just before the Revolution, Louis XVI had set out to better the executioners' lot, granting Charles-Henri Sanson a yearly fee of 16,000 pounds plus 2,000 crowns to keep up the scaffold and pillory — never dreaming the same man would one day be his own executioner.

Charles-Henri was a royalist and had no stomach for the deed.

On the 15th of August 1792 he was arrested as "the despot's employee," and for each execution he was marched from his cell to the Place du Carrousel, where the guillotine then stood, then marched back.

At first the killings were few, but once the blade was working at full swing and his expert hand couldn't be spared, they let him go.

He retired soon after he'd executed his own sovereign.

His son Henri carried the post through the Terror and every shaky regime that followed, right up to 1840, deep into the reign of Louis-Philippe.

And here's the strange thread that ties all of this to living memory: the film-maker Jean Renoir marvelled that his own grandfather, the painter Renoir's father, a tailor from Limoges, had known Henri Sanson's chief assistant during the Terror.

That old assistant, Renoir wrote, "may have passed Voltaire and Benjamin Franklin in the street, heard Mozart play the harpsichord, and attended performances of Monsieur Beaumarchais."

Old Renoir met him in 1845 — the year the family moved up to Paris from Limoges, and the last Sanson still held the post.

The two became friends, the tailor and the executioner's assistant, united by the plain pleasure of doing a good job.

"After all," Jean Renoir observed, "they were two good workmen: one cut out cloth, the other cut off heads just as conscientiously."

## 4. Sainte-Chapelle

From the Conciergerie, make your way on to Sainte-Chapelle, about a four-minute walk away.

Picture a king walking barefoot through the streets, dressed as a penitent, cradling a bundle of relics in his own arms.

For the last stage of the journey Louis carried them himself, and that very scene glows today in the Relics of the Passion window on the chapel's south side.

The relics had reached Paris in August 1239, brought up from Venice by two Dominican friars, and the king marked their arrival with a week-long celebration.

What was worth all that? A piece of the Holy Cross and the Crown of Thorns, which Louis bought from Baudouin II, the last emperor of Constantinople, desperate for funds to defend his city.

The price tells you everything about his priorities: 135,000 livres for the relics, another 100,000 for the silver Grand-Chasse to hold them, and just 40,000 to build and glaze the chapel around them.

The architects — Pierre de Montreuil, or maybe Jean de Chelles — were handed an unusual brief: make a chapel that looked as light and lacy as a reliquary itself.

It took barely five years.

And the result went stunningly beyond anything expected.

There are actually two chapels stacked here — the plain lower one for the servants, while the court climbed a spiral staircase to the upper chapel.

Up top, the walls are almost pure stained glass, held aloft by powerful supports the medieval builders cleverly disguised as clusters of pencil-thin columns so the stone seems to vanish.

When the sun pours through, the glowing blues and reds scatter across the interior like a swarm of brilliant butterflies.

Count them and you'd reach 1,113 panels, two-thirds of them original and the rest from a nineteenth-century restoration.

Together they run nearly the whole Bible — Genesis on the north wall, the Passion of Christ at the east end, the story of these very relics on the south, and the Apocalypse blazing in the rose window.

The men who made all this between 1242 and 1248? Their names are gone.

But the art historian Louis Grodecki teased out three separate workshops just from their styles.

The first shaped the apse and most of the north wall, with supple forms and simplified features.

The second, the Master of the Ezekiel Window, gave the Ezekiel, Daniel and Kings windows their elongated figures and angular drapery.

And the third, the Master of Judith and Esther, added the window of Job, with subtler faces that feel lifted straight from an illuminated manuscript.

Then came the Revolution.

The silver-and-gold reliquary that once held the Crown of Thorns was sent to the Mint, yet its contents — genuine or not — miraculously survived and rest today at Notre-Dame.

Even the standard-bearers of the Revolutionary ideal, it seems, didn't dare to tamper with the holiest of Christian symbols.

The chapel got no such mercy.

It became a flour store, then a depot for court archives, so run-down that by 1847 it still carried a sign reading 'National property, for sale.'

Enlightened voices finally called in Viollet-le-Duc, Lassus and others to rescue it, and 720 of the 1,134 original stained-glass scenes were preserved.

These days the chapel fills with classical concerts, and a ticket bought a day or so ahead spares you the long queue.

## 5. Square Jean XXIII

Leaving Sainte-Chapelle behind, head for Square Jean XXIII, about a 13-minute walk away.

Think back to the chapel you just left.

That building of glass cost only 40,000 livres to raise, while the relics it was built to shelter cost 135,000, and the great reliquary to hold them another 100,000.

In August 1239 two Dominican friars carried those relics from Venice, and for the final stretch Louis himself carried them, barefoot and dressed as a penitent.

This little garden takes its name from a pope who fell so hard for Paris that leaving broke his heart.

He was Angelo Roncalli, born in Lombardy in 1881 and dead in 1963, but before he became the "good pope" and the father of the Second Vatican Council of 1962 to 1965, he was the papal nuncio here, from 1944 to 1953.

Parisians still remember his long walks through the city, trailing friends of every stripe — Protestants, Catholics, Jews, agnostics, atheists.

"Jesus came to break down the barriers," he preached.

"The central point of his teaching is love."

He read French literature and knew the postwar intellectuals and theologians, the very people he would later invite to help reshape the Church at Vatican II.

At diplomatic receptions he happily took the champagne and the conviviality of men and women alike.

A savvy diplomat, he won the trust of the French government, and when President Auriol awarded him the Legion of Honor, he praised Roncalli for respecting "the traditions of justice and tolerance," the honor of the French nation.

So when Rome recalled him, it stung.

"I love France and I love Paris," he told a friend, "and I hoped to stay a little longer. I can't really see myself in Rome."

This inviting square beneath the flying buttresses is his memorial.

But the ground was holy long before him.

Until 1831, when a riot destroyed it, the palace of the archbishop of Paris stood right here, between the cathedral and the Seine.

The site's religious pull runs back to the fourth century, when Emperor Julian presided over a temple to Jupiter, and those Roman ruins became the foundation of the basilica of Saint-Etienne in 528 and then the first Notre-Dame.

The cathedral you see now was begun in 1163, and after centuries of renovation and the fanatical rampages of the Terror, it stands as one of the crown jewels of Gothic architecture across all of Europe.

Beneath the parvis out front, a crypt still holds Gallo-Roman walls, a quay, a hypocaust, and medieval cellars — two thousand years of history stacked underground.

And close by stands the Hotel-Dieu, the oldest hospital in Paris, founded around 651.

If the crowds inside the cathedral get to be too much, you're in good company wanting quiet.

Henry James's Lambert Strether, the protagonist of The Ambassadors, slipped alone into "the great dim church" and felt its "beneficent action on his nerves," a place of refuge with "a sense of safety, of simplification."

Out here the mood is often gentler than the interior, where you'll almost certainly get stuck behind a crush of tourists aiming cameras at the stained glass.

In April the cherry trees bloom along the south porches, screening but never quite hiding the original medallions carved with scenes of student life.

Beds of daffodils and tulips run the length of the cathedral's southern flank to the eastern end, where the flying buttresses rise over a small square generous with flowers, benches, and travelers taking their ease.

## 6. Notre-Dame Cathedral

From Square Jean XXIII, carry on toward Notre-Dame — about seven minutes on foot.

On 16 November 1717, a newborn was found on the steps of the church of Saint-Jean-le-Rond, which once stood near here.

He'd grow up to be Jean le Rond d'Alembert, mathematician and philosopher.

Ahead of you now is Notre-Dame, the heart of Paris and the symbolic heart of France.

Look down near the west door and you'll find a small bronze star set in the pavement, called kilometre zero — the point from which every main-road distance in France is measured.

The wide, windswept square you're crossing is the Parvis — the word comes from paradise — laid out by Haussmann in the 1860s, and the white paving blocks set into it trace the outlines of the cramped medieval streets and houses that once crowded this spot.

Now the facade.

It's the cathedral's most impressive face — the Romanesque still shows in that solid H-shape, but what you feel is lightness and grace, thanks to the filigree of the central rose window and the gallery above it.

Beneath that rose window runs a row of 27 huge figures, the biblical kings who were Christ's ancestors.

Every one is a copy — during the Revolution a mob mistook them for French kings and tore the originals down.

Above the central door, the carving to catch is the Day of Judgement, where in the lower frieze the dead heave themselves out of their graves while Christ presides above, sending the souls on his right to heaven and those on his left to hell.

Look at the arch — angels peer out all around it, alert and mischievous, said to be modelled on the cathedral's choirboys of the day.

The side doors tell gentler stories: the left portal shows Mary crowned by Christ with her life below, while the right shows the Virgin enthroned, and beneath her, scenes from the life of her mother St Anne and from the life of Christ.

The carvers packed them with visual devices and symbols meant to say more than the bare story — in the nativity, the infant Christ lies above Mary to mark his higher status, and rests on an altar rather than a crib, a sign of the sacrifice to come.

Step through the door and you're standing right under the bell tower.

Look up at the vault and you'll spot the trapdoor the bells were once hoisted through, and the two pillars carrying all that weight are massive.

Inside, the contrast hits you at once — the nave sits in shadow while light pours onto the first great clustered pillars of the choir.

That light comes from the transept end walls, nearly two-thirds glass, holding two magnificent rose windows that glow imperial purple; the vaulting and the shafts soaring to the springs of the vaults are pure Gothic, while the stout round pillars and the four-square heft are the older Romanesque still holding on.

At the rear, to the right, is the Treasury, holding what's purported to be the Crown of Thorns along with ornate nineteenth-century monstrances and chalices, which may or may not be your thing.

And right at the back, in a side chapel, a set of models shows how the medieval builders actually put this place up.

If you climb the towers — the entrance is round the side — you'll come face to face with Viollet-le-Duc's gargoyles and angels, and with the great thirteen-ton Emmanuel Bell in the south tower.

One of the largest and oldest bells in Europe, Emmanuel was forged in 1685 and spared during the Revolution of 1789 — unlike Marie, the north tower's bell, which was destroyed and melted down.

It only rings for Christmas, for great occasions, and in times of mourning, like the day after 9/11.

The other nine bells had drifted badly out of tune, so they were all replaced in 2013.

## 7. Shakespeare and Company

From Notre-Dame, make your way over to Shakespeare and Company, about a seven-minute walk.

In 1922, no publisher on earth would touch James Joyce's Ulysses — so Sylvia Beach printed it herself.

She'd opened her own shop, the first Shakespeare and Company, over on rue de l'Odeon three years earlier in 1919.

The one in front of you now is its descendant, at 37, rue de la Bucherie — scruffy, English-language, packed floor to ceiling with new and used books.

Turn right as you step in for a whole wall of books about Paris.

And if you can, come back on June the sixteenth — Bloomsday — when there are readings and wine-tasting upstairs.

George Whitman opened this reincarnation in 1951, and he still let writers sleep among the shelves in exchange for a little work.

He called them Tumbleweeds.

## 8. The Sorbonne

Leaving Shakespeare and Company behind, we head for the Sorbonne, about a fifteen-minute walk away.

As you walk this last stretch, think back over where we started — Pont Neuf, the bridge whose name means New Bridge though it's really the oldest in Paris, the first built here of stone and free of houses.

And the tip of the island, where Jacques de Molay, Grand Master of the Templars, was burnt at the stake and cursed the kings as the flames took him.

Now picture a teacher perched on wooden trestles, and his students sitting on bundles of straw they'd hauled in themselves.

That's how the Latin Quarter became, in the thirteenth century, the centre of learning for all of Western Europe.

The first official mention of the university — the Universitas Magistrorum et Scolarium Parisiensium — dates from 1221, and back then it had no home at all.

Lessons happened in the open air, on Place Maubert and mainly on nearby rue de Fouarre — 'fouarre' being old French for that straw, which is exactly how the street got its name.

The brawls and political agitation got so bad that in 1358 the Regent and future Charles V had the street barred with chains at both ends.

And yet the teachers here were giants — Albert the Great, Thomas Aquinas, and Brunetto Latini, the master of Dante.

Dante himself came to study on rue de Fouarre in the early fourteenth century.

In 1253, a college opened here, founded by Robert, chaplain and confessor to Saint Louis.

Born to a humble family in the village of Sorbon in the Ardennes, Robert became such a scholar that the King had him dine at his own table.

His college became the seat of the faculty of theology, and it took the name of his village — La Sorbonne.

It expanded so spectacularly that it soon overshadowed everything around it, and to this day many foreigners mistake it for the entire University of Paris.

Then the Hundred Years' War broke out, and the university — which had slowly lost its old privileges — sided with the English against the King of France, and later against Jeanne d'Arc herself.

Paris lay in ruins, wolves roamed the streets, and the Montagne Sainte-Geneviève had become, as they said, an 'annexe of Oxford.'

Peace came in the end, and in 1437 the university submitted to the secular authority of Charles VII.

Richelieu, who'd been a student here and rose to become its dean, took on the renovation.

He laid the cornerstone of the chapel in 1635, designed by the architect Jacques Lemercier.

But his project was purely architectural — the curriculum stayed locked to theology right up until the Revolution.

That beautiful compound, the work of Lemercier and Le Brun, was handled roughly by the anti-clerical Revolutionaries.

It was rebuilt and expanded in the nineteenth century, with new faculties of science and letters, inaugurated in 1901 beneath murals by Puvis de Chavannes.

Just over there, Place Maubert — 'la Maube' — had been the haunt of rowdy students and ruffians since the Middle Ages, a place of crime, vice, and public executions.

Erasmus called it 'the cesspit of la Maube,' and by the early twentieth century it was prowled by the terrifying 'Apaches' and their fellow hoodlums.

In Down and Out in Paris and London, published in 1933, George Orwell described the neighbouring rue du Pot-de-Fer, where he lived at number 6, as 'a ravine of tall, leprous houses, lurching towards one another in queer attitudes, as though they had all been frozen in the act of collapse.'

And among the destitute Arabs, Poles, and Italians, the respectable French shopkeepers quietly piled up small fortunes.

For all these layers, the ground beneath you is older still — the fifth arrondissement holds the longest history of any district in mainland Paris, going back to the Roman occupation of Gaul.

When the crowded Île de la Cité spilled onto the left bank, Roman villas rose on the slopes of Mount Leucotitius — the hill we now call the Montagne Sainte-Geneviève.

A north-south road, the cardo, ran along what's now rue Saint-Jacques, with a second cardo tracing today's Boulevard Saint-Michel.

By the end of the second century this was a neighbourhood of real luxury — the marble and polychrome mosaics dug up on rue de la Harpe prove it.

The Romans built three bath complexes here: the northern set at Cluny, the eastern at what's now the Collège de France, and the southern near rue Gay-Lussac.

And to feed them all with water, an aqueduct ran the whole way from the Rungis basin, near where Orly airport stands today.

And that brings our walk to a close.

Thank you for coming along with me today — when you're ready, take your time and keep exploring on your own.
