## 1. Place de la Bastille

It's July 1789, and revolution is stirring in the streets of Paris.

The king's troops had abandoned the city center and fallen back on their one remaining stronghold in Paris — a castle-like fortress guarding the eastern edge of town, the Bastille.

That fortress was a massive medieval castle, raised to defend the Paris of Charles V — thirteen years in the building, finished in 1382 — and the faubourg around it, the district that once lay outside the old city, still keeps a flavour of the past in its narrow streets.

By 1789 it doubled as a prison, and it had become the very symbol of royal oppression.

But here's what the crowd got wrong: it was far from the grim dungeon everyone pictured.

It ran more like a luxury prison for the well-connected.

Voltaire finished his tragedy Oedipe inside these walls, composing verses and dining well, and the notorious Marquis de Sade — held here that very year — draped his cell in fabric and had his own furniture moved in.

On the fourteenth of July, the people of Paris massed at the main gate and demanded the troops surrender.

Negotiations failed, so they stormed it.

Inside, the liberators found seven prisoners — seven — a far cry from the hundreds they'd imagined setting free.

For good measure, they mounted the heads of a few officials on their pikes.

This triumph of citizens over their king lit a fire across all of France and set the Revolution going.

A fortress that had taken years to raise came down in just four months, pulled apart brick by brick.

And now all that's left is that faint outline in the pavement of Rue St. Antoine — the round turrets of the Bastille traced in cobblestones, about thirty yards before you reach the square.

Now lift your eyes to the tall column at the center of the square, topped by a gilded Liberty carrying the torch of freedom into the future — a monument to France's long struggle to build a democracy.

It doesn't mark 1789 at all, but a second uprising in 1830, when the conservative Charles X — who'd apparently forgotten the Revolution his own generation lived through — had to be tossed out.

Through the unrest of the mid-1800s this square kept drawing worker rallies, and there were times the working class barricaded the streets of Paris — the very world Victor Hugo put on the page in Les Misérables.

It still draws demonstrations today, and every fourteenth of July the whole country remembers the day the Bastille fell — Bastille Day, France's national holiday.

## 2. Hotel de Sully

Leaving Place de la Bastille behind, head for the Hôtel de Sully, about a nine-minute walk away.

Before the Marais swallows you up, look down — that cobblestone outline in the pavement of Rue St. Antoine traces the turrets of the Bastille, the medieval fortress Charles V raised to defend Paris.

It took thirteen years to finish in 1382, and just four months to tear down in 1789.

When the crowd stormed it on July 14th, they freed only seven prisoners — one of them the Marquis de Sade.

Now turn your back on the square and head west down Rue St. Antoine, about four blocks into the Marais.

Turn right into rue de la Bastille, past Restaurant Bofinger at No. 3.

Marais means swamp — and that's exactly what this was, marshland, until Henry IV drained his court's ambitions into it.

During his reign, from 1589 to 1610, this became the hometown of the French aristocracy, where big shots built their private mansions — hôtels, they called them — close to Henry's stylish Place des Vosges.

The ones that survived the Revolution are museums, libraries, and national institutions now.

Hôtel de Sully went up in 1624 for Mesme Gallet, a gambler so notorious he lost his whole fortune in a single night's play and had to sell up.

The aged Duc de Sully — Henri IV's finance minister — bought it next.

But it was his young wife who made the place famous — her infidelities were legendary, so the pragmatic duke had a separate staircase installed, and her lovers could come and go without ever crossing his path.

Step into the courtyard, where nobles once rolled in by horse-drawn carriage and parked under the four arches to your right.

This elegant space kept the mansion apart from the noisy, very public street.

Look up: there's Autumn hauling grapes from the harvest, Winter as a feeble old man, and the four elements.

Just near here, in the early 1700s, a young writer named Voltaire got a beating from the lackeys of the Prince de Rohan.

Voltaire challenged the prince to a duel, but Rohan's powerful family had him thrown into the Bastille instead.

When he got out, he left for England — an exile that shaped his whole political philosophy and gave us his influential Letters on the English.

The main entrance is at 2, rue Saint-Antoine, opening onto a garden with benches and orange trees where you can't hear the traffic at all.

In the southwest corner there's a fine bookstore, the Librairie des Monuments Historiques, stuffed with books about Paris.

Head for the back courtyard, and noisy Paris takes a back seat.

Enjoy the oak tree, the manicured hedges, the vine-covered walls, and the warm stone and statues on the mansion's back side.

Those French doors at the far end belong to a former orangerie, a greenhouse that kept fruit and vegetables growing all winter — these days it just warms office workers.

Through an archway in the northeast corner is another entrance to Place des Vosges.

For all their show, mansions like this were often just second homes, the owners' real estates being far grander châteaux out in the countryside.

## 3. Musee Carnavalet

From Hôtel de Sully, carry on toward Musée Carnavalet — it's about a six-minute walk.

Three centuries ago, one of the greatest letter writers in French literature sat out in this very garden with pen and paper, utterly delighted by the place.

That was Madame de Sévigné, born 1626, died 1696, and she spent the last twenty years of her life in this hôtel, writing many of her letters outdoors, in the shadow of her home.

'It is an admirable affair,' she wrote, 'we shall all stay here and enjoy the fine air.'

'Since it is impossible to have everything, we shall have to dispense with parquet floors and fashionable little stoves; but at least we have a fine courtyard, a beautiful garden, and nice little blue girls who are most convenient.'

The 'blue girls' were nuns in blue habits, from a convent on what's now rue Sévigné — then rue Sainte-Catherine — where the Lycée Victor-Hugo stands today.

This was the Marais of the seventeenth century, when the nearby church of Saint-Paul-Saint-Louis was the stage for some of the greatest preaching and music of the age.

Step into the courtyard of Carnavalet and you're surrounded by one of the most formidable historic buildings in the whole Marais.

It went up in 1546 under the spell of the Italian Renaissance, two wings set at right angles, looking out over a strict, symmetrical little French garden.

On the main façade opposite the entrance, four stone reliefs — The Seasons — are the work of Jean Goujon, the Renaissance sculptor who lived from 1510 to 1564 and whom Bernini himself admired.

Goujon also carved the decorations on the southwest side of the Louvre's square courtyard, the Cour Carré, and on the façade of its ground-floor reception room.

Inside, the museum and the adjoining Hôtel le Peletier de Fargeau lay out the whole history of Paris.

Up on the first floor, room 21 recreates Sévigné's own apartments — her writing desk, and portraits of her and her literary friends.

After her death, though, her prim descendants sugarcoated some of her letters before publishing them.

The same thing happened in England, where the prissy daughter of Lady Mary Wortley Montagu 'edited' her mother's brilliant Embassy Letters of 1763 — a correspondence Voltaire rated even higher than Sévigné's.

Carnavalet is one example of a whole new kind of home that took over the Marais from the Renaissance on.

Wealthy families bought up the land, walled each plot, and cut an entrance wide enough for a horse and carriage.

Behind it lay a courtyard with room to turn the carriage, then the house, and behind that the garden.

The whole point was to live tucked between courtyard and garden, sealed off from the filth, noise and bustle of the Paris streets.

They called them hôtels particuliers — private town houses — and many, like this one, survive as museums.

## 4. Rue de Rivoli

From Musee Carnavalet, make your way on to Rue de Rivoli, about a two-minute walk away.

Right here, on 21 September 1792, Danton stood and proclaimed the First Republic — and on this same ground, soon after, Louis XVI was brought to trial and sentenced to death.

So how did a king's fate come to be decided on a street corner?

Because back in 1720, none of this looked like a street at all — rue de Rivoli didn't even exist yet, and on the edge of the Tuileries Gardens stood a manège, a riding school, built for the young Louis XV.

Once he left for Versailles, the place kept teaching well-bred youths to ride, fence, dance, and do their mathematics, everything the gentlemanly ideal demanded.

Then the Revolution moved in, and the hall that had trained aristocrats became the seat of the National Assembly, and after it the Convention.

Look now toward no. 258, a house crying out for repair, once the home of the great statesman Talleyrand — the one man who wormed his way through every shaky regime of the age.

His mansion was said to be the most sumptuous palatial residence in the capital, looking out over the Tuileries.

And where so many around him lost their heads, Talleyrand died here of old age on 17 May 1838, aged eighty-six.

## 5. Place des Vosges

Leaving Rue de Rivoli behind, head for Place des Vosges, about an eleven-minute walk away.

Think back over the walk you've just made.

You began at the Bastille, a medieval castle that took thirteen years to build, was finished in 1382, and then came down in just four months in 1789 — its fall freeing seven prisoners on the fourteenth of July.

From there you crossed the Marais, whose name simply means swamp, into the district that Henri IV's reign turned into the hometown of the French aristocracy.

Almost opposite the Church of Saint-Louis–Saint-Paul, this small neighborhood square faces rue d'Ormesson at the end of rue Caron.

Find a bench in the garden near the children's play area, watch them play à la française, and listen to the Hebrew, Yiddish, and Arabic drifting past — because this modest neighborhood square was, for nearly two centuries, the single most fashionable one in Paris.

If it's cold, duck into Café Ma Bourgogne on the northwest corner; the view through the window is nearly as good.

The central pavilion on the south side, at no. 1, is called the Pavillon du Roi, the King's Pavilion — and yet no king ever lived in it.

It was too small for the king and his retinue, so the very first resident was his concierge, the gatekeeper.

Since 1666 it's been rented out to a run of tenants, and today it's carved into apartments reached by ugly stairs and an elevator crammed into the right-hand side of the central arcade — an eighteenth-century intrusion that still rankles, because it blocks part of the view down the rue de Birague.

The writer Germain Brice was complaining about it as early as 1752, arguing the pavilion's three arcades should be torn down so the Louis XIII statue could be seen all the way from the rue Saint-Antoine: 'It has to hurt,' he wrote, 'to see one of the three arcades blocked to make a nasty stairway… we have such little care for public embellishments in Paris.'

Look up anyway — the balcony here is stone rather than wrought iron, and a bust of Henri IV watches the square from above.

Walk through the arcade to the rue de Birague side and you'll find the king's arms and initials above the arches, still there from the original construction.

At no. 3, press the button marked porte and step into the courtyard behind — the first in the square to be restored, and the only one with brick walls inside.

Except the brick isn't brick.

Look closely and it's painted, trompe l'oeil, right down to the pipes in the corners — pink where they cross the red 'brick,' ivory where they run against the stone trim.

The building rose in 1613 on the old stables of the Hôtel des Tournelles, two huge bronze urns still stand against the back wall, and the ground-floor shop now belongs to Issey Miyake.

Just off the northeast corner, at 36 rue des Tournelles, the richest and most glamorous people in Paris kept coming back to one salon — and it belonged to Ninon de l'Enclos.

Born in 1620, she had, like every Frenchwoman of the seventeenth century, exactly four paths open to her: wife, nun, prostitute, or governess.

The convent lasted about a year before that vocation fell to the ground, and she went home to host what became a fabled literary salon.

She refused to marry and lived as a celebrated courtesan, entertaining a stream of lovers — famous nobles right alongside the very priests and bishops who denounced her from the Sunday pulpit, offended less by the courtesan than by the woman and author who openly scorned organized religion.

Molière said she had 'the keenest sense of the absurd of anyone I know,' and his Les Précieuses Ridicules skewered exactly the idle chatter of the salons and their fawning salonnards.

It was her influence, more than her transgressive sexual mores, that frightened the pious Queen Anne of Austria, mother of Louis XIV, who had Ninon locked up in a convent for a few months.

'Feminine virtue,' Ninon observed, 'is nothing but a convenient masculine invention.'

Her guests hung on lines like that — the memoirist Saint-Simon among them, quietly taking notes.

And when she died, she left money to a nine-year-old boy she'd once met, so he could buy books; his name was Voltaire.
