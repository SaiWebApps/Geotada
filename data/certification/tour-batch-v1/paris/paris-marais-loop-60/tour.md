## 1. Place des Vosges

Settle in.

Find a bench near the children's play area, watch them play à la française, and listen — the Hebrew, Yiddish and Arabic around you sit oddly with the fact that this was, for nearly two centuries, the single most fashionable square in Paris.

If it's cold, there's Café Ma Bourgogne at the northwest corner, where the view through the window is nearly as good.

Just off the northeast corner, at 36 rue des Tournelles, one woman ran the salon the richest and sexiest of Paris couldn't stay away from.

Her name was Ninon de l'Enclos, born in 1620, and like every Frenchwoman of her century she had exactly four paths open to her: wife, nun, prostitute, or governess.

She tried the convent, lasted a year, and came home to host what became a fabled literary salon.

She was determined not to marry, and lived instead as a famous courtesan, taking many lovers — nobles, and even the priests and bishops who denounced her from the Sunday pulpit.

What galled the Church wasn't the courtesan so much as the author who openly scorned organized religion.

Molière adored her; he wrote that 'she has the keenest sense of the absurd of anyone I know.'

His play Les Précieuses Ridicules — The Pretentious Young Ladies — mocked the idle chatter of the salons and the sycophants who filled them.

The pious Queen Anne of Austria had Ninon locked in a convent for a few months — more for her influence than for her scandalous love life.

'Feminine virtue,' she said, 'is nothing but a convenient masculine invention.'

Her guests hung on lines like that; the memoirist Saint-Simon sat among them, taking notes.

And when she died in 1705, she left money to a boy she'd once met at the age of nine — Voltaire — to buy books.

That's Place des Vosges — the most fashionable square in Paris, and the corner where Ninon kept her salon.
