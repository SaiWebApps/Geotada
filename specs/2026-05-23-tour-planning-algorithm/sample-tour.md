# Sample Tour — Concorde to Notre-Dame

> Hand-authored worked example. Use this as the canonical reference for what the algorithm's output should *feel* like in practice. The Layer A portion (the 12-stop walking structure) shows what a good route looks like; the Layer B portion (sample narrative content at Pont Neuf and Place Dauphine) shows how the same stop produces different audio for different Profiles.
>
> **MVP scope reminder:** MVP runs the algorithm for one Profile per Trip. The three personas shown at Pont Neuf and Place Dauphine illustrate how Layer B differentiates audio across Profiles — this demonstrates the architecture (and validates that we baked the capability in) but is **not the MVP deliverable**. MVP delivers a single one of these per Trip. Multi-Profile family-stay-together is post-MVP work.
>
> All content authored by the founder. Preserved verbatim.

## Part A — The shared route (Layer A output)

### Inputs assumed
- **Start:** hotel near Place de la Concorde
- **Named destinations:** Louvre, Notre-Dame
- **Time budget:** ~3 hours (half-day)

### What the route looks like

This walk starts at **Place de la Concorde**, moves through the **Tuileries**, passes through the **Louvre**, follows the **Seine** to **Pont Neuf**, crosses onto **Île de la Cité**, passes **Place Dauphine**, the **Conciergerie**, and **Sainte-Chapelle**, and ends at **Notre-Dame**.

Without interior visits, plan for about **2 to 2.5 hours** at a relaxed pace. If you go inside Sainte-Chapelle, the Conciergerie, or Notre-Dame, it can become a half-day walk.

---

### 1. Place de la Concorde — anchor

Start at **Place de la Concorde**, one of the grandest squares in Paris. It sits between the **Champs-Élysées** and the **Tuileries Garden**, giving you one of the best big-picture views of the city.

**What to look for**
- **Luxor Obelisk** — the tall Egyptian obelisk in the center of the square is the main landmark. It gives the square a dramatic focal point.
- **The fountains** — the two monumental fountains on either side of the obelisk. They make the square feel formal and ceremonial.
- **The historic axis** — stand facing east toward the Tuileries and Louvre, then turn west toward the Champs-Élysées and Arc de Triomphe. This is one of Paris's great planned sightlines.
- **The revolutionary history** — this beautiful square also has a darker past. During the French Revolution, it was one of the major execution sites, including for Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette.

---

### 2. Tuileries Garden — anchor

From Place de la Concorde, walk east into the **Tuileries Garden**.

This is a good place to slow down. The garden connects the grandeur of Place de la Concorde with the Louvre, but it also gives you a softer, more relaxed stretch of the walk.

**What to look for**
- **Grand Bassin Rond** — the large circular pond near the Concorde side is a good first pause. Sit in one of the green chairs for a few minutes if you are not rushing.
- **Formal garden layout** — notice the straight paths, clipped trees, open sightlines, and sculptures. It is designed, but still feels lived in.
- **Sculptures along the paths** — the garden is full of statues. Do not just walk the center line. Drift slightly left or right and you will notice more.
- **Musée de l'Orangerie** — on the southwest side of the garden. This is where Monet's Water Lilies are displayed.
- **Jeu de Paume** — on the northwest side. Historically important and now associated with photography and contemporary image-based art.

---

### 3. Arc de Triomphe du Carrousel — supporting

At the eastern end of the Tuileries, you will reach the **Arc de Triomphe du Carrousel**.

This is the smaller triumphal arch near the Louvre, not the larger Arc de Triomphe at the end of the Champs-Élysées.

**What to look for**
- **The Napoleonic arch** — built under Napoleon and once marked the entrance to the Tuileries Palace, which no longer exists.
- **The view back west** — turn around and look back through the Tuileries toward Place de la Concorde and the Champs-Élysées. One of the best places to feel the scale and alignment of central Paris.
- **The Louvre ahead** — turn back east and the Louvre opens up in front of you. This is where the walk shifts from garden to palace.

---

### 4. Louvre Courtyards — anchor (destination)

Walk into the Louvre complex and toward the **glass pyramid**.

For this walk, treat the Louvre as architecture and atmosphere rather than a museum visit. The building itself is the experience here.

**What to look for**
- **The Louvre Pyramid** — now one of the most recognizable modern landmarks in Paris. It also acts as the main entrance to the museum.
- **The palace façades** — look around at the scale of the buildings surrounding the courtyard. Before it was a museum, the Louvre was a royal palace.
- **Cour Napoléon** — the large courtyard with the pyramid. It is busy, but it gives you the classic Louvre view.
- **Cour Carrée** — if you have time, continue east into the older square courtyard. It is usually quieter and gives you a stronger sense of the Louvre as a palace.
- **Optional nearby detour: Palais Royal** — just north of the Louvre, with its courtyard, striped columns, arcades, and garden. Add this if you want a stylish 20-minute detour.

---

### 5. Down to the Seine — segment

From the Louvre, head south toward the river.

This is where the walk changes mood. The formal palace setting gives way to the riverfront: bridges, boats, bookstalls, stone embankments, and long views across the Seine.

**What to look for**
- **The Seine embankments** — walk close to the river if you can. The lower quays are one of the best parts of central Paris.
- **Pont des Arts** — this pedestrian bridge near the Louvre has beautiful views back toward the museum and across to the Left Bank.
- **Institut de France** — across the river, look for the domed building facing the Louvre. It gives this stretch of the Seine one of its most classic views.
- **Bouquinistes** — depending on where you walk, you may see the green bookstalls along the river. They are part of the historic character of the Seine.

---

### 6. Pont Neuf — anchor (Area entry: Île de la Cité)

Follow the Seine east toward **Pont Neuf**.

**What to look for**
- **Pont Neuf** — despite its name, the oldest stone bridge in Paris. It connects both banks of the Seine with Île de la Cité.
- **The carved faces** — look along the bridge for the sculpted stone faces, called mascarons. Once you notice them, the bridge becomes much more interesting.
- **Statue of Henri IV** — at the point where the bridge meets Île de la Cité, look for the equestrian statue of Henri IV.
- **Views back toward the Louvre** — before crossing fully onto the island, look back west.

---

### 7. Square du Vert-Galant — mood/pacing

Once you reach Île de la Cité, drop down to **Square du Vert-Galant**, the small park at the western tip of the island.

**What to look for**
- **The point of Île de la Cité** — you are standing where the Seine splits around the island.
- **Riverboats and bridges** — a good place to watch boats pass and see the bridges from below.
- **A quiet pause** — a good place to sit for a few minutes before moving into the busier historic core of the island.

---

### 8. Place Dauphine — mood/pacing

Come back up from Square du Vert-Galant and walk into **Place Dauphine**.

**What to look for**
- **The hidden-square feeling** — tucked just behind Pont Neuf, calmer and more intimate.
- **Cafés and benches** — a good stop for coffee, wine, or a short rest.
- **The triangular layout** — the square narrows toward the west, which gives it a slightly unusual shape.

---

### 9. Conciergerie and Palais de Justice — anchor

Continue east across Île de la Cité toward the large palace and justice complex.

**What to look for**
- **Conciergerie** — part of the medieval royal palace and later a prison during the French Revolution.
- **Marie Antoinette history** — she was imprisoned here before her execution.
- **Palais de Justice** — still has a legal and institutional function.
- **Clock Tower** — look for the ornate clock on the side of the complex.

---

### 10. Sainte-Chapelle — anchor (interior option)

Next to the Conciergerie is **Sainte-Chapelle**.

**What to look for**
- **The stained glass** — the upper chapel is surrounded by enormous stained-glass windows.
- **The contrast with Notre-Dame** — Sainte-Chapelle feels delicate, vertical, and full of light. Notre-Dame feels heavier, grander, and more monumental.
- **A short but memorable visit** — if you only choose one paid interior stop on the walk, choose Sainte-Chapelle.
- **Combined ticket option** — pair Sainte-Chapelle with the Conciergerie for more history.

---

### 11. Flower Market Area — mood/pacing

Continue east toward Notre-Dame and pass near the **Marché aux Fleurs Reine Elizabeth II**.

**What to look for**
- **Small market atmosphere** — depending on the day and time, it may be lively or quiet.
- **A change in scale** — after the Louvre, Pont Neuf, and the justice complex, this area feels smaller and more local.
- **Good wandering streets** — this part of Île de la Cité is worth walking slowly.

*Note: this POI is not currently in the corpus. Algorithm would treat this as a corpus gap if it tried to fire content here. Authored intent: this is a pacing/mood stop between the heavy-history cluster and the climactic destination.*

---

### 12. Notre-Dame — anchor (destination)

End at **Notre-Dame de Paris**.

**What to look for**
- **The west façade** — start with the classic front view: the twin towers, three portals, and large rose window.
- **Point Zéro** — in the square in front of Notre-Dame, look for the small marker from which distances to Paris are traditionally measured.
- **The interior** — if the line and timing work, go inside. Notre-Dame is one of the defining Gothic cathedrals of Europe.
- **The back of the cathedral** — walk around the eastern end to see the flying buttresses and the cathedral from the river side.
- **The Seine views** — the area around the back of Notre-Dame gives you some of the best views of the river and nearby bridges.

## Part B — Sample stops (Layer B output)

The full Layer A walk above is what gets walked physically. For MVP, the audio is generated for one Profile. Below: the actual narrative content for **three illustrative personas** at two of the stops (Pont Neuf and Place Dauphine). Only one of these would actually fire for an MVP single-Profile Trip — the three together demonstrate how the same stop produces different audio for different Profiles, which is the architectural property the design preserves for post-MVP multi-Profile work.

The three personas are illustrative:
- **Adult A** — Royal history / Dark history / Architecture lenses (history-anchored)
- **Adult B** — Art / Nature lenses (visual / atmospheric)
- **Child (~10)** — kid_friendly profile, lens-flexible

---

### Stop 6 — Pont Neuf

#### Adult A (history / architecture) — ~120-150s

Alright, we're coming onto Pont Neuf, which literally means "New Bridge", but in classic Paris fashion, it is actually the oldest surviving bridge in the city.

This bridge was started in the late 1500s under King Henry III and completed under King Henry IV in the early 1600s. And when it opened, it was a big deal. Paris at the time was still a dense medieval city, with narrow streets, crowded buildings, and bridges that often had houses built directly on top of them. Pont Neuf was different. It was wide, open, and designed for movement, views, and public life.

So as we walk across, notice how open it feels. That was intentional. Unlike older medieval bridges, this one did not have houses built on it. That meant Parisians could actually see the river from the bridge. It turned the Seine into part of the city's public stage.

Now look along the sides of the bridge and you'll see these carved stone faces. These are called mascarons. Some are grotesque, some are funny, some look half-human, half-mythological. There are hundreds of them. They give the bridge personality. It is very Parisian: elegant from a distance, a little weird up close.

As we reach the middle, you'll see the statue of Henry IV on horseback. He is one of the most important kings in French history, partly because he helped bring some stability after years of religious conflict. He was also remembered as a practical, popular king, the kind of king people imagined wanted every family to have "a chicken in the pot" on Sundays.

This spot is also important because we are touching the western tip of Île de la Cité, the island where Paris began. Before Paris was the city of grand boulevards, museums, and cafés, it was a settlement on this island. So in a way, Pont Neuf is not just a bridge over the Seine. It is a bridge between versions of Paris: medieval Paris behind the island, royal Paris around the Louvre, and the modern city spreading out on both banks.

And this is why I love stopping here. From one bridge, you can see so much of the city's story. Behind us is the Louvre, once a fortress and then a royal palace. Ahead of us is Île de la Cité, with the Conciergerie, Sainte-Chapelle, and Notre-Dame. Below us is the Seine, the reason this city exists in the first place.

Before we keep going, take a second and look both ways along the river. This is one of those places where Paris makes the most sense.

#### Adult B (art / nature) — ~120s

Alright, let's slow down as we step onto Pont Neuf.

For a moment, don't think of this as a bridge. Think of it as a viewing platform. This is one of the best places in Paris to understand how the city composes itself visually.

Look first at the Seine. The river is not just moving through the city. It is shaping the whole scene. The light changes on the water every few seconds. The buildings along the banks are heavy and still, but the river keeps everything alive. That contrast is a big part of why Paris feels so painterly.

Now look at the way the bridge opens up the view. Older bridges in Paris often had buildings crowded onto them, but Pont Neuf was left open. That matters because it gives us space to see. From here, the river, the sky, the stone, and the island all come into one frame.

If you were painting this scene, you would probably not start with the buildings. You would start with the horizontal line of the river, then the soft curve of the bridge, then the vertical accents: towers, chimneys, trees, lampposts, and the statue of Henri IV. Paris works because it mixes order with movement. The architecture gives structure, but the river, trees, clouds, and people keep it from becoming rigid.

Now look closely at the bridge itself. Along the sides are these carved stone faces, called mascarons. They are strange, expressive, sometimes almost cartoonish. From far away, Pont Neuf looks elegant and symmetrical. Up close, it has humor and texture. That is one of the things I love about Paris. It is never only polished. There is always some weird human detail if you look closely enough.

Ahead of us is Île de la Cité, the island where the city began, but what I want you to notice right now is how natural it feels in the river. The island splits the Seine into two channels, so the water wraps around it. The trees at the tip of the island soften the stone. That little green point down there, Square du Vert-Galant, is one of the best places to feel the relationship between Paris and the river.

From here, you can also see why so many artists were drawn to the Seine. It gives you layers: foreground, water, bridge, island, skyline, sky. It is almost already a painting before anyone touches a brush.

So before we keep walking, take a second and just look in both directions. Notice where your eye goes first. Is it the water? The curve of the bridge? The trees? The stone faces? The light on the buildings?

That is the real beauty of Pont Neuf. It is not only a way to cross the river. It is a place where Paris teaches you how to look.

#### Child (~10, all lenses, kid_friendly) — ~60-75s

Alright, we're stepping onto Pont Neuf.

First funny thing: the name means "New Bridge," but this is actually the oldest bridge in Paris. So Paris basically named its oldest bridge "New Bridge." Very normal. Very French.

Now, as we walk, I want you to look along the sides of the bridge. See if you can spot the weird stone faces carved into it. They are called mascarons, but you do not need to remember that word. Just look for the funny faces.

Some look grumpy. Some look surprised. Some look like they just smelled something terrible. There are hundreds of them, so this is basically a giant stone face collection hiding on a bridge.

Now look down at the river. This is the Seine, and it is one of the main reasons Paris grew here in the first place. Before cars, trucks, and trains, rivers were like superhighways. People used them to move food, supplies, people, and ideas. So this river was not just pretty. It was useful.

Ahead of us is an island called Île de la Cité. That is where Paris first began. So we are not just crossing a bridge. We are walking toward the oldest heart of the city.

When we get to the middle of the bridge, look for the man on the horse. That is King Henri IV. He was a real king, but for today you can just think of him as the guy guarding the entrance to the island.

And here is the best part: from this bridge, you can see a lot at once. Behind us is the Louvre, which used to be a palace. Ahead of us is the island with old churches and towers. Below us is the river. Around us are boats, people, stone faces, and probably at least one person taking way too many photos.

So your mission while we cross is simple:
- Find the weirdest stone face.
- Then look both directions down the river and decide which view looks more like a movie scene.

### Stop 8 — Place Dauphine

#### Adult A (general adult / history) — ~60-90s

As the bridge gives way to the island, the city suddenly gets quieter.

This is one of the strange pleasures of Paris. You can move from one of the most famous bridges in the city into a square that many people miss entirely. Just through here is Place Dauphine, tucked behind the traffic and the stone walls, almost hiding in plain sight.

It was created in the early 1600s under Henri IV, the same king whose statue we just passed on horseback. He wanted this part of the island to feel orderly and elegant, but also livable. And you can feel that here. Place Dauphine does not try to overwhelm you. It is not a grand plaza like Place de la Concorde. It is smaller, softer, more human.

The shape is part of the charm. It narrows toward the western end of the island, almost like the bow of a ship pointing back toward the Seine. The buildings around it were designed with a kind of quiet harmony: brick, stone, regular windows, cafés at ground level, trees softening the space in the middle.

It is the kind of place that reminds you Paris is not only made of monuments. It is also made of pauses.

And that is why this square works so well here. Behind us, Pont Neuf gave us the open river and the big view. Ahead of us, the island becomes more serious: the old palace, the courts, Sainte-Chapelle, the Conciergerie, Notre-Dame. But Place Dauphine gives us this brief moment in between. A little pocket of calm before the city starts telling heavier stories again.

So as we pass, notice how quickly the feeling changes. The noise drops. The scale tightens. The city comes closer.

That is Place Dauphine's magic. It does not announce itself. It rewards you for noticing.

#### Adult B (art / nature) — ~90s

As we leave the openness of the river, the city folds inward.

The Seine gave us light, movement, and distance. Here, around Place Dauphine, Paris does something different. It gathers itself into a smaller frame.

This square is not dramatic in the way the Louvre is dramatic. It is not trying to dominate the skyline. Its beauty is quieter. The trees soften the stone. The façades create rhythm without becoming rigid. The cafés and windows bring the scale back down to daily life.

There is something almost painterly about the transition. On the bridge, the composition was wide: river, sky, island, long views. Here, the composition tightens. The eye moves from tree trunks to shutters, from café awnings to stone walls, from the open sky above the square down to the people moving through it.

Place Dauphine is one of those Paris spaces where nature does not need to be wild to matter. A few trees are enough to change the whole feeling. They break up the geometry. They let the light filter through. They make the square feel less like a design on paper and more like a place people actually inhabit.

The shape helps too. It is not a perfect open square. It has a narrowing, almost triangular quality because it sits near the pointed western end of Île de la Cité. That gives it a gentle sense of enclosure, like the city has made a small room for itself right in the middle of the river.

And after the carved faces of Pont Neuf, there is a similar pleasure here: beauty that does not need to shout. The details are domestic, human, and easy to miss. A balcony. A window. A tree bending slightly over a café table. The way the stone changes color when the light moves.

Paris is often described through its grand monuments, but places like this are where the city becomes intimate.

As we keep walking, hold onto that contrast: the river as movement, the square as stillness. Both are Paris. One lets the city breathe. The other lets it rest.

#### Child (~10, all lenses, kid_friendly) — ~45-60s

As we come off the bridge, this part of Paris starts to feel a little more secret.

Right over here is Place Dauphine. It is a small square hidden behind the busy streets, and a lot of people walk right past without even realizing it is there. That makes it feel a bit like a secret level in a video game.

It is not the biggest or loudest place we will see today. There are no giant towers, no huge fountains, no pyramid. But that is kind of the point. This is one of those places where Paris takes a break.

The shape is interesting too. It is not a normal square. It is more like a triangle, because we are on the pointy end of the island. If Île de la Cité were a ship, this area would be near the front, and Place Dauphine would be one of the quiet little cabins tucked inside.

Look at how different it feels from the bridge. On Pont Neuf, everything was open: the river, the boats, the big views. Here, the buildings come closer, the trees make shade, and it feels more like a neighborhood.

This is a good reminder that cities are not only made of famous things. They are also made of places where people sit, eat lunch, read, talk, or do absolutely nothing for a few minutes.

So your quick mission as we pass is this: decide whether Place Dauphine feels more like a secret hideout, a movie set, or a tiny village inside the city.

There is no wrong answer, but "secret hideout" is probably the best one.

## What this sample demonstrates

**Layer A delivers shape.** The same 12 stops, in the same order, define the walk. The route is selected for geographic flow + anchor coverage + mood pacing + interior options — not for any one person's interests.

**Layer B delivers personalisation.** Demonstrated across three personas at the same stop (only one runs in MVP):
- The set of "anchor points" mentioned overlaps heavily (mascarons, Henri IV, island gateway, "look both ways" close are all present in all three Pont Neuf versions).
- The *treatment* of each anchor point differs by lens: Henri IV is a royal-political anchor for Adult A, a vertical compositional accent for Adult B, "the guy guarding the entrance" for the kid.
- Durations scale by Profile depth — adult content is roughly twice as long as kid content at the same stop.
- All three close with a physical action cue.

**Cross-stop awareness.** Place Dauphine's audio for all three Profiles references Pont Neuf ("as the bridge gives way," "as we leave the openness of the river," "as we come off the bridge"). Layer B sees the sequence.

**Atmospheric content carries low-gravity stops.** Place Dauphine is a T2 POI with thin factual material. The audio leans on observational/atmospheric content rather than fact, for all three Profiles.

**Area framing is woven, not separated.** "You're touching the western tip of Île de la Cité, the island where Paris began" — that's the Area-framing duty, delivered inside the Pont Neuf narrative rather than as a separate beat.
