— Jardin des Tuileries —
  Settle in.
  Whether you walk west to east or east to west — the preferable route, along the 'grand axis' in the direction of the obelisk in Place de la Concorde, the Champs-Elysées, and the Arc de Triomphe — the garden of the Tuileries of central Paris has survived a history of violence such as you would never imagine from the perspective of today's fastidiously groomed flower beds and treescapes.
  Once the private preserve of royal families, the garden displays a dazzling formal design decorated with gleaming white sculptures of mythological and historical celebrities.
  The more than one thousand trees — linden, sycamore, pine, palm, maple, clipped horse chestnut, boxed orange — provide ample shade and breezes, and the basins are ringed with chairs.
  Just inside the entrance at Place de la Concorde — 'one of the true wonders of the world,' in the words of Ina Caro's Paris to the Past — to the left of the large octagonal basin, the statue of Le Silence stands out from the crowd, as if coolly contemplating the passage of time.
  A sensual and graceful figure, her dispassionate gaze registers the garden's mortal haunted beauty, its bloody history buried under the linden trees.
  So where does that name come from?
  The name Tuileries comes from tuileries, the French word for tile works — the site was originally owned and occupied by the city's tile manufacturers in the thirteenth century, and bought by François I in 1517.
  It was his daughter-in-law, Catherine de Médicis, who, as the grieving widow of Henri II, claimed the property for herself.
  The niece of a pope and daughter of a Florentine banker, Catherine loved elaborate gardens in the style of the Italian Renaissance popular in sixteenth-century Florence.
  In 1564 she laid out the original Tuileries Gardens in the Italian style of her native taste, forbidding access to the ramparts on the east side to protect her privacy in the small garden beside the palace — the grieving Medici queen mother who first established a lavish garden, with a palace thrown in on the side, in the heart of the capital.
  Paris disliked her extravagance: Ronsard (1524–1585), the 'prince of poets,' put it this way: 'The queen must cease building; Her lime must stop swallowing our wealth. . . . Of what use is her Tuileries to us! Of none, Moreau: it is but vanity.'
  Like Catherine herself, the Tuileries' history during her reign as queen dowager was tempestuous.
  Strolling under the lindens one August afternoon in 1572, the fanatically Catholic Catherine is reputed to have discussed, planned, and given the signal for the Saint Bartholomew's Day Massacre on August 24.
  With her approval, fifteen thousand Protestants were slaughtered in a frenzy of religious hatred, their bodies hacked to death around the Louvre, the Tuileries, and the French countryside.
  Catherine's pathetic son, King Charles IX, looked on from his window in the Louvre and cried, 'Kill them all! Kill them all!'
  In Rome, the triumphant pope ordered a Te Deum sung.
  Henri IV had Jean Le Nôtre restore the gardens after the wars of religion and planted mulberry trees, hoping to develop a French silk industry and break the Milanese monopoly.
  In 1664 Colbert commissioned Jean's grandson André Le Nôtre (1633–1700), the great landscape artist who designed both Versailles and the Tuileries we see today, to redesign the gardens west of the palace into a perfect jardin à la française.
  And in June 1662, Louis XIV — to celebrate the birth of his first son — held a spectacle of equestrian evolutions called a carousel.
  Fifteen thousand spectators packed the grandstand on the 2nd and 3rd, and, dressed as a Roman Emperor, the Sun King caracoled and strutted, playing to his future favourite Louise de la Vallière.
  The name of the garden has stuck ever since — though today's visitor usually associates it with Napoleon's 1805 Arc du Carrousel at its eastern edge.
  On 1 December 1783, the first free flight of a manned hydrogen balloon lifted off from the Tuileries, with Jacques Alexandre César Charles and Nicolas Louis Robert aboard.
  The King watched the flight from the tower of the palace, and Benjamin Franklin, the first American ambassador to Paris, had attended the balloon's first trial in the garden.
  The balloon — and its passengers — landed safely thirty-one miles from Paris, at Nesles-la-Vallée.
  On 6 October 1789 the infuriated people of Paris marched Louis XIV's great-great-grandson, Louis XVI, back from Versailles to the Tuileries — the Louvre being by then too dilapidated to inhabit.
  A long, strange procession escorted the royal coach: National Guards, royal troops, Parisian crowds, and carts of corn and flour watched over by sharp-eyed mutinous women.
  From that day the Tuileries replaced the Louvre as the residence of French monarchs.
  Two centuries after the massacre under the lindens, during the Terror, the garden witnessed the massacre of the Tuileries palace's Swiss Guards.
  The central pool was the departure point of the Revolutionary ceremony for the Supreme Being, orchestrated by a thickly powdered Robespierre dressed in blue, a bouquet of flowers in one hand, a torch in the other, haranguing the crowds.
  The master of ceremony, the painter David, fretted at the head of a red-painted float drawn by eight oxen, surmounted by a Liberty figure.
  Two thousand four hundred singers and a thousand musicians accompanied the procession into the Place de la Révolution — today's Concorde — where they skirted the guillotine before heading for the Champ-de-Mars.
  Meanwhile, a monster emerged from the pool in the shape of a pyramid, representing atheism, egoism, false modesty, discord, and ambition.
  Set alight, the monster was reduced to ashes, and Wisdom appeared in her splendour.
  All that survives of this inflamed idealism are some marble benches in a semicircle on either side of the entrance to the central alley — placed by the well-intentioned Robespierre for the aged participants.
  On 10 October 1794, the remains of J.J. Rousseau, wrapped in a gold-studded azure flag, were deposited by this same pool on their way from Ermenonville to the Panthéon.
  Robespierre was not present: the guillotine had disposed of him too, a couple of months before.
  After his 1851 coup Louis Napoleon promptly abandoned the Republican Palais de l'Élysée for the royal Tuileries.
  Louis-Philippe hated being watched by garden visitors walking past his window, so he had a large flower garden — protected by a moat — built to isolate his residence in the palace from the popular footpaths.
  Parisians hated it, and it contributed to his downfall in 1848.
  In 1871 the Paris Commune burned the palace to the ground, and the victorious infant Third Republic refused to restore the charred carcass — in 1882 it razed the ruin entirely, 'securing irrevocably,' as Vallois puts it, 'the definitive death of the monarchy.'
  But the garden, the oldest and largest public green space in Paris, has survived for almost five centuries.
  The Arc du Carrousel's esplanade once held the sumptuous Hôtel de Rambouillet — red brick and white stone in the style of Henri IV and Louis XIII, on the now-vanished rue Saint-Thomas-du-Louvre.
  The Marquise de Rambouillet held her salon littéraire here in her chambre bleue — her blue-hung drawing room.
  All the literary set attended: Descartes, Scarron, Corneille, Madame de Sévigné, Madame de La Fayette, Mlle de Scudéry.
  The aristocracy too — Richelieu, the Prince de Condé.
  This is where the French language, hitherto as crude as the uncouth society that spoke it, was purified and polished; where conversation developed into an art, even an affectation; where French society finally shed its medieval remnants and was initiated into the urbane lifestyle that would dominate the next two centuries.
  You can imagine Edith Wharton and her lover, Morton Fullerton, who in the early twentieth century sat on a terrace above the Seine, long after Marie Antoinette walked in the same spot, under the same trees.
  'He said "My love! My darling!" How happy I was! Never had we been happier. . . . Blissful hours,' Wharton later wrote.
  Today you can watch the children navigating boats with long poles, or, alongside the northern terrace — La Terrasse des Feuillants — delighting in the trampoline facing the statue of the brilliant author of fairy tales Charles Perrault (1628–1703), his Puss 'n Boots character tucked in to his side.
  Bright flowers decorate the lawns, a sweeping green, luminous under the wide spray of fountains.
  Cafés, inside the deep greenery of chestnut groves, offer shade and decent food; the café on La Terrasse de Pomone faces a pool of water lilies with a statue of Daphne pursued by Apollo.

— Arc de Triomphe du Carrousel —
  From Hotel Le Meurice, carry on to the Arc de Triomphe du Carrousel, about a 6-minute walk away.
  You're standing at the smaller of Paris's two triumphal arches.
  It was designed by Charles Percier and Pierre François Léonard Fontaine and raised for the Emperor Napoleon the First between 1806 and 1808, to commemorate his victories in the Wars of the Third and Fourth Coalitions.
  Why does it feel so? They modelled it openly on ancient Rome — in particular the Arch of Septimius Severus and the Arch of Constantine, built there in the year 312.
  Around the outside stand eight marble columns set in the Corinthian order, in the Neoclassical style.
  Those columns weren't cut for the arch at all: they were salvaged from the Château de Meudon, which had been pulled down in 1804.
  Look up to the tops of the columns: each is crowned by a marble soldier, and together the eight figures reproduce, in careful detail, the uniforms of the principal corps of Napoleon's imperial army.
  Only one of them portrays a real man — the sapper, modelled on Dominique Gaye Mariolle, a soldier known for his strength and for a height that topped two metres.
  He is remembered for a single gesture: during a review of the troops before Tilsit, in July 1807, he presented a cannon to Napoleon in place of a rifle.
  The carved panels set into the arch are a roll-call of Napoleon's triumphs, worked in rose-coloured marble.
  Among them are the Battle of Austerlitz, the surrender of Ulm, the entrances into Vienna and Munich, the Peace of Pressburg, and the conference at Tilsit.
  The battle subjects were chosen by Vivant Denon, director of the Musée Napoléon — then housed in the Louvre — and drawn up by Charles Meynier.
  The chariot and horses on top of the arch carry a story of conquest and reversal.
  When the arch went up, it was crowned by the Horses of Saint Mark, taken from St Mark's Basilica in Venice — Napoleon had seized them in 1798.
  They didn't stay long.
  In 1815, after the defeat at Waterloo and the return of the Bourbon kings, France ceded the horses to the Austrian Empire, which then held Venice under the Congress of Vienna; the Austrians sent them straight back to Venice.
  So in 1828 they were replaced by the group you see now, sculpted by Baron François Joseph Bosio: Peace riding in a triumphal chariot, led by gilded Victories on either side — raised not to Napoleon, but to the restored Bourbon monarchy that had outlasted him.
  The arch was ordered by Napoleon as the gateway to his palace, which once stood here overlooking the Tuileries Gardens.
  He then wanted a larger arch, which was ultimately to be the Arc de Triomphe.
  When the Tuileries Palace burned down in 1871, the two arches could be seen together for the first time, as the palace no longer blocked the view.
  On the top of the Arc de Carrousel Napoleon placed the four horses from St Mark's in Venice, which he had requisitioned.
  They had to be replaced by copies when the Venetians asked for them back.
  In 1815, after the defeat at Waterloo and the return of the Bourbon kings, the Venetians got their horses back — France ceded them to the Austrian Empire, which then held Venice under the Congress of Vienna, and the Austrians sent them straight back to Venice, leaving Paris to replace them.
  This arch was never meant to stand on its own: Napoleon ordered it as the ceremonial gateway to the Tuileries Palace, the imperial residence that once stood here overlooking the Tuileries Gardens and closed off this end of the Louvre.
  When that palace burned down in 1871, during the Paris Commune, clearing its ruins opened the long, unobstructed view west — and for the first time the two arches could be seen together.
  Look down that long axis to the west and you can pick out its far more famous sibling, the Arc de Triomphe de l'Étoile at the end of the Champs-Élysées — about twice this size, the larger arch Napoleon wanted next.

— Louvre Museum —
  From Arc de Triomphe du Carrousel, make your way on to Louvre Museum, about a 3-minute walk away.
  The Louvre was once the palace of the kings of France, built in the 12th century and enlarged by one ruler after another over the ages, until it sprawled into eight miles of corridors.
  Look at the sides of the courtyard and you can pick out the kings and queens by their initials.
  H is for Henry II, interlaced with C for his wife Catherine; L is for Louis XIII, interlaced with A for his wife Anne of Austria; and LB is for Louis de Bourbon.
  Above one of the exit arches, a rooster — the symbol of France — stands in the pediment.
  But the Louvre you're standing before began as something built for war.
  In 1190, before leaving on the Third Crusade, Philippe-Auguste ringed the right bank with a three-thousand-metre wall — three metres thick, ten metres high — and anchored it on the west with a fortress at the river's edge, facing the Tour de Nesle opposite.
  He had reason to worry: the Plantagenets sat just downstream in Normandy, and within living memory the Norsemen had invaded Paris the same way.
  Even his brother-in-law Richard the Lionheart, his companion on the holy journey to Jerusalem, was no more reliable than the rest.
  At the heart of the new fortification rose a round keep, nineteen metres across and thirty-one metres above ground — the first Louvre.
  On his return in 1191 he deposited the Crown treasures and archives here, deeming it the safest place in Paris — "la plus halte tor de Paris la citet," the tallest tower in Paris the city.
  Charles V, an enlightened king for all his devotion to fortifying his capital, made the Louvre his residence in 1368 and gathered the kingdom's manuscripts within its thick walls, founding the first royal library of France — the seed from which, two centuries later, the greatest painting collection in the world would grow.
  François I, settling into a new period of peace and refinement, decided to dispense with the defensive function of the Louvre and replace the medieval fortress with a Renaissance palace.
  The people of Paris were quite upset by the sudden disappearance in 1528 of la Grosse Tour — the Big Tower — where Charles V used to read and meditate.
  For François, fortresses carried unpleasant associations: he had recently been released from the fortress of Madrid, where Emperor Charles V of Spain had kept him captive after defeating him at Pavia in 1525.
  His overhauled Louvre was meant to be the opposite of a prison — a sumptuous backdrop for tournaments and jousts, where in 1540 he dazzled that same Emperor Charles V during his state visit.
  In 1593, four years after his accession, Henri IV bartered his Protestant religion for Paris to end the bloody strife between Protestants and Catholics — "Paris vaut bien une messe," Paris is well worth a mass.
  And Paris meant first and foremost the Louvre.
  Catherine de Medici had built the Palais des Tuileries facing the Louvre in 1564, to the west, running north–south along the Seine.
  Henri IV set out to connect the two palaces with a colossal wing of monumental grandeur — La Grande Galerie, four hundred and seventy metres long, also known as the Galerie du Bord de l'Eau because it ran along the water's edge.
  It met the Tuileries at right angles and was capped with the elegant Pavillon de Flore.
  The enterprise was a constant headache for his minister Sully, who had to squeeze segments of the population dry to raise the funds.
  Louis XIV was fourteen when he settled in the Louvre in 1652, after the princely rebellion — la Fronde — had been quelled and it was deemed safe for him to return to Paris.
  As long as he resided here, he channelled enormous energy and money into completing the construction around the Cour Carrée; the show had to go on, and to exceed in flashy splendour that of his predecessors.
  His was the reign of glamour par excellence.
  Scintillating balls, ceremonial dinners of twenty dishes, then performances of Corneille and Molière — His Majesty and his brother Monsieur were great lovers of theatre, unlike their grandfather Henri IV, who once confided to a woman friend: "Yesterday evening I saw the comedians play, when I fell asleep. It was midnight when they finished."
  During his reign the painting collection went from two hundred pieces to two thousand, largely thanks to his minister Mazarin's feverish acquisitions and Colbert's patronage — bolstered, before his disgrace, by ample donations from Nicolas Fouquet, the king's wealthy treasurer.
  He never liked the Louvre, and in 1682 he moved his court to the newly-built palace at Versailles, leaving the Louvre to fall to pieces.
  The King's vacant apartment became the premises of the Académie des Sciences, housing an elephant skeleton, a stuffed camel, and various anatomical organs floating in alcohol-filled jars.
  Booths and stalls crammed the courtyard.
  The magnificent halls were devastated, the panelling of the royal apartments torn off, the painted ceilings drilled through to make room for skylights and stove pipes.
  A motley population of artisans, parasitical courtiers, and prowlers replaced the splendid members of the court who had moved to Versailles — reluctantly in many cases, resentful at having to give up the independence they had enjoyed in the city.
  So when did all this become a museum anyone could walk into?
  The royal collections opened to the public at the end of the Revolution, in 1794.
  The painter Auguste Renoir grew up in one of the slum lanes that then ran through the Esplanade of the Louvre.
  He was one of the street urchins to whom Queen Amélie's ladies-in-waiting threw sweets through the window when they made too much of a nuisance of themselves.
  His son Jean, the film director, later wondered "how successive kings could have tolerated such a seedy neighbourhood under their very noses."
  The archaeological excavation of the medieval Louvre began in 1983, urged on by the dynamic Minister of Culture André Malraux.
  President Mitterrand, a lover of "grands projets," took up the scheme wholeheartedly — and the pyramid he championed would earn him the nickname 'the Sphinx.'
  I.M. Pei's glass Pyramid — twenty-one metres high by thirty-four wide — crowned the new underground entrance, a scintillating diamond by night, while the old Finance Ministry offices along the northern side were converted into museum galleries.
  Not everyone hailed it; some dismissed it as too avant-garde, others objected to its location on a historic site.
  Its supporters countered that its geometrical starkness and transparency blended harmoniously into the old environment.
  Either way, the Pyramid opened in April 1989, timed to the bicentenary of the French Revolution, and five million visitors swarmed in within its first six months, beating every record.
  For the first time since François I, they could see vestiges of the medieval Louvre, known previously only from the illuminations of the Duc de Berry's Book of Hours.

— Comedie-Francaise —
  Leave the Louvre behind now and head for the Comédie-Française, about four minutes on foot.
  You'll find it in the southwest corner of place Colette, at an entrance to the garden of the Palais-Royal — the theatre they call La Maison de Molière, built between 1786 and 1790 by Victor Louis, the architect of the Bordeaux theatre.
  The Comédiens-Français inaugurated it on 30 May 1799 with a double bill of Le Cid and L'École des Maris, starring the great Talma and the adulated Mlle Mars.
  Some of its traditions have never lapsed: the baton-beats that announce the curtain's rise, the declamation of the Marseillaise on Bastille Day, and the celebration of Molière's death every February.
  And that death happened just around the corner.
  On the corner of rue de Valois and rue Saint-Honoré, Richelieu had built Le Petit Cardinal for his palace, and it was there, on 17 February 1673 at ten in the evening, that Molière — playing Argan in Le Malade Imaginaire — collapsed on stage and died of a heart attack.

— Palais-Royal —
  From Comedie-Francaise, carry on to Palais-Royal, just ahead.
  The arcades wrapped around this calm garden have one of the noisiest pasts in the city.
  It began as the Palais-Cardinal, the palace Cardinal Richelieu built for himself, before he bequeathed it to Louis XIII — and after the king's death the royal family moved in.
  It stood conveniently close to Mazarin's home on the neighbouring rue Neuve-des-Petits-Champs, so a door was fitted into the wall around the palace to ease his rendezvous with Anne of Austria — the same door the Queen and her two sons, the future Louis XIV and Philippe d'Orléans, slipped through to escape to Saint-Germain-en-Laye during the princely revolt of the Fronde in 1650.
  The fifth Duc d'Orléans — Philippe, father of the future King Louis-Philippe — was deep in debt when he took the place over, so he built the arcades and their shops, rented them out, and turned it into a profitable business, with gambling houses and brothels opening up too.
  His royal cousin was unimpressed: "Cousin," said Louis XVI disdainfully, "you have turned shop-keeper and no doubt we shall see you only on Sundays."
  Philippe had his revenge — in 1793 he joined the Revolution, sat in the Convention as "Philippe Égalité," and voted for the death of the king; the guillotine took charge of him too, only a few months later.
  Along with its taverns, all this wild commerce inside the colonnades earned the Palais-Royal its reputation as 'the home of Parisian hedonism,' while the coffeehouses drew journalists and politicians, bourgeoisie and aristocrats, both before and after 1789, who argued the radical politics that led to the Revolution.
  The storming of the Bastille on 14 July 1789 had its origins here — legend has it the journalist Camille Desmoulins stood on a café table to launch a call to arms.
  Victor Hugo later called it 'the core of the comet Revolution.'
  At no. 161 rue Saint-Honoré stood the Café La Régence — the Mecca of chess players, according to Diderot's Neveu de Rameau.
  The chessboards were rented by the hour, more at night when a candle had to be fixed on either side.
  "Paris is the place in the world, and the Café de la Régence the place in Paris where the game is best played" — and no fiction: François-André Danican-Philidor, composer, co-founder of the Opéra-Comique, and world chess champion, took on the world's best players here simultaneously and beat them one by one.
  His Analyse du jeu des échecs, published in London when he was twenty-two, remains a classic.
  In 1794 a young Napoleon split a wretched existence between a dump of a hotel on rue d'Aboukir and La Régence, where he spent hours moving pieces on a chessboard — rehearsing, in his way, for the strategic showdown soon to come.
  One of Colette's (and Julia Child's) favorite restaurants, Grand Véfour at 17, rue du Beaujolais under the northwestern arcades, opened in the 1780s as the Café de Chartres and still serves its haute cuisine in an elegant dining room.
  The Muscade, a friendly modest café just south of it with outdoor tables, serves a 'salade Colette.'
  The Café de Chartres at 79–82 Galerie de Beaujolais once served a Monarchist set menu of vermicelle et poitrine de mouton aux haricots.
  Its descendant, Le Grand Véfour, has abandoned homely fare for one of the best kitchens in Paris — and was, for this reason, targeted by Leftists to be blown up in December 1983.
  Next door at no. 83, Le Véry created a sensation as the first Paris establishment to offer meals at fixed prices.
  Among its clientele was the painter Fragonard, who actually expired here while savouring a sorbet.
  By the time Colette was living at 9 rue de Beaujolais — a plaque still marks it at the north end of the garden — and Jean Cocteau at 36 rue de Montpensier, the Palais-Royal had assumed its present aspect of outdated serenity.
  Colette, 1873 to 1954, ranked by many critics as France's greatest natural writer, had finally found her earthly paradise living above this garden.
  In the mornings," she wrote, "we went out for a breath of fresh air — cat, bulldog and me — on the garden chairs, uncomfortable armchairs of venerable age," and she catalogued her nameless, silent fellow citizens: the elderly lady leaning on her stick, the gentleman cultivating little cacti on his window sill, the other gentleman in straw slippers on his morning walk, the earnest little boy who might one day lay a marble in the palm of your hand, the lady who might read you the ode she had written to Victor Hugo.
  In Places she remembered this masterpiece of landscape and architecture, for centuries the social centre of Paris and 'a unique point on the globe': 'Visit London, Amsterdam, Madrid or Vienna, you will see nothing like it... It is called the capital of Paris. Everything is to be found there.'
  She makes you feel her delight as she looks down over the clipped lime trees at dawn and sunset, smelling after rain the flowers 'that last into late autumn' under the 'great ceiling of the Parisian sky,' watching 'the tree trunks of the arbor, the quivering of whipped-up water in the great fountain' — an 'enchantment' deepened by the 'mutual courtesy' of neighbours who included her friend Jean Cocteau.

  During the Occupation, from 1940 to 1944, when the Nazis made daily raids through the Palais apartments hunting for Jews, Colette's neighbours hid her Jewish husband, Maurice Goudeket, in their tiny maids' rooms and crawl spaces, saving him from deportation.
  She, in turn, learning that some of her neighbours, like most of Paris, were hungry during the war, gave them food and money.
  When she died, the nearby church of Saint-Roch refused her a Catholic funeral Mass, yet she received the first state funeral France ever gave a woman.
  Her bier rested in the Palais-Royal's Cour d'Honneur, in front of Cardinal Richelieu's original palace, while thousands of Parisians, mostly women, filed past leaving bunches of wildflowers in her honour.
  Today the Palais-Royal is a refuge of tranquillity, beautiful in all seasons but especially in spring, when a profusion of yellow and white tulips frames a pretty fountain surrounded by magnolia and lime trees, and Parisians sit on benches reading, sleeping and taking the sun.
  Step into the main garden with its elegant frame of classical architecture — a haven with seating, a children's play area, a fountain, statues and flowerbeds — and on either side, under the arcades, the specialist shops: tin soldiers, vintage clothes, musical boxes, alongside cafés and restaurants.

— Bourse de Commerce — Pinault Collection —
  From Palais-Royal, make your way on to Bourse de Commerce — Pinault Collection, about a 5-minute walk away.
  Standing before this great circular building, you might wonder how a former commodities exchange ended up housing contemporary art — the answer runs back centuries.
  The round building you stand in began as a granary.
  Between 1763 and 1767, the City of Paris raised a circular hall here for the storage and sale of wheat — the Halle aux blés, or Wheat Exchange, designed by Nicolas Le Camus de Mézières around a circular central courtyard reached by a double staircase.
  That eighteenth-century layout has been kept ever since; the ring you walk today still traces the old grain market's plan.
  The rotunda was first built by Louis XVI as La Halle au Blé for the trade of corn, flour, and sugar; the present structure dates from 1889.
  Look at the elegant buildings lining the northern curve of the circular rue des Viarmes — they sit on Catherine de Medici's Hôtel de Soissons, where she had fled Saint-Germain-l'Auxerrois after Ruggieri's prophecy.
  The tower from which her astrologer scrutinised the stars still stands, its graceful carving partly surviving.
  Ruggieri used the tower until the end of his life, and despite his unfavourable predictions, he never fell into disgrace — he had also, after all, correctly foretold the mortal injury of Henri II by Montgomery in 1559.
  This site holds a survivor far older than the exchange itself.
  Between 1574 and 1584, the architect Jean Bullant built a grand hôtel particulier here for Catherine de' Medici, and beside it rose a tower with an encaged observation platform — now known as the Medici column — from which the stars could be observed for astrological purposes.
  The queen's house later passed to the Count of Soissons, becoming the Hôtel de Soissons; but its last owner died in debt, and in 1748 the house was demolished and its materials sold to pay the creditors.
  The column alone was spared: that same year, the writer Louis Petit de Bachaumont bought it and saved it from destruction.
  Now look up at the dome — the building's crowning glory and today a listed historical monument.
  The first version, a wooden cupola, was built in 1782–83 by Jacques-Guillaume Legrand and Jacques Molinos, following the principles defined by Philibert de l'Orme.
  On 16 October 1802 fire destroyed it.
  After some controversy, a new design by François-Joseph Bélanger was chosen: an iron dome sheathed in copper.
  Victor Hugo, writing in his 1831 novel Notre-Dame de Paris, described it as 'an English jockey-cap on a large scale.'
  The building you see owes its present shape to a major reconstruction begun by the architect Henri Blondel in 1885 and carried out in 1888–89 — the structure standing today dates from its completion in 1889.
  He kept the circular form — twenty-five bays divided by pairs of pilasters — and reworked the structure around the surviving cupola.
  Above the entrance, look for the pediment: four fluted Corinthian columns carry a sculpted group by Aristide Croisy, in which three allegorical figures represent the City of Paris flanked by Trade and Abundance.
  Step inside and look up at the curved wall beneath the dome.
  The rotunda is wrapped in a painted panorama, the work of five painters — Alexis-Joseph Mazerolle, Évariste Vital Luminais, Désiré François Laugée, Georges Clairin and Hippolyte Lucas.
  Painted panels personify the four directions — North, East, South and West — while frescoes depict the history of trade between the five continents.
  Classified as a historical monument in 1975, the murals were restored in 1998.
  For more than a century this was a working trading floor.
  Futures markets ran here from the start — first in wheat, oats, flour, oil, sugar, alcohol and rubber, and after World War II in white sugar, cocoa, coffee, soybean meal and rapeseed.
  Then, in 1998, computerization ended trading in the building, and the exchange now survives as an electronic market within Euronext.
  In 2016 the mayor of Paris, Anne Hidalgo, offered the collector François Pinault a fifty-year lease on the Bourse de Commerce for a lump sum of fifteen million euros plus yearly fees, and the city approved turning the old exchange into a home for contemporary art.
  The boldest stroke came from the architect Tadao Ando: a thirty-foot-high concrete cylinder set inside the historic building to serve as the main exhibition gallery, standing within the nineteenth-century shell.
  Delayed from 2020 by the COVID-19 pandemic, the museum opened in mid-May 2021.
  The museum's first show borrowed its name from music: 'Ouverture' — the introductory piece played at the beginning of an opera.
  It introduced works by international artists including Urs Fischer, Kerry James Marshall, Marlene Dumas, Luc Tuymans and Cindy Sherman.

— Les Halles —
  Leaving the Bourse de Commerce behind, head for Les Halles — about a 4-minute walk.
  You are standing on ground that has been a market for nearly nine hundred years.
  In 1135 Louis VI set up a primitive market here on a piece of land allotted by the Bishop of Paris, against a return of one-third of its revenue.
  Its first name was Les Champeaux — Little Fields — probably drawn from the marshy market-gardens that surrounded it.
  In 1183 Philippe-Auguste replaced the open market with two stone buildings to shelter goods and vendors in bad weather, securing them with locks against theft.
  To raise the funds, he confiscated the possessions of the Jews.
  The market became known as Les Halles — 'pour ce que chacun y allait,' for everyone went there — a name that stuck for eight centuries, through which the place stayed Paris's pulsating lifeblood.
  From the 14th century on, the scaffold was part of the landscape here too.
  The King's pillory, largest of all the pillories in Paris, was a sizeable octagonal structure with a pointed roof and weathervane — resembling, oddly, a chapel.
  The Great Executioner often lived on the ground floor, while convicts were exposed in the tower above.
  The excited mob relished the sight, taunting the exposed victims and pelting them with rubbish.
  There were no gallows at Les Halles, however — the King conceded this was no place to leave bodies rotting on a rope.
  François Villon, in his Ballade des Femmes de Paris, had summed up the shrill fishwives of this market: "Il n'est bon bec que de Paris" — the big mouths of Paris are second to none.
  Those women, the Dames des Halles — the notorious poissardes, or fishwives — occupied a unique position in Parisian society: on ceremonial occasions they were admitted into the King's Palace to pay their respects to the royal couple.
  After a hearty meal they would return to Les Halles to pass on the latest Palace gossip, the physical proximity of high and low breeding a certain familiarity.
  On 30 August 1608, one of them, a market-woman named Femme Ladoucette, wrote an astonishing letter to Marie de Medici, Henri IV's second wife.
  She introduced herself as a Dame des Halles "from mother to daughter since Saint Louis" — and a mother of four by her husband, a guarantee of moral integrity — admitting to a forthright tongue, but not, she added, a lying one.
  Then she complained about the Queen's philandering husband, the King — a good fellow deep down, but one who fidgets at the sight of a coquette and scatters bastards around despite being blessed with a most appetising Queen, so well conditioned to have little princes.
  If the King ever wandered through Les Halles, she wrote, she would give him a sound thrashing for the love of her Queen.
  Far from being scolded, Femme Ladoucette was promoted — fournisseuse royale, royal purveyor.
  Henri II rebuilt the market in 1553; three centuries later, in the Industrial Age, Napoleon III repeated the move.
  Impressed by Victor Baltard's newly-built Gare de l'Est, the emperor commissioned him to rebuild Les Halles and pressed iron over stone: "Faites-moi des parapluies!" Make me umbrellas!
  Fourteen iron pavilions went up between 1851 and 1857, and stood until 1969.
  The corner house at 2 rue Sauval, in the heart of Les Halles, was the birthplace of Molière in 1622 — the playwright whose stormy funeral at Saint-Eustache nearby would one day require the King's personal intervention.
  The original house was later demolished and replaced by the one still standing today, where the composer Richard Wagner reportedly stayed in 1839.
  And why "the belly of Paris"? In 1873 Emile Zola gave Les Halles its most enduring nickname as the title of his novel.
  He had spent a year haunting the market at all hours, recording the ever-changing hues of this gigantic digestive apparatus of the capital: bluish-grey metal against a light grey sky in wet weather; sheets of gold on the hard-packed earth when the sun broke through; the glowing glass panes of the pavilions at sunset; gas-lamp lights against black roofs at night.
  Poultry, fish in wicker baskets, meat, cheese, butter and milk each had its own pavilion.
  Bouquets of thyme, laurel and garlic lay in neat rows at the foot of Saint-Eustache, the aroma of Provence vying with the smell of carrots and celery.
  He watched les forts des Halles — the robust porters, "all nerves and muscles" — then descended into the underground where the nightly slaughter was done, reeking of blood and overrun by rats.
  Meanwhile, in the upper world, bourgeois and artists drifted in from cabarets and theatres in the early hours, looking for strong emotions, finishing the onion soup and glass of wine with a chunk of baguette dunked in a café crème to celebrate the new day.
  Evelyn Waugh ate exactly that same onion soup at Au Père Tranquille in 1929.
  At level -1 of the Forum, the Musée Grévin presents twenty-one scenes from Belle Époque Paris in wax — kitschy and sketchy, but instructive: Victor Hugo, Emile Zola, Jules Verne, Pasteur, the old village of Montmartre, the 1900 Universal Exposition, the scintillating bourgeoisie at the Palais Garnier opera.
  At the Vidéothèque, the entire memory of Paris is preserved in video archives — fiction and documentary alike — and the siting is pointed: it sits on the ground where the organic heart of Paris once beat.
  A viewer need only press a button and the city comes to life on the screen.
  Of late, private homes have had links installed to the Vidéothèque, sparing the owners the bother of going outside — while disconnecting them from the real city around them.
  Next to it is another world of visual illusion.
  At the Parc Océanique Cousteau, video has replaced both the ocean's waters and its creatures — the fish spared from languishing in boredom in an artificial environment by the simple expedient of not being there at all.
  Exorbitant admission fees fund the Cousteau Society's quality-of-life-on-our-planet work; the gift shop next door continues the same cause.

— Conciergerie —
  From Les Halles, carry on to the Conciergerie, about a nine-minute walk away.
  The arcade on the right of the Cour de Mai was the entrance to the Conciergerie before 1825.
  The Conciergerie is one of the few remaining vestiges of the old medieval Palais de Justice — the royal palace before the Louvre was built — and it's Paris's oldest prison, where Marie-Antoinette and the leading figures of the Revolution were held before execution; some 2,600 prisoners passed through here on their way to the guillotine, and today Marie-Antoinette's cell can be visited in what is now the Museum of the Revolution.
  Inside are several splendid, vaulted Gothic halls, including the Salle des Gens d'Armes, built between 1301 and 1315; you come here for French history, but the Gothic halls and kitchen fireplaces earn their keep for architecture alone.
  The far end is separated off by an iron grille; during the Revolution this area was reserved for the pailleux — prisoners who couldn't afford to bribe a guard for their own cell and had to sleep on straw, or paille.
  The kitchen pavilion at the northwest corner — added around 1353, set slightly apart from the main building to reduce the risk of fire — was originally a two-storey operation: meals for the royal family and their guests cooked upstairs, meals for the staff cooked downstairs.
  On 6 January 1378, both kitchens worked together at full stretch.
  Charles V was hosting the Banquet of the Three Kings — welcoming his uncle Charles IV, Holy Roman Emperor, and his cousin Wenceslaus, King of the Romans.
  Three courses of ten dishes each were served in the Grand Hall to the royal guests and to eight hundred German and French knights.
  Look up at the Tour de l'Horloge — completed in 1350 under John II — the Conciergerie's tallest tower and Paris's oldest clock tower.
  Henri de Vic installed the mechanism here during the reign of Charles V: the first public clock in Paris.
  In 1585 Henry III dressed it up with a new face — gold fleurs-de-lis on a blue background, framed by statues of Law and Justice by Germain Pilon.
  The decoration was vandalised in the Revolution, and the great bell, rung for major events, was taken down and melted; the tower was most recently restored in 2012.
  In the 15th century passers-by could still hear the screams of tortured prisoners from the Tour Bonbec, the westernmost tower of the Conciergerie.
  During the tower's restoration in 1828, workers found two oubliettes in the basement: concealed dungeons spiked with iron points and connected to the river.
  Needless to dwell on the fate of the prisoners dropped into them.
  So why bon-bec? The name — medieval French for prate — probably alluded to the interrogations on the ground floor, where a varied spectrum of torture methods was devised to extract confessions and denunciations.
  North of the Gothic halls ran a section of the prison leading to the dark cells, known as rue de Paris — named after the Great Executioner, Monsieur de Paris.
  That post was held by seven successive generations of the Sanson family, from 1688 to 1847, transmitted from father to son.
  Shortly before the Revolution, Louis XVI had set his heart on improving the executioners' plight and granted Charles-Henri Sanson an annual fee of 16,000 pounds plus 2,000 crowns for the upkeep of the scaffold and pillory — never suspecting he would become his own executioner.
  Being a royalist, Charles-Henri had no desire to carry out that deed.
  On 15 August 1792 he was arrested as "the despot's employee"; with each execution he was dispatched from his cell to the Place du Carrousel, where the guillotine then stood, and afterwards taken back.
  Early on executions were few, but once the guillotine began to work at full swing and his expert hand could not be spared, he was released.
  He retired soon after he had executed his sovereign.
  His son Henri held the position through the Terror and the precarious regimes that followed, until 1840 — well into the reign of Louis-Philippe.
  On 10 August 1792, the first Paris Commune and the Sans-culottes seized the Tuileries and took charge of the Conciergerie.
  Three weeks later, during what came to be called the September Massacres, militants entered the city's prisons and in four days killed more than thirteen hundred prisoners.
  Here at the Conciergerie, of 488 inmates, 74 women and 36 men were released by the mob — and 378 were killed, the largest group dying in the Women's Courtyard on 2-3 September 1792.
  Of all the victims at this prison, only one was a woman: Marie Gredeler.
  Even during the Terror, the Conciergerie ran a tariff.
  A wealthy prisoner could rent a bed for 27 livres 12 sous for the first month, 22 livres 10 sous after that — eventually dropped to 15 — and the prison commanders made a fortune.
  As the Terror escalated, a prisoner could pay for a bed, be guillotined a few days later, and free the bed for a new inmate who would then pay as well.
  One memoirist called the Conciergerie 'the most lucrative furnished lodgings in Paris,' though for most prisoners the cells were cramped, rat-infested, and stinking of urine, and the special privileges of the wealthy were largely cut as the Terror peaked.
  The Revolutionary Tribunal moved in upstairs, in the Grand Chamber between the Silver and Caesar towers, renamed the 'Hall of Liberty'.
  Its public prosecutor, Antoine Quentin Fouquier-Tinville, set up his office and his residence next door.
  Trials were public, rapid, unappealable.
  The Law of Suspects on 17 September 1793 sent monthly executions from 11 to 124.
  On 10 June 1794 the law was tightened again — witnesses no longer required, 'suspect' enlarged — and by the end of that month an average of 38 people a day were tried, sentenced, and sent to the guillotine, four out of five condemned to die.
  In August 1794 Fouquier-Tinville himself was arrested.
  He spent nine months in this prison — pleading that 'the execution of the laws, justice and humanity were always my sole rules of conduct' — before going to the guillotine on 7 May 1795.
  In 780 days the Tribunal had sentenced and executed 2,780.
  During the Revolution the Conciergerie boasted an impressive list of prisoners awaiting their deaths.
  There was Madame Roland, who had supported the Revolution but sided with the Girondins at the wrong moment.
  There was Marie-Antoinette, who occupied a dreary cell from 2 August to 16 October 1793, until she was taken to the guillotine on the Place de la Révolution — today's Place de la Concorde.
  Later Danton, Robespierre, and Saint-Just were detained here in turn, Danton and Robespierre occupying the cells adjacent to the Queen's.
  In later centuries the notorious names held here included Ravaillac — the assassin of Henri IV — the mass poisoner La Marquise de Brinvilliers, who had eliminated the better part of her own household, and Cartouche (1693–1721), the most notorious bandit of his age, all three awaiting execution here.
  To be admitted into Cartouche's gang you had to prove yourself an expert cutpurse: candidates practised on dummies hung with little bells, and had to strip them without the slightest tinkling.
  The film-maker Jean Renoir marvelled that his own grandfather — the painter Renoir's father, a tailor from Limoges — had known Henri Sanson's chief assistant during the Terror.
  The old assistant, Renoir wrote, "may have passed Voltaire and Benjamin Franklin in the street, heard Mozart play the harpsichord, and attended performances of Monsieur Beaumarchais." Old Renoir met him in 1845, the year the family had moved up to Paris from Limoges and when the last Sanson still held the post.
  The two became friends — the tailor and the executioner's assistant — united by the pleasure of doing a good job.
  "After all," Jean Renoir observed, "they were two good workmen: one cut out cloth, the other cut off heads just as conscientiously."
  And that brings our walk to a close.
