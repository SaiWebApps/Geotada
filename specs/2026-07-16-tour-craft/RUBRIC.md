# What Makes a Good Ondoway Tour

**Companion document:** `specs/2026-07-19-tour-quality-standard/01-standard.md` —
the mechanical FLOOR/GATE checks (C1-C12 / G1-G8) and the gold-text example this
rubric's checklist is validated against.

## Definition

A good Ondoway stop is a short story told to one walker through earphones at the moment they arrive, not an encyclopedia entry read aloud. It opens on something happening or a question worth answering, aims the walker's eye at a thing they can actually see, carries one strong idea plus one human detail, hangs its names and dates off human stakes (who wanted what, what was at risk, what changed), speaks in second person and present-tense immediacy in plain spoken sentences you can say in one breath, and ends on a fact, an image, or an open thread — never on a stated moral. The failure mode every professional source names, and the one our testers named ("like reading Wikipedia aloud"), is the monotone recital of scripted facts with no voice, no person, no look-cue: correctness without a reason to care.

## VOICE

- **Second person, direct address — the walker is the protagonist standing there.** Rick Steves: "You're standing at the center of France, the point from which all distances are measured." (Rick Steves, *Paris Historic Walk* transcript; VoiceMap "Make Your Listener the Principal Character")
- **Present tense for immediacy — collapse the distance to the event.** "Shift into the present tense and tell the story as though it's unfolding right now." (VoiceMap, *Writing Scripts That Make Listeners Feel Something*; Listen Technologies, *Write Out Loud*)
- **A subjective narrator with a point of view — opinions, asides, warmth — not a neutral fact-reader.** "Your opinions, anecdotes and feelings for a place breathe life into it." (VoiceMap, *Your Perspective*; Autio: "a friend who knows the land")
- **Conversational register: contractions, plain words, "would you say it that way?"** Ira Glass: the more you "sound like a human being and talk like yourself, the more compelling." (NPR Training, *Would You Say It That Way?*; journalism.university, *Scriptwriting Tips*)

## STRUCTURE / HOOK

- **Open at the moment something is happening — never on a label, name, or founding date.** "Don't open with description. Open at the moment something is happening." (VoiceMap tip #5; look2innovate five-beat: open with "a curious question, surprising fact or vivid image")
- **Provoke, don't instruct — spark curiosity and relate the place to the listener's own experience.** "The chief aim of Interpretation is not instruction, but provocation"; interpretation that does not relate to "something within the personality or experience of the visitor" is sterile. (Freeman Tilden, via HeritageDestination *Tilden's Tips*)
- **Cue the eye to a specific named, visible feature — not "on your left."** "the figure on the right… holding the lantern," not "Look to your left." (look2innovate, *Museum Audio Guide Script Best Practices*; Nubart: "Take a step back and look at its sheer size")
- **Reward a little mental work and reveal an insider detail they'd otherwise miss.** "When you ask listeners' brains to do a little work in a way that rewards them, a switch flips"; "Make them feel like insiders." (VoiceMap tips #7 and #10)
- **Give the whole tour an arc that peaks — set-up, build-up, blow-up, up-shot — and use cliffhangers to carry the walk.** "A cliffhanger at the end of a stop can turn a walk into a page-turner"; present a whole rather than a part. (VoiceMap tips #11–12, Tom Darbyshire; Tilden principle 5; Autio)

## FACT-INTO-MOMENT

- **Tell one identifiable person's story, not the crowd's or the statistics'.** "Instead of telling the story of thousands who fought in a battle, tell the story of just one: the victor, the villain, or the victim." (VoiceMap, *Writing Scripts That Make Listeners Feel Something*; Pathoura: add "a human detail")
- **Facts are the material, not the point — lead with the stakes and hang names/dates off that spine.** Tours "come alive through personalities, relationships, emotions and drama," go dull on "names, dates and facts." (VoiceMap; Tilden: "Information does not equal interpretation")
- **Show, don't tell — one fresh concrete image or metaphor beats abstraction.** Rick Steves: "The Grand Canal was a Venetian chorus line of palaces doing an impressive can-can." (Rick Steves, *Venice Grand Canal* transcript; VoiceMap tip #3, "Theatre of the Mind")
- **Anchor to what the walker senses on-site right now.** "the cobbles under your feet are original"; "Venice is a barrage on the senses. Water, boats, tourists with luggage…" (Rick Steves, *Venice Grand Canal*; Tilden 2nd principle)
- **Default the stop's SUBJECT to the visible object in front of the walker, not an unseeable abstraction.** A seeable thing usually beats a story about something that can't be seen — make the object they're looking at the grammatical subject of the stop, not just a look-cue add-on. (Method Writing, *How to Write an Audio Guide Tour Script* — GOOD-TOURS-RESEARCH §e.2)

## RHYTHM-FOR-THE-EAR

- **Write for the ear: short sentences, one idea each (~12–18 words), said in a single breath.** "Sentences should be short, usually 12 to 18 words, with one idea each." (look2innovate; MediaHelpingMedia, *How to Write a Radio News Script*: heard "only once")
- **Vary sentence rhythm — a fragment next to a run-on — so cadence sounds spoken, not recited.** Listen Technologies broke the rules and "spoke in sentence fragments and run-on sentences." (Listen Technologies, *Write Out Loud*; VoiceMap "Write how you speak")
- **Round numbers and prefer active voice for the ear.** "nearly two thousand people," not "1,988 people"; "police arrested the man." (MediaHelpingMedia, *How to Write a Radio News Script*)
- **Strip eye-only punctuation before TTS: parentheticals, colons, em-dash asides, nested lists.** "Avoid brackets, dashes, colons and inverted commas or parentheses." (VoiceMap, *Writing for Voice*)
- **One strong idea per stop, ruthlessly tight — "if they can see it, cut it."** "Attempting to narrate every object usually dilutes quality… Design a journey, not a catalogue." (VoiceMap tip #13; Pathoura, *Why Museums Need Audio Guides*)
- **Size the stop to the walk: ~110–170 words for a standing POI, hard-capped near 750 words / 5 min, and match audio length to travel time.** "below 750 words (5 minutes)… attention wanders if you speak for longer"; "60–90 seconds… 110–170 words." (VoiceMap, *Word Counts* and *Ingredients of a Perfect Audio Tour*; look2innovate)
- **Cap the whole tour, not just each stop: a linear walk runs ~30–40 min max, with audio time matched to travel time and no long silences.** A tour-LEVEL ceiling (distinct from the per-stop budget) keeps the walk from sprawling. (Method Writing; VoiceMap, *Ingredients of a Perfect Audio Tour* — GOOD-TOURS-RESEARCH §e.3)

## WHAT-TO-AVOID

- **No stated meaning / hollow inspirational pivot ending.** "This isn't just about tech. It's about humanity" is fake profundity; end on an earned, concrete reflection. (Charlie Fink, *The Seven Tells of AI Writing*, Forbes; Ira Glass on the real "moment of reflection")
- **No significance-inflation copulas or participle tails: "stands as a testament to," "serves as," ", underscoring its importance."** Wikipedia flags copula-swaps and trailing "-ing" significance clauses as AI tells. (Wikipedia, *Signs of AI Writing*)
- **No "not just X, but Y" contrastive framing or rule-of-three triplet stacking.** "X isn't just Y. X is Z"; "Not for advertising. Not for distribution. For AI training." (Charlie Fink, Forbes; Wikipedia, *Signs of AI Writing*)
- **No promotional puffery: "nestled," "vibrant," "in the heart of," "steeped in history," "rich tapestry."** Listed as advertisement-language tells. (Wikipedia, *Signs of AI Writing*)
- **No em-dash-heavy, written-for-the-eye prose or forced synonym-swapping ("the edifice," "this hallowed pile").** The em dash is "an instant tell for A.I. writing"; excessive word-swapping to avoid repetition is a tell. (NYT Magazine, 2025-12-03; Wikipedia, *Signs of AI Writing*)
- **No cataloguing every POI, no pointing out the obvious.** Audio guides that state "what everyone already knows" feel like "a romantic dinner via a pre-recorded voicemail." (PrivateMuseumTours; LivingMuseum, *The Death of the Audio Guide*)

## Reviewer Checklist (score each 0/1, target ≥ 8/10)

A human reviewer or the acceptance agent applies this to a real generated tour. Each point cites the principle it enforces.

1. **Hook, not label.** Does the stop open on action, a question, or a surprising claim rather than "This is X, built in Y"? (STRUCTURE/HOOK — VoiceMap #5) — ___/1
2. **Look-cue to a visible thing.** Is the walker's eye aimed at a concrete, nameable feature they can see now? (FACT-INTO-MOMENT — Rick Steves) — ___/1
3. **A human carries it.** Is there one identifiable person or human stake, not just aggregate dates/names? (FACT-INTO-MOMENT — VoiceMap #2, Tilden) — ___/1
4. **You + present tense.** Is it second person, addressing the walker, with a live present-moment frame? (VOICE — Rick Steves, VoiceMap #8) — ___/1
5. **One idea, tight.** One strong idea per stop; nothing plainly visible is over-described; within the word budget for the segment? (RHYTHM — VoiceMap #13, Pathoura) — ___/1
6. **Ear-speakable.** Short, varied sentences you can say in one breath; contractions; no eye-only punctuation? (RHYTHM — look2innovate, VoiceMap *Writing for Voice*) — ___/1
7. **Earned point, not an inflated moral.** Does it end on an *earned, concrete* reflection, image, or open thread — the payoff to the question the stop raised — and NOT on hollow inflation ("a testament to," "a reminder that")? Note: a stop with a point is the goal; the failure is an *unearned/inflated* one, OR a flat pointless fact-list with no reflection at all — both fail. (Ira Glass: a story needs its "moment of reflection"; the tell is inflation, not having a point — GOOD-TOURS-RESEARCH §e.1; WHAT-TO-AVOID — Fink, Tilden) — ___/1
8. **No machine signatures.** Free of "not just X but Y," triplet stacking, puffery ("nestled/vibrant/in the heart of"), significance-participle tails? (WHAT-TO-AVOID — Fink, Wikipedia, NYT) — ___/1
9. **Insider / non-obvious.** Does it reveal something a generic guidebook wouldn't, rather than the headline everyone knows? (STRUCTURE/HOOK — VoiceMap #10, PrivateMuseumTours) — ___/1
10. **Momentum + peak.** Does the tour build to a climax and do stops pull forward (a thread/cliffhanger), rather than resetting flat at each POI? (STRUCTURE/HOOK — VoiceMap #11–12, Tilden 5) — ___/1

**Kill-switch (any one = automatic fail regardless of score):** the stop is a monotone fact-list with no person, no look-cue, and past-tense only — the "reading Wikipedia aloud" mode our testers flagged. (LivingMuseum, PrivateMuseumTours, Autio) — the very failure this rubric exists to design against.
