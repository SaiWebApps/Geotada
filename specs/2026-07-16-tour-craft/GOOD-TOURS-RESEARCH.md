# Good Tours Research — What a Genuinely Good Audio Tour Stop Sounds Like

**Purpose.** Ground Ondoway's tour-craft rubric in what recognized audio-tour and
interpretation professionals actually do, so we know a good dwell-stop opener from
sources — not invention. Cross-referenced against `RUBRIC.md` in this folder.

**Method.** All findings below were gathered live via WebFetch (2026-07-17) from the
real published pages cited. Every craft claim carries the URL it came from. Verbatim
excerpts are kept short (each under 15 words), quoted, and attributed, one per source.

**One honesty caveat up front.** The Rick Steves excerpt comes from a *community*
transcript repo that itself disclaims "transcription imperfections"
(https://github.com/chauduyphanvu/rick-steves-audio-tour-transcripts). Treat its
exact wording as approximate; the *pattern* it illustrates (named-feature look-cue,
second-person present) is what matters and is corroborated by the official tour pages.

## (c) The great dwell-stop opener — distilled pattern (do / don't)

A dwell stop is where the walker has *stopped and is looking at a thing*. The pros
converge on a tight recipe. Each item below is traceable to a source above.

**1. Open on a hook, not a label.**
- DO: open on an action, a question, a surprising claim, or a vivid image
  (VoiceMap: open at the moment something is happening;
  https://docs.voicemap.me/tour-publishers/writing-scripts-that-make-listeners-feel-something/).
- DON'T: open with the definitional label — "X is a [square/church/statue] in [borough],
  built in [year]." That is an encyclopedia entry, the exact failure Tilden calls the
  difference between information and interpretation
  (https://www.heritagedestination.com/hdc-library---tildens-tips/).

**2. Put the walker in the scene — second person, present tense.**
- DO: "You're standing…", "Look up…", "Notice how…" — the walker is the protagonist,
  standing there now (Rick Steves; Method Writing's "You're now standing in front of…";
  https://method-writing.com/how-to-write-an-audio-guide-tour-script/).
- DON'T: narrate the place in detached third-person past ("It was constructed…").

**3. Aim the eye at a specific, NAMED, visible feature — and prefer that feature as the stop's subject.**
- DO: name the thing they can see ("that big round window," "the 200-foot bell towers")
  and hang the stop on it. Method Writing: an object that can be seen usually beats a
  story about something that can't
  (https://method-writing.com/how-to-write-an-audio-guide-tour-script/).
- DON'T: say "look to your left," and DON'T over-describe what is plainly in front of them —
  "If they can see it, cut it" (Darbyshire).

**4. Carry it with ONE person, not the crowd or the statistics.**
- DO: tell one identifiable person's story — the victor, villain, victim, or the builder;
  describe everyone else by their relation to that one (VoiceMap, identifiable-victim
  effect). Detour's whole pitch: "the people who made it happen"
  (https://www.producthunt.com/products/detour).
- DON'T: recite aggregate history ("thousands of worshippers over the centuries…").

**5. Weave facts in as the material of a moment — don't recite them as the point.**
- DO: lead with the human stake (who wanted what, what was at risk, what changed) and
  let names/dates hang off that spine. NPS: forge a connection to *meaning*, not deliver
  facts (https://nairegion9.com/about/).
- DON'T: front-load the Wikipedia label. Tilden: "Information does not equal interpretation."

**6. Handle dates & numbers for the ear.**
- DO: round and humanize — "nearly two thousand people," "two-hundred-foot towers,"
  "about four hundred years ago." Give a number a stake before you give the number
  (Rick Steves' spoken style; MediaHelpingMedia radio-script guidance, cited in RUBRIC.md).
- DON'T: read precise figures or full dates aloud ("constructed 1163–1345"); they don't
  survive one listen.

**7. Give it momentum, and end on a small earned point — not a stated moral.**
- DO: structure like Ira Glass's anecdote — a sequence that keeps a question alive ("a
  train that has some destination"), then a brief moment of reflection that pays it off
  (https://www.natelistrom.com/2022/12/07/anecdote-reflection.html). A light forward pull
  or cliffhanger into the next stop keeps the walk a page-turner (Darbyshire).
- DON'T: end on hollow inflation — "stands as a testament to…," "a reminder that…," "this
  isn't just about X, it's about Y." Those are AI/promotional tells
  (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing).

**8. Length: size the stop to the stand, and the tour to the walk.**
- DO: keep a standing dwell stop short (~60–90 seconds / ~110–170 words per RUBRIC.md's
  cited figures); match audio time to travel time; don't hold the walker for more than a
  few minutes (VoiceMap ingredients;
  https://docs.voicemap.me/tour-publishers/the-ingredients-of-a-perfect-audio-tour/). Keep
  the whole linear tour tight — Method Writing suggests ~30–40 min max
  (https://method-writing.com/how-to-write-an-audio-guide-tour-script/).
- DON'T: narrate every object; "design a journey, not a catalogue."

## (d) What this means for Ondoway's opener fix

Our known failure: dwell stops that open by reciting the definitional label — the live
example being a stop that begins **"X is a public square in the City of Westminster."**
Everything the professionals say identifies this precisely as the wrong move:

- It is *information, not interpretation* — Tilden's core distinction. The label answers
  "what is this" (which the walker can mostly see) instead of provoking "why should I
  care" (https://www.heritagedestination.com/hdc-library---tildens-tips/).
- It violates the single most repeated pro rule: **open at the moment something is
  happening, not with a description** (VoiceMap). "Public square in the City of
  Westminster" is a gazetteer entry, not a hook.
- It fails the NPS test — a stop should *forge a connection between the walker's interest
  and the meaning of the place*, not deliver its administrative classification
  (https://nairegion9.com/about/).
- It breaks "If they can see it, cut it" — the walker is *standing in the square*; telling
  them it is a square, in Westminster, is describing the visible (Darbyshire).

**The fix pattern, concretely.** For a dwell stop, forbid the copula-label opener
("X is a [type] in [place], [built/known for] Y") as the first sentence. Instead require
the opener to do at least one of: (a) a named look-cue at a visible feature the walker
should find ("Find the lamppost with the crown on top — the oldest one here"), (b) a
question or surprising claim that reframes what they're looking at, or (c) drop them into
one person's moment on this spot. The label facts (name, type, borough, date) may still
appear — but *later, rounded, and hung off the human stake*, never as the first breath.
This is exactly the "hook, not label" line the rubric already draws; the fix is to make it
a hard gate on sentence #1 of every dwell stop, since that is where the recital lives.

## (e) Cross-reference vs `RUBRIC.md` — where we match the pros, where we're thin

**Already solid and confirmed by the primary sources (no change needed):**

- *Hook not label; open on action* — matches VoiceMap and Tilden exactly (RUBRIC STRUCTURE/HOOK).
- *Look-cue to a specific visible feature, not "on your left"* — matches Rick Steves and
  Method Writing (RUBRIC FACT-INTO-MOMENT).
- *One identifiable person carries it* — matches VoiceMap (identifiable-victim) and Detour's
  "the people who made it happen" (RUBRIC FACT-INTO-MOMENT).
- *Second person + present tense; subjective narrator* — matches Rick Steves, VoiceMap,
  Method Writing (RUBRIC VOICE).
- *Facts are material not the point; provoke don't instruct* — matches Tilden and NPS
  verbatim (RUBRIC FACT-INTO-MOMENT).
- *Write for the ear; short varied sentences; round numbers; strip eye-only punctuation* —
  matches Darbyshire, look2innovate, radio-script guidance (RUBRIC RHYTHM).
- *Avoid AI/promotional tells* — matches Wikipedia *Signs of AI Writing* verbatim (RUBRIC WHAT-TO-AVOID).
- *Momentum + peak + cliffhanger* — matches Darbyshire's "work backwards from a climactic
  location" and Ira Glass's anecdote-momentum (RUBRIC checklist #10).

**Genuinely missing or under-weighted (recommend adding):**

1. **Reflection is required, not banned — the rubric over-corrects.** Checklist item #7
   ("No stated moral") and the kill-switch are aimed at hollow inflation, which is right.
   But Ira Glass is explicit that a story *needs* a moment of reflection — the point,
   the payoff on the question the anecdote raised
   (https://www.natelistrom.com/2022/12/07/anecdote-reflection.html). The failure isn't
   *having* a point; it's an *unearned/inflated* one ("a testament to…"). Recommend
   rewording #7 to "ends on an *earned, concrete* point or open thread — not an inflated
   stated moral," so we don't push the model toward flat, pointless fact-lists (the
   opposite failure). This nuance is currently absent.

2. **"Prefer a visible object as the stop's SUBJECT," not just as a look-cue add-on.**
   Method Writing's rule — a seeable object usually beats an unseeable abstraction — is
   sharper than our look-cue line. Our rubric aims the eye at a visible feature, but
   doesn't say the *subject* of a dwell stop should default to the thing in front of the
   walker. Worth making explicit (https://method-writing.com/how-to-write-an-audio-guide-tour-script/).

3. **Whole-tour duration ceiling, not just per-stop budget.** The rubric caps per-stop
   words (~110–170) and 750-word/5-min stops, but not the *total* walk. Method Writing's
   ~30–40 min linear-tour ceiling and VoiceMap's "match audio to travel time / no long
   silences" are tour-level constraints we don't currently encode
   (https://docs.voicemap.me/tour-publishers/the-ingredients-of-a-perfect-audio-tour/).

4. **Navigational glue as a first-class element (izi.TRAVEL).** izi.TRAVEL treats the
   between-stops "navigational stories" as part of the design, planned before writing
   (https://izi.travel/en/help/production/creating-a-city-audio-tour-main-steps). Our
   rubric is stop-centric; the walk *between* stops (already a known Ondoway concern —
   the walk-past vignette / nav-glue work) is under-represented as a craft surface here.

**Net:** the rubric is well-grounded and matches the professionals on ~9 of its core
lines. The one substantive correction is the reflection nuance (#1) — the rest are
sharpenings (#2) and additions at the tour level (#3, #4).
