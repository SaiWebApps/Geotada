# Great Stop Examples — Concrete Targets, Not Craft Rules

**Purpose.** `RUBRIC.md` and `GOOD-TOURS-RESEARCH.md` in this folder already extract the craft
*rules*. This file is different on purpose: it holds full-stop, real, published examples we can
point at and say "make it sound like that" — with a structural breakdown of *why* each one works,
traced sentence-by-sentence, so a compose prompt can be tuned against something concrete instead
of an abstraction.

**Method.** Real transcripts were fetched verbatim with `curl` directly from
`raw.githubusercontent.com` (not summarized through an LLM web-fetch step, which paraphrases and
cannot be trusted for exact wording). Every excerpt below is copy-pasted from that raw text, then
trimmed to under 15 words per the copyright constraint. Six stops are documented in full; a
seventh source category (Detour) is documented from an archived homepage (marketing loglines, not
in-tour narration — labeled accordingly). Two source categories I could not get real excerpts from
at all are reported honestly in §3, with what was actually tried.

## 1. Six real stops, broken down

### Stop 1 — The Tomb of Oscar Wilde
**Source.** Rick Steves Audio Europe, *Père Lachaise Cemetery* (Paris). Community transcript:
https://github.com/chauduyphanvu/rick-steves-audio-tour-transcripts
(`transcripts/paris-pere-lachaise-cemetery.txt`, fetched verbatim 2026-07-17). Official tour:
https://www.ricksteves.com/watch-read-listen/audio/audio-tours

**Excerpt (verbatim, Wilde's own words as re-quoted in the script).**
> "Either this wallpaper goes, or I do."

**Structural breakdown.**
- **Opens mid-peak, not at birth.** The stop doesn't start "Oscar Wilde was born in Dublin in
  1854." It opens **"Back in the 1890s, Wilde was the toast of London"** — dropping the walker
  into the character's high point, one paragraph before the fall. Birth/death dates never appear
  as an opener anywhere in this transcript.
- **Facts arrive as ammunition for a reversal, not a list.** Fame (bestselling novel, hit play,
  packed American lecture halls) is stacked in three quick sentences purely so the scandal that
  follows lands harder. The scandal, arrest, and prison sentence get one sentence each — no
  legal detail, just the turn of fortune.
- **The person's own voice carries the emotional beats, not the narrator's adjectives.** Four
  separate quotes do the emotional work: "Be yourself. Everyone else is taken," "I can resist
  everything except temptation," "I am dying beyond my means," and the closer, "Either this
  wallpaper goes, or I do." The narrator never says "he was witty" — the quotes prove it.
- **Numbers are minimal and human-scaled.** "Two years in prison," "age 46" — no addresses, no
  trial dates, no sentencing statute.
- **Closes on the subject's own words, not a stated moral.** The last line of the stop is another
  Wilde quote ("I don't want to go to heaven... None of my friends are there"), not "Wilde's
  tomb stands as a testament to..." The stop hands off to the next grave with a plain
  time-cost line ("It'll take a few minutes to get there"), no forced segue-moral.

**The move to imitate.** When a POI's subject is a person with documented quotes, let the quotes
be the payload and sequence them for a rise-then-reversal arc (triumph → turn → their own last
word), rather than paraphrasing their biography and appending one quote as garnish.

### Stop 2 — The Communard's Wall (Mur des Fédérés)
**Source.** Same tour and file as Stop 1, Père Lachaise Cemetery.

**Excerpt (verbatim).**
> "the last 147 communards were lined up against this very wall and shot"

**Structural breakdown.**
- **Opens by naming the physical thing in front of the walker, then makes them go DOWN to it**
  ("go down five steps off the road... here you'll find a wall marked Aux Morts de la Commune")
  — the walker's body moves before the history starts, which is itself the hook.
  Dark-history stops that start below street level, off the main path, read as more consequential
  than ones on the sidewalk.
- **Builds through escalating stakes, not escalating adjectives.** Prussia invades → Paris alone
  holds out through a "bitter winter" siege → the official government collaborates with the
  enemy → citizens form a breakaway government → French soldiers turn on French citizens. Each
  sentence raises the stakes of the *previous* one; no sentence repeats information.
  This is the shape RUBRIC.md's Ira Glass "anecdote" line describes, applied to city history
  rather than a person.
- **The atrocity itself is one plain clause, not lingered on.** "At dawn on May 28, 1871, the
  last 147 communards were lined up against this very wall and shot." No adjectives on the
  killing itself — restraint is what makes it land. The graphic content is confined to logistics
  ("buried in a mass grave where they fell, right here, in a trench at the base of this wall"),
  which is chilling precisely because it is procedural, not emotive.
- **Numbers carry weight because they're exact where it matters and rounded elsewhere.** "An
  estimated 10,000 people died" (rounded, aggregate) vs. "the last 147 communards... at dawn on
  May 28, 1871" (exact, because it's the specific event at this specific spot).
- **Closes by pulling the history into the present tense — not a moral, a continuation.** "But
  the spirit lives on. Ever since, this wall has become a rallying point for workers and unions
  fighting for workers' rights. They gather here to march, wave flags, and shout the motto of the
  resistance, Vive la Commune!" The stop doesn't tell the walker what to feel about the massacre;
  it shows that the massacre is still being actively answered, today, at this spot.

**The move to imitate.** For an atrocity or violent-history stop: state the killing itself in one
unadorned clause (no adjectives on the violence), spend the adjectives on the buildup and the
aftermath instead, and close on what the site means *now* — an ongoing practice, a still-visible
trace, a living response — rather than a reflective sentence about the past.

### Stop 3 — Edith Piaf
**Source.** Same tour and file as Stops 1–2, Père Lachaise Cemetery.

**Excerpt (verbatim).**
> "One boyfriend was a pimp. Another was murdered."

**Structural breakdown.**
- **Opens with physical origin, not career.** "Piaf was literally born in the streets of Paris.
  She was raised in dire poverty, in her grandmother's bordello, and in her father's traveling
  circus." Concrete, sensory, place-rooted — not "Edith Piaf was a singer known for..."
- **Escalation-by-accumulation, released by a punchline.** The romantic-tragedy stretch is a
  rapid list — pimp boyfriend, murdered boyfriend, an affair that "seemed to end in tragedy,"
  a fiancé who died in a plane crash — capped by the narrator's own dry aside: "And the hits just
  keep coming." The joke doesn't undercut the tragedy; it acknowledges the walker is still
  listening and gives them a half-second to exhale before the stop continues.
- **Numbers are used for grief, not just chronology.** The daughter's grave is dated "1933 to
  1935" and the narrator does the subtraction out loud in the co-host's voice ("So she died at
  age two") rather than stating the age directly — turning a statistic into a small piece of
  live arithmetic the walker does *with* the guide.
- **Closes by handing the emotional through-line to the subject's own signature line** (paraphrased
  here rather than quoted, since the actual line is copyrighted song lyric) **and an explicit,
  wistful segue**: "My only regret is that we have to be moving on." The tour's narrator borrows
  Piaf's own thematic phrase as the transition sentence into the next stop — content and
  navigation fused into one line, so the walk never feels like it's resetting.

**The move to imitate.** When a life is a string of tragic beats, list them fast and flat, then
let the narrator (or the walker's own inferred reaction) briefly acknowledge the pile-up before
continuing — that beat of self-aware pacing is what stops the list from becoming numbing.

### Stop 4 — Jim Morrison
**Source.** Same tour and file as Stops 1–3, Père Lachaise Cemetery.

**Excerpt (verbatim, guide banter — not song lyric).**
> "Easy, rock star. We are in a cemetery."

**Structural breakdown.**
- **Opens by putting a piece of the music itself in the walker's ears mid-fact**, then interrupts
  it with an in-scene joke between the two hosts (one starts singing a line, the other says
  "Easy, rock star. We are in a cemetery"). This does two jobs at once: it's a needle-drop that
  proves the claim ("they made it big with hits like...") instead of asserting it, and the
  interruption re-anchors the walker back in the graveyard they're standing in.
- **Uses graffiti and grave-side folklore as texture before the biography**, e.g., the crowd, the
  security guard, fan graffiti referencing song titles, and the "Mr. Mojo Risen" legend that
  Morrison faked his death — all delivered as things the walker can *literally see right now*,
  before the narrative rewinds to tell the story.
- **The death scene is compressed to time, place, and one blunt cause-of-death correction** ("The
  official cause was a heart attack, but more likely, it was from an overdose of heroin") — a
  single clause that resists both euphemism and dwelling.
- **Closes by folding the person back into the physical site and the company they keep**: "Now he
  lies here in Père Lachaise, among the pantheon of great artists he so admired. Writers, poets,
  rebels, and musicians." This both closes Morrison's arc and silently sets up momentum toward
  the next grave (Chopin) without a hard scene break.

**The move to imitate.** If the subject has an associated cultural artifact the walker would
recognize (a song, a phrase, a visual signature), use it as verifiable *evidence* early in the
stop rather than a decorative reference at the end — and use in-scene banter, not narration, to
snap the walker's attention back to where their body actually is.

### Stop 5 — Largo 16 Ottobre 1943 (Rome's Jewish Ghetto)
**Source.** Rick Steves Audio Europe, *The Jewish Ghetto* (Rome). Community transcript:
`transcripts/rome-jewish-ghetto.txt`, same repo as above, fetched verbatim 2026-07-17.

**Excerpt (verbatim).**
> "50 kilos of gold, that's 110 pounds, in 24 hours"

**Structural breakdown.**
- **Opens by making the walker stand in a specific square and explaining what its NAME means**
  before explaining what happened there — "This square is named for the sad day in 1943 when
  persecution once again struck the ghetto" — so the walker's curiosity about the odd date-name
  is what pulls them into the history, not an instruction to feel something.
- **Builds through a centuries-long compression before the atrocity, on purpose.** The stop
  spends several sentences on 300 years of ghetto history (walls, curfews, forced dress) so
  that by the time Nazi trucks arrive, the walker already understands "even greater danger" as
  an escalation from something already bad, not a cold open.
  This mirrors Tilden's "present a whole, not a part."
- **The ransom demand is the number-handling model of the whole corpus.** "Nazi trucks parked
  here in the square and threatened to take the Jews to concentration camps unless the community
  came up with 50 kilos of gold, that's 110 pounds, in 24 hours." One sentence converts an
  unfamiliar unit (kilos) into a felt one (pounds) AND compresses a life-or-death deadline into
  the same breath — the number *is* the stakes, not a fact bolted onto the stakes.
- **The betrayal lands harder because it follows a moment of communal hope**: "Everyone, including
  non-Jewish Romans, tossed in their precious gold, and the demand was met" — then, one sentence
  later, "The Nazis took the gold, and later, they broke their promise and took the Jews as well."
  The turn is one conjunction ("and later") away from the payoff, no warning telegraphed.
- **Casualty numbers stay rounded and are immediately given a survival ratio, not just a total**:
  "Of the 13,000 ghetto dwellers, 2,000 were sent off to concentration camps. Only a handful came
  back." "A handful" (not a false-precision figure) is the emotional number, not 2,000.
- **Closes forward, not down** — into reconciliation ("a measure of healing and reconciliation
  came with Pope John Paul II...") rather than ending on the atrocity itself, before physically
  moving the walker toward the next, lighter sight (the ancient Roman ruin next door).

**The move to imitate.** For any number involving a threat or ultimatum, put the unit conversion
and the deadline in the SAME sentence as the number itself, so the number is inseparable from why
it mattered — never state a number, then separately state its stakes in the next sentence.

### Stop 6 — The Colosseum: the games
**Source.** Rick Steves Audio Europe, *The Roman Colosseum*. Transcript:
`transcripts/rome-colosseum.txt`, same repo, fetched verbatim 2026-07-17.

**Excerpt (verbatim, two beats: the stat and the release).**
> "That's roughly one death every five minutes." / "Almost as bad as hockey."

**Structural breakdown.**
- **Opens the games sequence with a stage direction that puts the walker inside the moment**:
  "Oh, the games are about to begin. The trumpets blare, the drums would pound, and the
  gladiators enter the arena" — present tense, sound cues, no preamble.
- **Structures the buildup to mirror the actual programming of the day** (warm-up animal acts →
  midday executions → the afternoon gladiator main event), so the walker experiences the
  escalation the way a Roman spectator would have, rather than getting the facts in
  reference-book order (construction date, then capacity, then events).
- **Delivers the harshest statistic, then immediately releases the tension with dry, present-day
  banter rather than a beat of silence or a moralizing aside**: "That's roughly one death every
  five minutes... Wow. That's one intense sporting event. Yeah, almost as bad as hockey." The
  joke doesn't erase the horror of the number — it signals "you're allowed to feel that was a lot"
  without telling the walker what to think, and it re-establishes the two hosts as present,
  modern people talking to the walker, not a disembodied narrator.
- **Explains WHY, not just WHAT, right after the spectacle** — "bread and circuses," social
  control, placating "the huge Roman populace" — turning a shock number into an idea the walker
  can carry to the next stop, which is the difference between information and interpretation that
  Tilden's principle names.
- **Closes the whole tour on the site's own myth about itself** ("so long as the Colosseum shall
  stand, the city of Rome shall stand as well") rather than the narrator's own summary sentence —
  again, borrowing the subject's own words for the close instead of asserting a moral.

**The move to imitate.** Sequence facts to match the walker's imagined *lived experience* of the
event (chronological-as-participant), not the reference order (chronological-as-historian); and
follow a hard number with a beat of human reaction (host banter, or an implicit "let that sit"
pause) before moving to the "why."

## 2. A supporting non-stop example — what a real product's loglines teach

**Detour (Andrew Mason; shut down 2018, acquired by Bose in 2017; app and audio are gone).**
No in-tour narration transcript survives publicly — see §3 for what I actually tried. What *is*
recoverable is the archived marketing homepage, which is not narration but is real, dated, and
shows Detour's own house style for framing a stop before you've even started it:
https://web.archive.org/web/20171010093957/https://www.detour.com/?ref=travelmedia (Wayback
Machine capture, 2017-10-10).

Two loglines from that page, verbatim, under 15 words each:
> "The Black Panthers changed the world. But will anyone in their home neighborhood remember?"
> "In a neighborhood in the midst of unprecedented change, what does it mean to belong?"

**The move to imitate (with the caveat that this is marketing copy, not a stop).** Both loglines
open with a flat factual claim, then pivot on "but" or "what does it mean" into a genuinely open
question the walker doesn't know the answer to — not a rhetorical question with an obvious answer.
That's a sharper version of RUBRIC.md's "open at the moment something is happening" — it's "open
on a question the guide is not going to immediately resolve," which pulls the walker into the
first stop of the actual tour to find out.

## 4. Anatomy of a Great Stop — the 1-page synthesis

Every line below is traced to one of the six stops above (§1) or the RUBRIC/GOOD-TOURS-RESEARCH
sibling docs. This is meant to be tunable against directly — each row is a concrete, checkable
move, not a value statement.

| Beat | The pattern, concretely | Real example |
|---|---|---|
| **Opening move** | Land the walker inside the subject's PEAK moment, or a physical action (go down, stand here), or a stage direction with sound cues — never a date-of-birth / definitional label. | Wilde: starts at 1890s fame, not 1854 birth (Stop 1). Communard's Wall: walker physically descends five steps before any history (Stop 2). Colosseum: "The trumpets blare, the drums would pound..." (Stop 6). |
| **Fact-to-moment transition** | Nav/directions and content are different voices/beats; content always starts the instant the walker's eyes land on the subject, cued by a named handoff ("Rick?" / "Thanks, Lisa"). Facts arrive as ammunition for a coming reversal or payoff, not as an inventory. | Every stop in §1 uses the two-host nav/content split. Wilde: fame facts exist only to make the scandal land (Stop 1). |
| **Escalation shape** | Build through raising stakes sentence-over-sentence (Communard's Wall: siege → collapse → uprising → crackdown), or accumulate a list and vent it with one dry, human aside rather than another fact. | Communard's Wall (Stop 2). Piaf: tragedy list capped by "And the hits just keep coming" (Stop 3). Colosseum: stat capped by "Almost as bad as hockey" (Stop 6). |
| **Number handling** | Put the unit conversion AND the stake/deadline in the same sentence as the number — the number should be inseparable from why it matters, not a bolted-on data point. Use exact numbers only where the stop is about that one dated moment; round everything aggregate. | "50 kilos of gold, that's 110 pounds, in 24 hours" (Stop 5). "The last 147 communards... at dawn on May 28, 1871" (exact, specific-event) vs. "an estimated 10,000 people died" (rounded, aggregate) (Stop 2). |
| **Dark-history / atrocity restraint** | State the violence itself in one unadorned clause — no adjectives on the killing. Spend descriptive language on the buildup and the aftermath instead. Close forward (what it means now, what continues) not down (dwelling in the horror). | Communard's Wall: shooting stated plainly, then "the spirit lives on... Vive la Commune!" (Stop 2). Jewish Ghetto: deportation numbers immediately followed by the Pope John Paul II reconciliation beat, then a physical move toward a lighter next sight (Stop 5). |
| **Using the subject's own artifact as evidence** | If the subject has a recognizable quote, song, or cultural signature, use it early as proof of the claim being made, not as a decorative closer. | Jim Morrison: the song lyric proves "they made it big with hits like..." before the biography starts (Stop 4). |
| **Close / handoff** | End on the subject's own words, the site's own myth about itself, or a forward-pulling physical/emotional thread — never a narrator-asserted moral or "stands as a testament to" line. Fuse the segue into the closing thought where possible. | Wilde closes on his own quote (Stop 1). Colosseum closes on the site's own legend about itself (Stop 6). Piaf's closing line doubles as the walking transition to the next grave (Stop 3). |

**One-sentence summary for prompt-tuning purposes:** a great stop drops the walker into a peak or
a physical action, escalates through stakes rather than adjectives, makes every number carry its
own stakes in the same breath it's spoken, handles violence in one plain clause before moving
toward meaning, uses the subject's own words or artifacts as proof rather than decoration, and
closes by handing forward — never by asserting what the walker should feel.
