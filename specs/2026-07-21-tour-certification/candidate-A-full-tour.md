## 1. Pont Neuf

Settle in. You're standing on Pont Neuf, and the name is the first little joke this city plays on a newcomer. Pont Neuf means New Bridge, and yet it's the oldest bridge still standing in Paris. Twelve arches of stone, and the first bridge here built without the usual medieval clutter of houses piled on top — radical enough in its day to earn the name New. The king who built it, Henri IV, was one of Paris's first great town-planners, and he came to inspect the work so often that one day he delighted the masons by taking a flying leap over an unfinished arch. Those semicircular bays along the sides once held market stalls, worked by teeth-pullers, quack doctors and other merchants hawking to the crowd. Halfway across, Henri IV still rides in bronze. But it's not the original — revolutionaries destroyed that first statue in 1792, and only when the Bourbons returned did the city recast it, in 1818, paid for by public subscription. The bronze came from an unlikely donor, a melted-down statue of the general Louis Charles Antoine Desaix, poured into a fresh cast taken from a surviving mould of the original. And before the sculptor François-Frédéric Lemot sealed the new horse and rider, he tucked four boxes inside. Inside are a history of Henri IV's life, a 17th-century parchment certifying the old statue, a document on how the new one was commissioned, and a list of every citizen who'd chipped in. Now picture this bridge caught by an early camera. Louis Daguerre's 1838 shot of the Boulevard du Temple is usually called the first photograph to show a human being. But two years earlier, in 1836 or 1837, Daguerre was running outdoor tests of his new technique, and he aimed one at this very bridge, capturing the arches and the equestrian statue of Henri IV. Down in the lower-left, in the statue's shadow, a figure — maybe two — lies slumped against the fence, what looks like a worker. If the dating holds, that blur may be the first human ever photographed. Lean toward the parapet and you'll find faces — three hundred and eighty-one carved stone heads, no two alike. They're forest and field gods from old mythology, satyrs and sylvains, copies of originals by the Renaissance sculptor Germain Pilon, the man who carved the tomb of Henry II and Catherine de' Medici at Saint-Denis. Pilon's originals lasted until the bridge was completely rebuilt between 1851 and 1854. Six went to the Musée Carnavalet, eight to the Cluny, and they've since moved to the Musée National de la Renaissance at Château d'Écouen. The copies you're looking at came from Hippolyte Maindron, Hubert Lavigne, Antoine-Louis Barye and Fontenelle — Fontenelle alone carved sixty-one, all on the upstream side between the right bank and the Île de la Cité. Just off the bridge lies the Place Dauphine, and once a year, on the Petite Fête-Dieu — the 'Small Corpus Christi', eight days after the main feast — young painters hung their canvases here from six in the morning until noon. It was an unofficial salon, the one chance for an unknown to be spotted, a forerunner of the Salon des Refusés that would later spark the Impressionists. In 1720, a twenty-two-year-old — the son of the man who kept the king's billiard tables — showed an antique bas-relief here. The painter J.-B. Vanloo stopped, stared at it a long while, saw real quality, bought it, and took the young man under his wing. Eight years on, that unknown was Vanloo's colleague at the Academy of Painting — his name, Jean-Baptiste-Siméon Chardin. See those steps beside the statue? They drop down to the square du Vert-Galant, where Jacques de Molay, Grand Master of the Templars, was burned at the stake, cursing the kings of France as the flames took him.

## 2. Square du Vert-Galant

Leaving Pont Neuf behind, head for Square du Vert-Galant, just ahead. Take the steps down behind Henri IV's equestrian statue on the Pont-Neuf, and you drop into a tranquil, tree-lined garden at the very tip of the island, tucked into its triangular stern. Its name, Square du Vert-Galant, honours the king up on that horse — the Green Gallant, a nickname for a lusty gentleman that his legendary love life earned him young. But this ground is far older than any king, because it's the original settlement of the Parisii, the Celtic tribe Caesar subdued, and the Romans called it Lutetia — in 358 AD the emperor Julian called it Cara Lutetia, "my beloved Lutetia." Henri reigned from 1589 to 1610, and he wanted to make Paris beautiful and tranquil, nothing less than a wonder of the world. He delivered — the Pont Neuf you just crossed, the oldest bridge in Paris despite its name, plus the Place Dauphine facing it and the Place Royale, later renamed the Place des Vosges. The love life won Parisians' hearts at first — two wives, at least fifty-six mistresses, and households full of bastard children. But the charm faded as he aged, and the dozens of bastards scattered through the kingdom bred a resentment that nearly tipped France back into civil war. Worse, he meant to marry his favourite, Gabrielle d'Estrées, an outrage to the raison d'état that only her early death prevented. As one Femme Ladoucette wrote to the Queen, in the kingdom of France one separated the sheep from the goats. Yet above all this restless lover was a peacemaker. Raised a strict Protestant, he converted to Catholicism, the faith of the majority, to end the long religious wars that had left Paris starving and looking like a bomb site. Then in 1598 he drafted and signed the Edict of Nantes, writing tolerance for the very religion he'd renounced onto the books. The fanatical Catholic who stabbed him to death, Francis Ravaillac, was not persuaded. Even so, Henri — who called kindness and mercy the first virtues of a prince — still stands among France's heroes, beside Charlemagne, Joan of Arc, and Saint-Louis IX. Find the tree-dotted knoll at the extreme point of the island, the prime spot to stand, and look out. The Hôtel de la Monnaie and the dome of the Institut de France sit to your left, the Louvre to your right, the Pont-des-Arts straight ahead — and the river boats dock just off the north side of the square. By night it's an enchantment.

## 3. Conciergerie

From the Square du Vert-Galant, carry on toward the Conciergerie — about a nine-minute walk. You just left the tip of the island, and it was there that Jacques de Molay, Grand Master of the Templars, was burnt at the stake — cursing the kings of France as the flames took him. In the fifteenth century, if you'd walked past this building, you could have heard them: the screams of prisoners being tortured inside the Tour Bonbec, the tower at the western end. When crews restored that tower in 1828, they found what lay beneath — two hidden dungeons spiked with iron points and connected straight to the river. You can guess the fate of the prisoners dropped into them. The tower's name comes from bon-bec, medieval French for a prattler — a grim nod to the interrogations on the ground floor, where a whole spectrum of tortures was used to squeeze out confessions and denunciations. This is Paris's oldest prison, one of the last surviving pieces of the medieval Palais de Justice. Step inside and the vaulted Gothic halls open up, among them the Salle des Gens d'Armes, raised between 1301 and 1315. At the far end, an iron grille penned off the pailleux — prisoners too poor to bribe a guard for a cell, left to sleep on the straw, the paille, that gave them their name. Past that runs a corridor where prisoners could wander freely. And among the reconstructed rooms is the salle de toilette, where the condemned had their hair cropped and their shirt collars ripped away, the neck bared for the guillotine. The Revolution packed these cells with the famous and the doomed. Madame Roland had backed the Revolution, then sided with the Girondins at exactly the wrong moment. Marie-Antoinette sat in a dreary cell from the 2nd of August to the 16th of October 1793, before they took her to the guillotine on the Place de la Révolution — what you'd now call the Place de la Concorde. Then came the men who had sent others here — Danton, Robespierre, and Saint-Just, one after another, Danton and Robespierre in the cells right beside the Queen's. Upstairs, a mock-up of her cell survives, the condemned queen's crucifix hanging forlornly against peeling fleur-de-lys wallpaper. You come for the history, but the Gothic halls and the great kitchen fireplaces are worth the visit for their architecture alone. North of the halls ran rue de Paris, the passage down to the dark cells — named for the Great Executioner, Monsieur de Paris. For seven generations, from 1688 to 1847, one family held that title and handed it down father to son: the Sansons. Just before the Revolution, Louis XVI set his heart on improving the executioners' lot and granted Charles-Henri Sanson a yearly fee of 16,000 pounds, plus 2,000 crowns to keep up the scaffold and pillory — never suspecting the same man would one day be his own executioner. Charles-Henri was a royalist, and he had no wish to do it. On the 15th of August 1792 he was arrested as "the despot's employee," and for each execution he was marched from his cell to the Place du Carrousel, where the guillotine then stood, and marched back afterward. At first the executions were few — but once the blade was working at full swing and his expert hand couldn't be spared, they released him. He retired soon after he had executed his own sovereign. His son Henri carried the post through the Terror and the shaky regimes that followed, all the way to 1840, deep into the reign of Louis-Philippe. Here's the strange coda. The filmmaker Jean Renoir liked to marvel that his own grandfather — father of the painter Renoir, a tailor from Limoges — had known Henri Sanson's chief assistant during the Terror. That old assistant, Renoir wrote, "may have passed Voltaire and Benjamin Franklin in the street, heard Mozart play the harpsichord, and attended performances of Monsieur Beaumarchais." The tailor met him in 1845, the year the family moved up from Limoges to Paris, when the last Sanson still held the post. The two became friends — the tailor and the executioner's assistant — bound by the pleasure of doing a good job. "After all," Jean Renoir observed, "they were two good workmen: one cut out cloth, the other cut off heads just as conscientiously."

## 4. Sainte-Chapelle

From the Conciergerie, it's about a four-minute walk on to Sainte-Chapelle. Picture an emperor so short of cash he pawns his holiest treasure. That was Baudouin II, the last emperor of Constantinople, desperate for funds to defend his city — and Saint Louis bought the piece of the Holy Cross and the Crown of Thorns he was selling, then built this chapel to house them. The relics reached Paris in August 1239, carried all the way from Venice by two Dominican friars. Louis threw a week-long reception to welcome them. For the final stretch he carried them himself, barefoot and dressed as a penitent — a scene you can still find in the Relics of the Passion window on the south side. And the price tells you everything about his priorities. The relics cost 135,000 livres, the silver Grand-Chasse built to hold them another 100,000 — while the chapel itself, all of this, came to just 40,000. The brief handed to the architects — Pierre de Montreuil, or perhaps Jean de Chelles — was an odd one: make a building with the light, lacy look of a reliquary. It took barely five years, and the result went stunningly beyond what anyone expected. There are actually two chapels stacked here. The plain lower one was for the servants; the upper, reached by a spiral staircase, was reserved for the court. Step into that upper chapel and the walls all but vanish into stained glass, and the stone holding it up is there, but the medieval builders split those powerful supports into clusters of pencil-thin columns so they'd look delicate, almost fragile. When the sun pours through, blues and reds scatter across the interior like a swarm of bright butterflies. That glass runs to 1,113 panels, two-thirds of them original and the rest from a nineteenth-century restoration. Together they tell almost the whole Bible: Genesis on the north wall, the Passion of Christ at the east end, the story of these very relics on the south, and the Apocalypse blazing in the rose window. The men who made all this glass between 1242 and 1248 left no names behind. But the art historian Louis Grodecki teased three separate workshops out of the styles. The first did the apse and most of the north wall — supple forms, simplified faces. The second, the Master of the Ezekiel Window, handled the Ezekiel, Daniel and Kings windows, with elongated figures and angular drapery. The third, the Master of Judith and Esther, did those two plus the window of Job — subtler faces, the feel of an illuminated manuscript. The relics themselves nearly didn't survive the Revolution. The silver-and-gold reliquary that once held the Crown of Thorns was melted down at the Mint, yet its contents — genuine or not — came through, and they're kept at Notre-Dame today. Even the standard-bearers of the Revolution, it seems, wouldn't dare touch the holiest of Christian symbols. The chapel got no such mercy. It became a flour store, then a dump for court archives, and by 1847 it was so run-down it still carried a sign reading 'National property, for sale.' Then enlightened souls stepped in — Viollet-le-Duc, Lassus and others were called to restore it, and 720 of the 1,134 original stained-glass scenes were saved. One last thing — the chapel still hosts classical concerts, and a ticket bought a day or so ahead spares you the queue.

## 5. Square Jean XXIII

Leaving Sainte-Chapelle behind, head for Square Jean XXIII, about a 13-minute walk away. You've just come from the chapel Louis raised over the relics of the Passion — carried from Venice to Paris in August 1239 by two Dominican friars, with the king himself walking the final stretch barefoot and dressed as a penitent. He paid 135,000 livres for the relics, and only 40,000 for the chapel built to hold them. In 1831, a riot tore down the palace of the archbishop of Paris, which had long stood on the land between Notre-Dame and the Seine. But this quartier was sacred long before any archbishop — its religious roots run back to the fourth century, when the Emperor Julian presided in a temple to Jupiter. Those Roman ruins became the foundation of the basilica of Saint-Etienne in 528, and of the very first Notre-Dame. The cathedral you see now was begun in 1163, and after centuries of renovation and the fanatical rampages of the Terror, it still stands as one of the crown jewels of Gothic architecture across all of Europe. But it's the man the square is named for who's worth lingering over. These gardens honor Pope John XXIII, born Angelo Roncalli in Lombardy in 1881, who served as the papal nuncio in Paris from 1944 to 1953. Parisians remember his long walks through the city, surrounded by friends who were Protestants, Catholics, Jews, agnostics, atheists. "Jesus came to break down the barriers," he preached; "the central point of his teaching is love." At diplomatic receptions he enjoyed the champagne and the conviviality of men and women, he read French literature, and he knew the postwar French intellectuals and theologians he would later invite to help reform the Church. A savvy diplomat, trusted by the French government, he was awarded the Legion of Honor by President Auriol, who praised him for respecting "the traditions of justice and tolerance," the honor of the French nation. That reforming instinct made him the progenitor of the Second Vatican Council of 1962 to 1965, the great opening-up of the Church he'd set in motion as the "good pope" before his death in 1963. When Rome recalled him, he confessed a deep sadness. "I love France and I love Paris," he told a friend, "and I hoped to stay a little longer. I can't really see myself in Rome." This inviting square is a fitting memorial to such a genial man. In April, flowering cherry trees bloom along the south porches, screening but never quite hiding the original medallions carved with scenes of student life. Beds of daffodils and tulips run the whole southern flank to the eastern end, where the flying buttresses climb above a small square generous with flowers, benches, and travelers taking their ease. The mood here is calmer than inside the cathedral, where you'll almost certainly get stuck behind a crush of tourists aiming cameras at the stained glass. Henry James felt that calm too — his Lambert Strether, in The Ambassadors, notes the "beneficent action on his nerves" of sitting alone in "the great dim church," a "place of refuge" with "a sense of safety, of simplification."

## 6. Notre-Dame Cathedral

From Square Jean XXIII, carry on to Notre-Dame Cathedral, about a 7-minute walk away. Notre-Dame sits at the heart of Paris, and by the west door a bronze star set in the pavement marks kilometre zero — the point every main road in France is measured from. The windswept square you're crossing is the Parvis, from the old word for paradise, laid out by Haussmann back in the 1860s. Those white paving blocks trace the medieval streets and houses that once crowded this very spot. Now look up. The facade is the showpiece here. You can still read its Romanesque roots in that solid H-shape, but the filigree of the central rose window and the gallery above lift the whole thing into lightness and grace. Beneath the rose window runs a row of 27 huge figures, the biblical kings who were the ancestors of Christ. Look closely, because these are copies — revolutionaries mistook them for French kings and tore the originals down. Over the central portal, the carving to catch is the Day of Judgement. In the lower frieze the dead scramble up from their graves, while Christ presides above, sending the saved to his right and the damned to his left. And around the arch, watch for the angels — alert and mischievous, said to be modelled on the cathedral's own choirboys. The left portal shows Mary crowned by Christ; the right seats her enthroned, with the life of her mother St Anne and the life of Christ carved below. These aren't just illustrations — the carvers packed them with meaning. In the nativity, the infant Christ lies not in a crib but on an altar, set above Mary, a quiet hint of the sacrifice to come. Step through the door and you're standing right under the bell tower. Look up at the vault and you'll spot the trapdoor the bells were hoisted through, with two massive pillars carrying all that weight overhead. Walk into the nave and the contrast hits you — deep shadow all around, and the choir ahead catching the light. That light pours in through the transept end walls, nearly two-thirds glass, with two rose windows glowing imperial purple. The soaring shafts and vaulting are pure Gothic, but the stout round pillars and the four-square solidity keep one foot in the Romanesque. At the rear on the right, the Treasury holds what's claimed to be the Crown of Thorns, along with ornate nineteenth-century monstrances and chalices, if that's your thing. And right at the back, a side chapel keeps a series of models showing how the medieval builders actually raised the place. If you climb the towers — the way up is round the side — you'll meet Viollet-le-Duc's gargoyles and angels face to face, and the great thirteen-ton Emmanuel bell in the south tower. Forged in 1685, it's one of the largest and oldest bells in Europe, and it was spared in the Revolution of 1789 — unlike Marie, the north tower's bell, which was melted down. Emmanuel only rings on special days — Christmas, moments of mourning, the day after 9/11. The other nine bells had drifted badly out of tune, so they were finally replaced in 2013.

## 7. Shakespeare and Company

From Notre-Dame, make your way on to Shakespeare and Company, about a 7-minute walk away. In 1922, when no other publisher would touch it, Sylvia Beach printed James Joyce's Ulysses herself. She'd opened her shop three years earlier, in 1919, over on rue de l'Odéon. The bookshop in front of you is its descendant. George Whitman opened this one in 1951, and it still lets wandering writers sleep among the shelves — Tumbleweeds, he called them. It's a scruffy English-language place at 37, rue de la Bucherie, packed floor to ceiling with new and used books. As you step in, look to the right wall for a good run of books about Paris. And if you're here on June 16, Bloomsday, come back for readings and wine-tasting upstairs.

## 8. The Sorbonne

Leaving Shakespeare and Company behind, head for The Sorbonne, about a 15-minute walk away. You've come a long way today — remember the Pont Neuf where we started, the New Bridge that's really the oldest in Paris, where one of Daguerre's outdoor tests from 1836 and '37 may have frozen the first human ever photographed. In the thirteenth century, this Latin Quarter became the schoolroom of all Western Europe — and at first it had no buildings at all. Lessons happened outdoors, on Place Maubert and mostly on a lane nearby, with the teacher up on wooden trestles and the students seated on the straw they'd hauled in themselves. That straw was 'fouarre' in old French, which is exactly how the street got its name — rue de Fouarre. The first official mention of the place comes in 1221, under a name to match its ambitions: the Universitas Magistrorum et Scolarium Parisiensium. The brawling and political agitation got so ugly that in 1358 the Regent — the future Charles V — had the street chained off at both ends. And the teachers here were giants: Albert the Great, Thomas Aquinas, and Brunetto Latini, the master of Dante — and Dante himself came to study on rue de Fouarre in the early fourteenth century. So where does the name Sorbonne come from? In 1253 a college was founded here by Robert, chaplain and confessor to Saint Louis, born to a humble family in the village of Sorbon in the Ardennes and risen so high as a scholar that the king seated him at his own table. His college became the seat of the faculty of theology and took the name of that village, La Sorbonne, and it grew so fast it overshadowed everything around it — which is why foreigners still mistake it for the whole University of Paris. Then came the twist. When the Hundred Years' War broke out, the university — by now stripped of its old privileges — threw in with the English against the King of France, and later against Jeanne d'Arc herself. Paris lay in ruins, wolves prowled the streets, and the Montagne Sainte-Geneviève had become, as they put it, 'an annexe of Oxford,' until peace returned and in 1437 the university bowed to the secular authority of Charles VII. The man who rebuilt the place was Richelieu, who'd studied here and later served as its dean. He laid the cornerstone of the chapel in 1635, designed by Jacques Lemercier — but his project was all stone and no substance, since the curriculum stayed locked on theology right up to the Revolution. That beautiful compound, the work of Lemercier and Le Brun, got rough handling from the anti-clerical Revolutionaries. It was entirely rebuilt and expanded across the nineteenth century, with new faculties of science and letters, inaugurated in 1901 beneath murals by Puvis de Chavannes. Not everything around here was scholarship. Place Maubert — 'la Maube' — had been a den of rowdy students and ruffians since the Middle Ages, thick with crime, vice, and public executions; Erasmus flatly called it 'the cesspit of la Maube,' and by the early twentieth century it drew the terrifying 'Apaches' and hoodlums. In Down and Out in Paris and London, published in 1933, George Orwell — who lived at number 6 on the neighbouring rue du Pot-de-Fer — described it as 'a ravine of tall, leprous houses, lurching towards one another in queer attitudes, as though they had all been frozen in the act of collapse,' where amid destitute Arabs, Poles and Italians the respectable French shopkeepers quietly piled up small fortunes. And under all of it lies something older still. The 5th arrondissement has the longest history of any district in mainland Paris, going all the way back to the Roman occupation of Gaul, when the crowded Île de la Cité spilled onto the left bank and Roman villas rose on the slopes of Mount Leucotitius — the hill you now call the Montagne Sainte-Geneviève. A great north-south road, the cardo, ran along today's rue Saint-Jacques, with a second one following the Boulevard Saint-Michel. By the end of the second century this was a quarter of pure luxury — the marble and polychrome mosaics dug up on rue de la Harpe still prove it. The Romans built three bath complexes here — the northern one at Cluny, the eastern one where the Collège de France stands, the southern one near rue Gay-Lussac — all fed by an aqueduct that ran the whole way from the Rungis basin, out by present-day Orly airport. And that brings our walk to a close. Thank you for coming along with me today. When you're ready, take your time to keep exploring on your own.
