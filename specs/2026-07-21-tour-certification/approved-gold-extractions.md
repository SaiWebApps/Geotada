## Physical-staging examples

> Find a bench in the garden, near the children's play area, so you can watch the children play *à la française* — and despite the mix of Hebrew, Yiddish, and Arabic you'll hear, you're standing in what was for nearly two centuries the single most fashionable square in Paris. If it's cold, step into Café Ma Bourgogne at the northwest corner; the view of the square is nearly as good from the window.

Source: Place des Vosges gold, line 13.

> Stand halfway across the bridge, by the statue. Look around.

Source: Île de la Cité gold, line 13.

> Here, at the corner of rue de Castiglione and rue de Rivoli, stands the Hotel Le Meurice. Its discreet entrance is at 228 rue de Rivoli.

Source: Le Meurice exact gold, line 25.

## Narrative-motion examples

> In 1626 Cardinal Richelieu, first minister of Louis XIII, forbade dueling on pain of death. The ban itself was the challenge: a year later, a six-man duel — two principals and four seconds, who were expected to do more than hold the combatants' coats — took place right in front of the Pavillon du Roi. Two dead, two fled to England, two off to Lorraine. The latter two were caught en route, brought back to Paris, and decapitated, over the protests of the nobility of France. Richelieu was not a man to take insolence lightly.

Source: Place des Vosges gold, line 25.

> During the Revolution, Notre-Dame was nearly lost forever. Stripped of its treasures and rededicated to the Cult of Reason, it was put up for sale as building material. A certain *Citoyen* Simon nearly purchased the cathedral — one of the closest brushes with destruction in the building's history.

Source: Île de la Cité gold, line 153.

> But did Orwell really work here?
>
> He never named the hotel. His biographers have placed it elsewhere in these streets—at the Lotti, just around the corner on rue de Castiglione, or at the Crillon. Paris lore, however, has long pinned the story on Le Meurice.

Source: Le Meurice exact gold, lines 41–43.
