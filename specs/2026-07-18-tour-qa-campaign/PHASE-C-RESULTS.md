## PARIS — Île de la Cité, 90 min (6 stops; 4 authored, 2 stitch)

### STOP 0 — Notre-Dame Cathedral  [converged in 4]

Step through the right-hand portal, and let your eyes climb the stone. Here the Virgin sits enthroned, St Anne's life and Christ's life carved alongside. And in the Nativity, the child rests above his mother on an altar—already, in his first hour, the carvers hint at sacrifice. To the left, another portal gives you Mary's coronation and the story of her life. Now go inside. The nave holds you in darkness while the choir ahead burns with light, and the pull between them is the whole point. See how the transept walls dissolve—nearly two-thirds glass—and how two imperial-purple rose windows pour that brightness in.

Feel the building argue with itself, too. The vaulting soars, the shafts rise Gothic and weightless; yet the round pillars, the stubborn four-squareness, stay Romanesque. One church, two styles in conversation.

And in the Trésor, quieter, wait the monstrances and chalices of the nineteenth century, caught in shadow.

### STOP 1 — Sainte-Chapelle  [converged in 1]

Climb the spiral staircase, and the room opens above you—this is the upper chapel, the one kept for the court, while below it the servants had their own. Look how little wall there is. It's almost all stained glass, and the supports that hold the ceiling up have been thinned to pencil-slim columns, made to look too delicate for the job. When the sun finds them, it throws blue and red across everything.

There's a memory hidden in that glass. Taken out during the war, it came back and was varnished in 1945 to guard it from bombing dust and scratches—though over the years that same varnish darkened, dimming the images until a restoration between 2008 and 2015 finally lifted the haze.

And yet the closest call came earlier. On the twenty-fourth of May, 1871, the Communards doused Sainte-Chapelle in petrol. Only a shortage of time saved it. Everything here, they nearly burned.

### STOP 2 — Conciergerie  [GROUNDED-STITCH FALLBACK]

In the 15th century passers-by could still hear the screams of tortured prisoners from the Tour Bonbec — the westernmost tower of the Conciergerie. During the tower's restoration in 1828, workers found two oubliettes in the basement: concealed dungeons spiked with iron points and connected to the river. Needless to dwell on the fate of the prisoners dropped into them. The name bon-bec — medieval French for prate — probably alluded to the interrogations on the ground floor, where a varied spectrum of torture methods was devised to extract confessions and denunciations. The Conciergerie is one of the few remaining vestiges of the old medieval Palais de Justice and is Paris's oldest prison — where Marie-Antoinette and the leading figures of the Revolution were incarcerated before execution. Inside are several splendid, vaulted Gothic halls, including the Salle des Gens d'Armes, built in 1301 to 1315. The far end is separated off by an iron grille; during the Revolution this area was reserved for the pailleux, prisoners who couldn't afford to bribe a guard for their own cell and had to sleep on straw (paille).

### STOP 3 — Palais de Justice  [converged in 2]

Picture the moat, the drawbridge — every defense this palace had. On the afternoon of 22 February 1357, none of it mattered. With Jean II le Bon, of the Valois dynasty, held captive in England, the provost of merchants, Etienne Marcel, believed he could topple the monarchy with English help. So his men burst in on the young Dauphin, the future Charles V, and cut down two of his counsellors where he sat. The boy was untouched. But their blood soaked his clothes, and he never forgot it. Prudent, deservedly called le Sage, he slipped out one night by the river, withdrew to the remote Hôtel de Saint-Pol in the Marais, then arranged a new residence within the safer walls of the Louvre, where he spent time from 1368 onward. The kings never came back to the island.

Look right, at the arcade off the Cour de Mai — before 1825, this was the entrance to the Conciergerie. Through it, 2,278 of the Tribunal's victims passed on their way out — to the jeers of delighted tricoteuses huddled on the perron.

### STOP 4 — Square du Vert-Galant  [GROUNDED-STITCH FALLBACK]

The leafy green triangular square behind the Pont Neuf is named for Henri IV, the lusty gallant on horseback who loved his city mightily, especially its wine, women, and good times. This romantic western tip of the boat-shaped Île de la Cité is the original settlement of the Parisii, a Celtic tribe subdued by Caesar — the Romans called it Lutetia, or Cara Lutetia, "my beloved Lutetia," in the words of the late Roman emperor Julian in 358 AD. As king, le Vert Galant (1589–1610) declared his intention "to make this city beautiful, tranquil, desiring to make a whole world of this city and a wonder of the world." Such triumphs as the elegant Pont Neuf — the "new bridge" is the oldest bridge in Paris — the Place Dauphine opposite the bridge, and the Place Royale (later renamed Place des Vosges) show his genius as architect and urban visionary. His energetic love life — he had two wives, at least fifty-six mistresses, and households full of bastards — also won him the heart of Parisians, until his serial amours began to bore and irritate. But Henri the obsessive lover was above all a peacemaker. Born and raised a Protestant by a rigidly reformist mother, he converted to Catholicism, the religion of the majority, to end the long religious wars between Catholics and Protestants that by the end of the sixteenth century had left Paris starving and looking like a bomb site. Detesting religious partisanship, he drafted and signed the Edict of Nantes (1598), which put toleration of the religion he had renounced on the books. The fanatical Catholic who stabbed him to death, Francis Ravaillac, was not persuaded. But to this day, together with Charlemagne, Joan of Arc, and Saint-Louis IX, Henri IV remains one of France's heroes. He declared kindness and mercy the primary virtues of a prince.

### STOP 5 — Pont Neuf  [converged in 2]

Feel the heat first—here at the square du Vert Galant, Jacques de Molay, Grand Master of the Templars, was burnt at the stake, and as the flames took him he cursed the kings. Now look at the span beneath you. They call it Pont Neuf, the New Bridge, yet it's the oldest bridge in Paris—the first Paris bridge built of stone, and the first left free of houses. Those semicircular side bays once sheltered teeth-pullers, quack doctors, merchants hawking their cures. The original statue dates to 1614, was destroyed in 1792, then rebuilt in 1818 by public subscription, its bronze obtained by melting down a statue of Louis Charles Antoine Desaix. And here's what few standing where you stand ever learn. Lemot sealed four boxes inside the new figure: a history of Henry IV's life, a seventeenth-century parchment certifying the original, a document on how the new statue was commissioned, and a list of the public-subscription contributors.

## PARIS — Place des Vosges, 60 min, dark_history+social_change (4 stops, all authored)

### STOP 0 — Place des Vosges  [converged in 2]

Find a bench near the children's play area, and watch them play à la française, the air braided with Hebrew, Yiddish, and Arabic — yet this was, for nearly two centuries, the single most fashionable square in Paris. If the cold bites, step into Café Ma Bourgogne at the northwest corner; from the window, the view is nearly as good. But not all the action here happened outdoors. As the place Royale became the centre of Parisian social life, fashionable ladies of the neighbourhood — Ninon de Lenclos, Marion Delorme among them — invented a new and durable institution. They met at intimate gatherings called ruelles, where the elegant guests rivaled one another in wit, fine speech, social finesse. Molière parodied these ladies' excesses in his play Les Précieuses Ridicules. And why did they meet in the hostesses' bedrooms? Perhaps because an amazing number were as renowned for their sexual prowess as their social. Several of the great ladies of the place Royale, the story goes, could hardly keep track of their lovers.

### STOP 1 — Musee Carnavalet  [converged in 2]

She wrote out here, in the garden, pen moving while the light held.

For the last twenty years of her life, Madame de Sévigné lived inside these walls, the Hôtel Carnavalet, and she filled letter after letter from this very ground. In one, she names them without ceremony — the "blue girls," nuns in a blue habit from a convent just up the lane, then called rue Sainte-Catherine, now rue Sévigné. Look for the convent and you won't find it. The Lycée Victor-Hugo stands there instead, students where the sisters once were.

And on the days she wanted more than a garden and a page, she worshipped at Saint-Paul-Saint-Louis, ninety-nine rue Saint-Antoine.

A Jesuit church, that one. Its shape was borrowed whole from the Gesù in Rome, an idea carried across a continent and set down here, for a woman who wrote her world into being, sentence by sentence.

### STOP 2 — Rue de Rivoli  [converged in 1]

Listen for hooves. In 1720 a riding school — a manège — rose here for young Louis XV, on what was then the edge of the Tuileries Gardens, before rue de Rivoli existed at all. When Louis left for Versailles, upper-class youths took his place, drilling in fencing, dancing, and mathematics, everything the gentlemanly ideal required. Then the ground turned. Early in the Revolution the National Assembly sat here, and after it the Convention. On this exact spot, on the twenty-first of September 1792, Danton proclaimed the First Republic — and soon after, Louis XVI was brought to trial here, and sentenced to death. One man walked through all of it untouched. The house at no. 258 is crying out for repair now, but it once held Talleyrand, who wormed through every shaky regime of the age, in the most sumptuous palace in the capital, overlooking the Tuileries. Here he died of old age, eighty-six, in 1838.

### STOP 3 — Rue des Rosiers  [converged in 3]

Breathe in—falafel, shawarma, the char of kefta drifting from kosher kitchens along the street. This is the backbone of the pletzel, Yiddish for "little square," the historic Jewish quarter of the Marais, its heart at the corner with Rue des Ecouffes—a street named for a bird of prey, a derogatory reference to moneychanger shops. The roots run deep. At number twenty stood the Hotel des Juifs; nearby, Jo Goldenberg's venerable deli stood at the intersection of Rue des Rosiers and Rue Ferdinand Duval, where a 1982 bombing left a plaque that marks the spot.

The street itself is older than any of that. It ran just inside the city wall of 1180, where no building could rise against the wall, so the strip was planted with rose gardens that gave it its name.

And one memorial breaks you. From a local school, one hundred sixty-five pupils were deported under the Occupation. They never came back.

## NEW YORK — Lower Manhattan, 90 min (5 stops; 3 authored, 2 stitch bookends)

### STOP 0 — Castle Clinton  [GROUNDED-STITCH FALLBACK]

Walk into Battery Park and toward the river. Find a spot where you can see the water — that open view of the harbor is where this walk begins. This circular stone structure — taller in some earlier incarnations — has been at the center of New York life over the years. In 1807, the West Battery, as it was then called, was built as a fort on a landfill island in the water off Manhattan, to ward off British invasions. It never saw action, however: during the War of 1812 the British attacked Washington, D.C., instead. In 1817 it was renamed Castle Clinton in honor of Mayor De Witt Clinton, and in 1823 the federal government ceded the site to the city. In 1855, the space was transformed once more, into the city's first immigrant processing center. Over eight million new arrivals — a full two-thirds of those who came to the United States at this time — spent their first hours in the country here, registering their names with the government, exchanging money, and getting information on jobs, medical care, and lodgings. By 1890, the vast number of immigrants, and the growing problem of outsiders scamming them, necessitated a move to a larger and more easily patrolled offshore space: Ellis Island, which you can see from here, to the right of the Statue of Liberty, its main hall reaching into the sky with four little spires.

### STOP 1 — Battery Park  [converged in 4]

Look out at the water and imagine sails, 1609, sixteen men seeking Asia, following this river. Henry Hudson never found Asia. What he found instead, his reports told — the pelts, the harbor — and those reports did the work that no map could, drawing the Dutch here to settle. Then came something even his glowing words never promised. The Erie Canal reached inland from this same Hudson and tied it to the Great Lakes, and that single connection lifted New York into the most powerful city in the nation. All of it began with a wrong guess about a passage to Asia. So stand here a moment longer. The harbor that misled one captain made a metropolis. And this patch of ground where you're standing, Battery Park, turned up on screen in nineteen eighty-five, in Desperately Seeking Susan — a small, unlikely footnote to a very long story.

### STOP 2 — National Museum of the American Indian  [converged in 3]

Look down at your feet: this is the foot of Broadway, and the road you're standing on runs thirteen miles from here to the top of Manhattan. A Dutch fort once stood on this very ground. Now something grander rises above it.

Before there was an income tax—before 1913—customs were the main federal revenue. And three-quarters of it came through the Port of New York. So a building had to match that importance.

Cass Gilbert designed it, finished in 1907, and named it the Alexander Hamilton U.S. Customs House—over four hundred fifty thousand square feet.

It's called Beaux Arts, which simply means "fine arts," from the École des Beaux Arts. But here in New York, the term means something more: buildings that express civic pride and grandeur, scaled to match the civic work done inside them.

### STOP 3 — Bowling Green Park  [converged in 2]

Stand here and face north, up Broadway, toward the bull. That avenue runs twelve miles, the longest in America, though for a long stretch it barely began — until the second quarter of the nineteenth century, the city went no farther than City Hall. Beneath your feet is where New York started small. This was the city's first official park, laid out in 1733, a patch that colonists worked as cow pasture, drilled across as parade ground, rolled balls on as a bowling field.

Now the bull. Arturo Di Modica made Charging Bull in 1989, after a recession, a symbol of markets rising again. Nobody at the New York Stock Exchange asked for it; he simply dumped it under a Christmas tree one night — seven thousand pounds of it, left in the dark.

And the statue proved so popular that the city kept it, and relocated it.

### STOP 4 — Wall Street  [GROUNDED-STITCH FALLBACK]

On the site of today's U.S. Steel Building at 1 Liberty Plaza once stood one of the great downtown buildings, the Singer Building. Perhaps the mourning wasn't as great as when Pennsylvania Station was slaughtered in 1963, but the Singer Building's demolition brought tears of rage consistent with its long-standing place in the skyline. Its tragic epitaph: it is the tallest building ever to be demolished. At 55 Wall Street stands a building designed by Isaiah Rogers in 1842 to replace the Merchants Exchange, which had burned with seven hundred other buildings in the Great Fire of 1835. It served as the U.S. Custom House from 1863 to 1899 and later as a branch of Citibank. It's notable for a seamless arrangement of structure: the lower Ionic colonnade of 1842 — each column a single block of stone — works hand in glove with an upper Corinthian colonnade added seventy years later. The mammoth lobby measures 198 feet long and 132 feet high, and houses the largest display of Wedgewood china in the world around its central chandelier.

## LONDON — Westminster, 75 min (5 stops; 2 authored, 3 stitch — thin-corpus ceiling)

### STOP 0 — Big Ben  [GROUNDED-STITCH FALLBACK]

Big Ben is the nickname for the Great Bell of the Great Clock of Westminster, and, by extension, for the clock tower that stands at the north end of the Palace of Westminster in London, England. The tolling of the bells is broadcast live in the UK on BBC Radio 4 and internationally on both the BBC World Service and online on BBC Sounds twice a day, with an additional broadcast on Sundays. The clock uses its original mechanism and was the largest and most accurate four-faced striking and chiming clock in the world upon its completion. Its nickname, "Big Ben", derives from the tall Sir Benjamin Hall, who oversaw its installation. A Latin inscription celebrates Queen Victoria, under whose reign the palace was built.

### STOP 1 — New Scotland Yard  [GROUNDED-STITCH FALLBACK]

The force moved from Great Scotland Yard in 1890 to a newly completed building on the Victoria Embankment, and the name "New Scotland Yard" was adopted for the new headquarters. A third building was added in 1940. In 2013, it was announced that the force would move again to the Victoria Embankment at Westminster's Curtis Green Building, which following tradition was renamed "New Scotland Yard". Scotland Yard (officially New Scotland Yard) is the headquarters of the Metropolitan Police, the territorial police force responsible for policing Greater London's 32 boroughs, and several additional authorities throughout the United Kingdom. The Scotland Yard entrance became the public entrance, and over time "Scotland Yard" came to be used not only as the common name of the headquarters building, but also as a metonym for the Metropolitan Police Service (MPS) itself and police officers, especially detectives, who serve in it.

### STOP 2 — Westminster Bridge  [converged in 2]

Put your hand on the rail and feel where the river bends beneath you, because everything crossing here has somewhere to be. Cars and footsteps share this span alike. Westminster Bridge carries them over the Thames, tying Westminster on the western bank to Lambeth on the east, and it isn't the only crossing along this stretch. Look downstream and you'll find the next bridge, the Hungerford Bridge and the Golden Jubilee Bridges; turn the other way, upstream, and the next one is Lambeth Bridge. So this crossing is one voice in a longer conversation across the water. And here's the thing you might not guess from how settled it looks: not so long ago, between 2005 and 2007, the whole of it was stripped back and made new. The iron fascias were replaced. Then the entire bridge was repainted, end to end. What you're leaning on now was, quite deliberately, made to last.

### STOP 3 — Palace of Westminster  [GROUNDED-STITCH FALLBACK]

Construction started in 1840 and lasted for 30 years, suffering delays, cost overruns, and the deaths of Barry and his assistant, Augustus Pugin. The Palace of Westminster is the meeting place of the Parliament of the United Kingdom and is located in London, England. The monarch moved to the adjacent Palace of Whitehall, but the remainder of the palace continued to serve as the home of the Parliament of England, which had met there since the 13th century. The Elizabeth Tower of the palace houses the bell nicknamed Big Ben and is a landmark of London and the United Kingdom in general. Extensive repairs had to be made after the Second World War, including rebuilding the destroyed Commons chamber.

### STOP 4 — Westminster Abbey  [converged in 1]

Stand still a moment, and count the company beneath your feet: more than three thousand three hundred people lie here, many of them prominent in British history. Monarchs and prime ministers, poets laureate, actors and musicians, scientists, military leaders — and among them the Unknown Warrior. All of them gathered into one Anglican church in the City of Westminster, formally titled the Collegiate Church of Saint Peter at Westminster.

The story runs deep. By the mid-tenth century an abbey housing Benedictine monks already stood on this ground, though the church's earliest origins stay obscure. Look up, and the Gothic lines take their cue chiefly from thirteenth-century French and English styles, with older Romanesque work and later Baroque and modern touches folded in.

Then, in 1559, the monastery was dissolved. Elizabeth the First made this church a royal peculiar — accountable, from that day, directly to the sovereign alone.
